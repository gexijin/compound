• The blunt verdict: there is a publishable novel inside this design, but I would not acquire or draft this
  version yet. I would invite a serious revise-and-resubmit if the sample pages demonstrated the promised
  voice.

  The premise is excellent. The current execution is overengineered, overpopulated, and too determined to prove
  its thesis. It often reads less like a novel about people and more like an exceptionally intelligent
  financial-literacy machine disguised as a novel.

  ## What is genuinely strong

  The core formulation is first-rate:

  > Three women responding separately to the same bill because each mistakes carrying it alone for love.

  “Solitary rescue” is specific, emotionally credible, generational, and capable of sustaining a book. It is
  much better than a generic story about a family keeping secrets. The garage sale is an excellent opening
  arena: visual, transactional, funny, socially porous, and thematically relevant without needing explanation.

  Keisha, Estelle, and Denise form a strong central triangle:

  - Keisha’s civil-engineering fixation and dream of maintenance rather than rescue are memorable and
    character-specific.

  - Estelle’s distinction between discussing historical prices and concealing present positions is superb.
  - Denise may be the most emotionally potent character in the book. Her competence, envelope system, pride in
    owing nothing, and inability to ask are painful without making her foolish.

  The chain is a powerful object. Its mispricing, recovery, gift, resale, and unrecoverable destruction make
  physical what the family keeps doing emotionally. Gary is also unusually well designed. “Listening as
  product” is chilling, contemporary, and more interesting than an obvious fraudster. His polite departure and
  continuing church advertisement are exactly right.

  The best scenes are scenes in which behavior carries the theme:

  - Denise and Keisha counting perfectly without speaking.
  - Estelle defending Gary because “he remembered Nate’s name.”
  - Three women waiting at the table when a pitch designed for one isolated widow arrives.
  - Denise failing to finish “I don’t owe anybody a—”
  - The dealer having already melted the chain.

  Those are novelistic. Trust them.

  ## The largest structural problem

  You currently have two novels joined by thematic parallels rather than causation.

  Novel one is a funny summer hustle story: McDonald’s, the stand, Max, the stock, the permit, JazzFest, the
  tuition clock.

  Novel two is a multigenerational family drama and soft financial thriller: Estelle’s hidden fortune, Gary,
  the contract, the 529, the chain, the dinner.

  They share the subject of money, but Keisha does not enter the Gary story because of anything she learned or
  did in the stand-and-stock story. She crashes, runs out of options, and then happens to help sort paperwork.
  The plot simply changes tracks at full-price-spine.md:106.

  That is not enough. The first plot must cause her entry into the second. The chain, the garage-sale anomaly,
  the Sunrise objects, Verna, or Denise’s behavior should become an accumulating question Keisha actively
  follows. Otherwise Gary feels like the more important novel waiting for the tuition novel to finish.

  A related problem: the reader learns in the 2009 flashback that Estelle’s next $5,000 was always coming. From
  that point, the advertised tuition suspense substantially collapses. The central question becomes not “Will
  Keisha get back to school?” but “When will these women finally talk?” That can absolutely work—but the pitch
  and pacing should admit that this is a family-secrecy novel, not primarily a tuition race.

  ## The arithmetic currently weakens the premise

  A book this exact about money cannot survive imprecise central arithmetic.

  Keisha does not have a $7,800 “gap.” She has a $7,800 bill and $1,500 saved, meaning she initially faces a
  $6,300 gap. Once Estelle’s recurring $5,000 is known, Keisha’s assigned share is $2,800. The documents use
  those concepts interchangeably.

  More importantly, the ending says her summer covers $2,800 partly through “a campus library job lined up for
  fall” full-price-spine.md:135. A future job cannot pay a present bill unless there is a payment plan, bridge
  loan, receivable purchase, or someone advances the money. You already identify this as an open bug. It is not
  a minor bug; it compromises the satisfaction of the entire money plot.

  The book also needs a stronger reason both Keisha and Denise interpret the previous $5,000 as strictly one-
  time. “Estelle never said otherwise” is thematically neat but dramatically fragile. The whole summer depends
  on two people refusing to ask the obvious question. Give them an actual prior statement, misunderstanding, or
  emotionally costly precedent.

  ## The design is strangling the people

  Almost every character has:

  - A money wound
  - A money physics
  - A right belief
  - A wrong belief
  - A speaking register
  - A prop
  - A secret ledger
  - A comic runner
  - A thematic dyad
  - A coda payoff

  The result is impressive and synthetic. The cast begins to feel like a financial curriculum divided among
  human delivery systems.

  Emily is precision and avoidance. Sydney is consumption and emotional value. Max is speculation. Sofi is
  labor pricing. Lourdes is liquidity and informal economies. Denise is cash discipline without growth. Estelle
  is compounding without disclosure. Gary is predatory advice.

  That is too clean. Humans overlap, contradict their assigned function, act pointlessly, and occasionally fail
  to illuminate the theme. Right now even the jokes have accounting rules about whether they qualify as non-
  thematic comedy.

  The racial patterning increases this risk. The Black Carter women, Mexican-American Ortiz family, and white
  Ellis and Larsen families each embody distinct money cultures with remarkable neatness. The specificity is
  often good, but taken together it can feel like an ethnographic teaching grid. You need more mess, cross-
  current, and behavior unrelated to financial meaning.

  Keep the depth of the profiles as private knowledge. Stop requiring all of it to pay off.

  ## Act-by-act problems

  ### Act 1 is overloaded

  The opening sale contains a retail war, sticker war, envelope, flashback, chain chase, banking reveal, Denise
  interlude, merchandise re-absorption, stand origin, wage calculation, Max’s entrance, and several long-term
  plants. That is not a clean six-beat opening. It is half a novel’s symbolic inventory unloaded in one
  afternoon.

  The chain, tuition reveal, and Max entrance can carry the opening. Some combination of the envelope, stand
  formation, Gary planting, and Denise cutaway should breathe into later chapters.

  ### Acts 2–3 contain the most contrived beat

  At JazzFest, Keisha is about to sell at the stock’s exact peak when the cash box disappears for an hour,
  after which the price is gone full-price-spine.md:87. This feels manufactured.

  A stock trade takes seconds. A missing cash box does not prevent her from selling, particularly if she
  believes selling will save her education. Worse, the coincidence partially excuses her despite the book’s
  insistence that the loss belongs to her thumb.

  Let her consciously decide not to sell. Greed, overconfidence, a raised target, or belief in her superior
  reading of the system should cost her. The cash-box confession can remain, but it should not mechanically
  cause the investment loss.

  ### Act 5 tries to detonate everythingL

  The dinner includes:

  - Keisha’s confession
  - Estelle’s $900,000
  - The 529 system
  - The fairness rule
  - The hard-work rationalization
  - Denise’s portal reveal
  - The chain sale
  - The chain’s full 1987 history
  - The envelope’s return
  - The rent calculation
  - A new question about the 529

  Any three or four of these could produce a devastating climax. Together, they risk feeling like the author
  closing every open ledger line. The chain story in particular needs silence around it. If another revelation
  arrives thirty seconds later, its emotional cost is discounted.
  streak, Max reconciliation, job offer, Vibe, cat, Sofi’s account, permit, concessionaire, potluck, Gary’s
  advertisement, heron. This is a sitcom finale in which every recurring bit takes a bow.

  Keep perhaps:

  - The chain cannot be recovered.
  - Denise enrolls in the 401(k) and changes “even” to “ahead.”
  - Keisha gets—or declines someone else’s—heron photograph.

  Let most other runners end earlier or remain unresolved.

  ## Max is a serious acquisition problem

  Max is twenty-five. Keisha is nineteen. He has known her through a neighborhood relationship since she was a
  child, becomes her financial confidant, helps her put her savings into his speculative trade, and becomes
  romantically involved with her. After she loses the money, he gives a bad apology, learns almost nothing,
  starts promoting another sure thing, and is welcomed back into a warm romantic ambiguity.

  You can write “never villainous” in the guardrails, but readers decide how he reads. Many will see a six-
  years-older man leading a vulnerable nineteen-year-old into gambling and then escaping meaningful
  accountability.

  The “I’m not a fiduciary” joke does not solve this. Nor does the fact that she pressed the buttons herself.

  You have three viable options:

  1. Age Max down to roughly twenty-one or twenty-two.
  2. Remove the romance and keep him as a neighborhood foil.
  3. Give him an actual reckoning and a substantive apology rather than treating his failure to say “I told you
     so” as moral credit.

  The current coda’s lesson—that Keisha should not wait for a perfect apology—risks sounding like an argument
  for women accepting inadequate accountability. I would flag this hard in acquisitions.

  ## Positioning
  first-person narrator, summer business, friend ensemble, first romance, college jeopardy, and stock-trading
  boy all suggest YA or New Adult. The revived New Adult category explicitly targets roughly 18–24-year-old
  concerns, but much of its current publishing infrastructure is romance-forward. Publishers Weekly describes
  that expanding category and its romance roots
  (https://www.publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/industry-news/publisher-news/article/99962-new-adult-comes-of-age.html).

  To remain adult book-club fiction, the marketing and manuscript should foreground the three Carter women, not
  primarily Keisha’s hustle and Max. Come and Get It supports an adult campus-adjacent money novel, but its
  publisher positions it around desire, consumption, bad behavior, and morally complicated adult
  relationships—not education. That distinction is visible in the publisher’s description
  (https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/665335/come-and-get-it-a-gma-book-club-pick-by-kiley-reid/9780593328217/).

  Remove “quietly educates and encourages young women to handle money” from the pitch. That is an authorial
  intention, not a reader promise. It makes the novel sound medicinal. Sell the family conflict, humor,
  jeopardy, and revelation; let readers discover that they learned something.

  The comps are individually defensible but collectively aspirational rather than precise. Margo promises
  audacity and radical voice; Come and Get It promises social ambiguity; Black Cake promises a broad family
  saga. Your current design is tidier, more instructional, and more plot-clocked than any of them.

  ## My editorial prescription

  Before drafting, I would make five large changes:

  1. Make the tuition/stock plot causally uncover the Estelle/Gary plot.
  2. Fix the central arithmetic and the August 28 payment mechanism completely.
  3. Rework Max’s age, romance, and accountability.
  4. Remove at least a third of the supporting-character mechanisms and half the coda payoffs.
  5. Give Denise more narrative room and simplify the dinner so its largest losses can breathe.

  My acquisition verdict: strong premise, memorable central women, excellent thematic object, potentially
  commercial voice—but not yet controlled enough to buy. The danger is not that the book lacks ideas. It is
  that the author currently loves every idea equally. The next design pass should be an exercise in hierarchy
