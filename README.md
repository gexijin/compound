# Compound — Build Brief

Compound is a story-driven, education-only web app that teaches money and investing to teen girls (ages 16–18). The name carries the whole idea: compound interest, and a *compound* as a place where a community lives and grows together — the neighborhood club at the heart of the story compounds, and so does their money.

---

## 1. Overview & Vision

An educational website that teaches teen girls how to manage money and invest — **fun, interactive, and genuinely appealing to girls**, not a pink-washed textbook.

Locked-in decisions:

- **Audience:** older teens, **16–18**.
- **Scope:** **education only.** No banking, debit cards, custodial accounts, or real-money movement. This sidesteps fintech infrastructure, KYC, and most COPPA weight (COPPA applies under 13). We teach; we don't hold money.
- **Primary experience:** **interactive lessons + quizzes**, wrapped in an ongoing **story** with recurring characters.
- **Lesson length:** **8–12 minutes** each — room for a story beat, the teaching, and a quiz.

### The market gap

The landscape splits into three buckets, and nobody owns the intersection:

1. **General teen money apps** (Greenlight, Copper, Step, FamZoo, BusyKid) — fintech-first, parent-controlled, gender-neutral; education is bolted on. KidVestors is the education-first exception but targets all kids 8–18, both genders.
2. **Girl/women-focused programs** (Invest in Girls, Girls Who Invest, Girls That Invest, Her First $100K) — right mission, but aimed at college/adult women or delivered as school curriculum. No fun, self-serve web destination a 17-year-old would choose on her own.
3. **Gamified learning** (Stock Market Game, Wall Street Survivor, Investopedia Simulator) — engaging but gender-neutral and fixated on trading simulations.

Compound's lane: self-serve web + genuinely fun/interactive + built from the ground up for teen girls.

### The strategic thesis: confidence

Only ~12% of girls feel very confident making personal financial decisions, and roughly **one-third of the gender gap in financial literacy is confidence, not knowledge.** Every competitor optimizes for knowledge transfer. Compound optimizes for **confidence** — relatability, seeing women like them, low-stakes practice, and permission to ask "dumb" questions. This is primarily a tone and story decision, not a feature.

## 2. Design & Tone Principles

- **Do not pink-wash.** 16–18-year-old girls have finely tuned radar for condescension and "finance but make it sparkly." The tone that works (Her First $100K, Girls That Invest) is smart, a little irreverent, and treats them as capable.
- **Useful now.** This age hits real financial firsts (first paycheck, first bank account, a Roth IRA she can fund with a summer job). Teach the real thing, not hypotheticals.
- **Confidence over completeness.** Better she finishes a lesson feeling "I can do this" than that she memorized every term.
- **Story does the emotional work.** When an anxious character faces her fear and acts, the student feels *permission* in a way a bullet point never delivers.
- **Show, don't lecture.** Consequences beat warnings. Let the student make a choice for a character and watch it play out.

---

## 3. Pedagogy — The Lesson Loop

Every lesson follows the same shape so it feels like a game, not homework:

**Story beat → Decision point (student chooses *for* a character) → Consequence plays out → The "why" (the teaching) → Quick quiz.**

The student makes choices and sees outcomes *before* the principle is named — which sticks far better than principle-first teaching. Quizzes should have personality-quiz energy, not exam energy.

The complete scope Compound will eventually teach. The investing module (Section 5) ships first; the rest is the map.

## 5. First Build — The Investing Module

Six lessons in a deliberate order that follows a story arc: **problem → hope → vehicle → catch → fix → action.** Each lesson creates the question the next one answers.

### Lesson 1 — Inflation *(the problem: doing nothing loses money)*
- Prices rise, so each dollar buys less over time
- **Grandma's hook:** what $100 bought in the 1960s vs. today
- Why "safe" cash under the mattress quietly shrinks
- The rough number (~2–3%/yr) and what it does over decades
- Takeaway: doing nothing isn't neutral — it's a slow loss

### Lesson 2 — Compound Interest *(the hope: growth over time beats inflation)*
- Interest earning interest — the snowball
- Why it starts slow, then explodes
- Time is the biggest lever: starting at 16 vs. 26
- Small amounts + early start > big amounts + late start
- **Grandma's $100/month reveal:** what it *could* have become

### Lesson 3 — What Is a Share of Stock (vs. a Lottery Ticket) *(the vehicle: what you actually buy)*
- A share = owning a tiny slice of a real company
- You're a part-owner, not a bettor (the lottery contrast)
- Where returns come from: the business grows and profits
- Owning brands she already knows and buys
- Reframe: not gambling, not get-rich-quick

### Lesson 4 — Risk & Diversification *(the catch: one bet is dangerous)*
- Risk is real and normal, not something to fear
- The single-stock danger: one company can go to zero
- Don't put all your eggs in one basket
- Why young = able to ride out the ups and downs
- Sets up the question: how do I spread out without being an expert?

### Lesson 5 — Index Funds & Why They Work *(the fix: own everything, easily)*
- A basket of hundreds of companies in one purchase
- Buying "the whole market" instead of guessing winners
- Instant diversification (answers Lesson 4's question)
- Why they quietly beat most stock-pickers (low fees, boring wins)
- The message: you don't have to be smart, just consistent

### Lesson 6 — Types of Accounts *(the action: where it all lives, go do it)*
- The account is the container; the fund is what's inside
- Custodial brokerage (what a teen can open with a parent)
- Roth IRA — funded by *her own* earned income, grows tax-free
- Why "earned income" matters (her summer job unlocks it)
- The practical "here's where to actually start" close

**Through-line:** inflation makes the student realize saving alone isn't safe → compounding gives hope → stocks show what to buy → risk scares her back → index funds rescue her → accounts tell her where to press "go." Grandma's $100/month anchors Lessons 1–2; Maya's resale profits fit naturally into Lessons 3–6.

## 6. Characters & Backgrounds

A group of **neighborhood girls** who've known each other for years, each from a family with a different financial situation at home — and those differences drive the plot. The four core girls sort by **experience level**, so almost any student finds her entry point.

### Core four (teen protagonists — appear constantly)

**Sofi, 15 — the blank slate.** *For the student who knows nothing.*
Never had a bank account, debit card, or paycheck. Her family are immigrants who run on **cash** via a small family business, with a deep distrust of fees and paperwork. Nothing is wrong at home — her parents are hardworking and proud — but the banking system is foreign territory. She asks the beautifully basic questions with **zero embarrassment**, giving every student permission to not know things. *Plot hooks:* she translates lessons back to her parents (real stakes); tension in learning things that make her family's way look "behind" without disrespecting it. She's the bridge between two financial worlds. *Arc:* from "I've never held a bank card" to opening her first account and getting it.

**Emily, 16 — the avoider.** *For the scared student. The emotional spine of the course.*
Sharp, good grades, completely frozen around money. From the outside her family looks fine; inside, money is **tense** — a recent shock (a parent's layoff, a divorce in progress, or unnamed debt) has made it the subject that ends dinners in silence. She copes by not looking: unopened banking app, guilt about every purchase, a quiet belief that she's "just bad at this." *Plot hooks:* the home tension is a live wire that escalates over the summer and forces her to engage; the club is the safe place she can finally face money. *Arc:* the biggest of anyone — by the "built for her" material she's calmly doing the thing she was terrified of at the start.

**Maya, 17 — the hustler + spender.** *For the ambitious, already-earning student.*
Runs a thriving **resale shop** (thrifted/vintage clothes flipped online). Earns real money and has the entrepreneurial instinct, but it burns a hole in her pocket — profit becomes new inventory, food, and fun before it becomes anything lasting. Confident to the point of impulsive; allergic to "boring" advice. Her household has **three generations under one roof** (Grandma Elena lives with them; big sister Nadia is home from college), so it's the whole money story in one family. *Arc:* income into **wealth** — learning that making money and keeping money are different skills. She's how Compound reaches the girl who'd never click a "financial literacy" lesson.

**Keisha, 18 — one step ahead.** *For the senior about to face it all. Carries the season's stakes.*
College-bound and grounded, facing real adult decisions *right now*. First-gen college; her parents did everything "right" — steady jobs, sacrifice — and college is **still** a genuine financial stretch. She's the oldest, about to leave, and doesn't want to bury herself in loans or burden the parents she's watched work so hard. She hits real walls (student loans are legitimately hard), so she models competence *and* struggle. *Arc:* navigating adult firsts (loans, first W-2 paycheck, a Roth IRA) with a cool head.

### Supporting cast (appear when their function is needed)

**Nadia, 22 — the near-peer mentor.** Maya's older sister, home from college for the summer, already investing a little ($25/month, nothing fancy). Credible instead of preachy because she made the mistakes recently — a bit of freshman-year credit card debt she dug out of, a first paycheck she blew, a negotiation she fumbled. The "cool older sister" who tells the truth and roots for you. She sparks the club and leads the "built for her" material.

**Grandma Elena — the living proof.** Maya and Nadia's grandmother; the friend group has adopted her as the honorary grandma of the club. She **earned $100 a month in the 1960s** — the most powerful storytelling asset in the cast:
- **Compound-interest showstopper, made personal:** what if she'd invested even part of that $100/month and left it alone? (Compute the exact figure in the lesson; a few years of small 1960s contributions compounding to today lands in the six figures.) The honest twist: she *didn't* invest — she kept it safe in cash. So the lesson carries both the magic of compounding *and* the quiet cost of playing it too safe. The girls get to teach *her* something, too.
- **Emotional anchor for "built for her":** a woman who earned her own money in the 1960s but often couldn't get a credit card or loan in her own name without a man to co-sign (not fully outlawed until a 1974 federal law) — a living witness to *why Compound exists*.
- **Mindset nuance:** her thrift is real wisdom, but you can show where discipline curdled into fear/scarcity. Takeaway: keep her discipline, lose her fear.

**Three generations of women in one family** — Grandma (earned her own money, never invested), Nadia (learning to invest now), Maya (earns but spends it all) — is a built-in arc about patterns passing down or getting broken.

## 7. The Season / Plot

**Setting:** summer, one neighborhood, four girls from four money worlds.

**Inciting spark:** the girls help Grandma Elena clear out the garage and find her **1960s pay envelope — $100 a month** — tucked in an old bank passbook. They howl laughing ("a *hundred dollars*?!"), and Grandma stops them: *that hundred dollars was worth more than you think — and could've become more than you'd ever believe.*

**The club:** Nadia proposes a summer pact — part book club, part challenge — meeting weekly on Grandma's porch.

**The unifying device — the $100 Challenge:** echoing Grandma's paycheck, each girl takes a symbolic $100 (real, pooled, or simulated) and spends the summer learning what it can *become*. One number threads the season: inflation (what $100 bought *then* vs. now), compounding (what $100 could *grow* into), and each girl's different choices reflect her different world. Grandma's $100 in the 1960s **rhyming** with the girls' $100 today is the emotional and mathematical spine.

**Driving goals (two flavors, so different students latch on):**
- **Keisha — the ticking external goal:** a real **tuition gap** (a hole between her aid offer and the cost) she has one summer to close before fall. Concrete, high-stakes, gives the season a finish line and an emotional climax. Lets Compound teach the hard real stuff (loans, aid, true cost of borrowing) through someone the student aches for.
- **Maya — the aspirational goal:** catches the wealth bug from Nadia and declares she'll be financially free young (her version of FIRE). Where Keisha's goal is *survive this fall*, Maya's is *build a whole future*.
- **Emily & Sofi — small, internal goals on purpose:** Emily's is to *stop being scared to look*; Sofi's is to *open her first account*. Keeps the range honest — not every girl starts with a five-figure dream.

**Where the conflict lives (so it's a plot, not a lecture):**
- The ticking tuition clock, and the temptation to chase it with something risky.
- Emily's home tension escalating until she has to engage.
- Maya's fork: blow the resale profits, or invest them.
- Quiet friction *between* the girls — a moment where their different family situations collide (someone with more says something tone-deaf to someone with less) and the friendship has to navigate it. This dramatizes the confidence mission: everyone's money journey starts from a different place.

## 8. Technical Notes

**Hosting:** GitHub Pages (free static hosting). The app is education-only with no backend, so static hosting costs nothing — all lesson content lives in the repo, all state is client-side.

**Stack:**
- **Vite + React + TypeScript** — a pure client-side SPA. (Not Next.js: its server rendering and API routes need a running Node server that Pages doesn't provide.) `vite build` produces a static `dist/` folder, which is exactly what Pages serves.
- **Tailwind CSS** for styling. Establish a distinctive visual identity, not a default-template look (see Design Principles).
- **Charts/visualizers** (compound-interest slider, inflation then-vs-now) as self-contained components — Recharts or hand-rolled SVG.

**Two GitHub Pages gotchas to get right up front:**
- **Base path:** Pages serves a project site from `username.github.io/repo-name/`, so set `base: '/repo-name/'` in `vite.config.ts` or every asset 404s. (A custom domain serves from root and removes this.)
- **Client-side routing:** Pages has no SPA fallback, so refreshing a deep link 404s. Use React Router's `HashRouter` for the MVP (zero-config, bulletproof), or a `404.html` redirect for clean URLs.

**Deployment:** the official GitHub Actions method — in Settings → Pages set Source to "GitHub Actions," then a workflow builds and deploys on every push to `main` using GitHub's own `configure-pages` / `upload-pages-artifact` / `deploy-pages` actions. Free, no tokens or secrets. (Avoid the older `gh-pages` npm package / `peaceiris` action seen in many tutorials.)

**Architecture:**
- A reusable **Lesson engine** rendering the shared loop: `storyBeat → decisionPoint → consequence → teaching → quiz`.
- Lessons authored as **data** (one file per lesson, JSON/TS) so non-engineers can edit story and quiz copy without touching code. Suggested shape per lesson: `id`, `title`, `arcRole` (problem/hope/vehicle/catch/fix/action), `characters`, and `beats[]` — ordered segments typed `story | decision | consequence | teaching | quiz`. Decision beats carry choices and the consequence each leads to; quiz beats carry questions, options, and friendly feedback.
- A **character registry** (name, age, one-line role, background, voice notes) referenced by lessons for consistent portrayal.
- A lightweight **progress indicator**, persisted in `localStorage` (works normally on a real Pages deploy; keep persistence swappable).

**Build order:**
1. Lesson engine + character registry + one fully authored lesson (**Lesson 1 — Inflation**, built around Grandma's $100 pay envelope).
2. The compound-interest interactive (Lesson 2) — its visual "wow" is the flagship moment.
3. Remaining four investing lessons.
4. Module wrapper: intro (the garage/pay-envelope scene), the $100 Challenge framing, and a closing capstone.

**Constraints & guardrails:**
- Any numbers presented as fact (inflation rates, historical prices, compound-growth figures) should be computed transparently in-code or clearly labeled as illustrative.
- Keep all content original — no copyrighted lyrics, quotes, or third-party curriculum text.

**Escape hatch:** GitHub Pages can't run serverless functions. If Compound later needs a backend (accounts syncing progress across devices, analytics, email, anything with secrets), move the repo to **Cloudflare Pages** (or Netlify/Vercel) — same free static hosting and git-push deploy, with serverless functions available, and almost no code change.

**First milestone:** a running local app with the lesson engine + Lesson 1 (Inflation) fully playable end-to-end — story beat, decision point, consequence, teaching, quiz — using Grandma's $100 as the hook.
