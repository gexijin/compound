# Full Price — Working Design Document

*The single source of truth during the design phase: cast, spine, and rules in one self-contained document. The chapter plan gets built on top once this is locked. Money figures appear where the story needs them and are subject to a separate fact-checking pass.*

**The book:** an adult book-club novel, funny and fast, that quietly educates and encourages young women to handle money. Keisha Carter, 19, first in her family to enroll in college, spends one Sioux Falls summer trying to close a $7,800 tuition gap entirely alone — grinding fryer shifts, running an agua fresca stand, and riding a boy's stock tips — while her mother and grandmother, unknown to her and to each other, are responding separately to the same bill. Three rescues, one number, three women paying separately for the same fear. The family disease is not lying; it is *solitary rescue* — loving people by carrying things alone, in a family that accepts money more easily than it discusses money — and the climax is three women saying their numbers out loud at one kitchen table.

**Voice and register:** first-person present tense, Keisha, no hindsight — funny, specific, observant, evasive; panic converted into systems. Comps: *Margo's Got Money Troubles* (broke-girl voice, zero self-pity), *Come and Get It* (money as exact social texture — real prices, real brands, no authorial judgment), *Black Cake* (the matriarch's history in installments) — on a Sheldon chassis: short chapters, hard outs, a reader who always holds one devastating card the heroine doesn't. The dramatic irony comes from two interleaved strands in close-third past tense:

- **The Essie strand** — five of Estelle's memories (1965, 1976, 1987, 2009, 2021), each triggered by a present-day object Keisha just touched, so the past reads as detonation, not flashback. Rules: one scene, one artifact, ends on an act, no lesson, no hindsight.
- **The Denise strand** — four short interludes revealing the second parallel rescue as it runs: the portal, the Friday shift, the recalculation, the sale.

**The summer calendar** is the connective tissue: Falls Park market Saturdays, Levitt concert crowds Thursday and Friday nights, Keisha's weekday fryer shifts — all counting down to the bill's late-August due date.

## Cast

### Keisha Carter, 19 — narrator

Black; born and raised near-north Sioux Falls; sophomore-to-be in civil engineering at SDSU in Brookings — ordinary infrastructure under stress, a fascination entirely hers and comic in its specificity. She rates the I-29 overpasses on expansion joints, drainage, sightlines, and the quality of ugly repairs; has opinions about culverts; and rejects on principle the claim that bridges are interesting because they *bring people together*. **First person in her family to enroll in college.**

- **Job and money physics:** a fast-food fryer job — weekday lunch shifts, never market days — the wage physics she grew up inside. Hours-for-dollars is the only lever she has ever seen pulled; equity feels like pretend money.
- **Coping style:** she turns panic into systems; she doesn't say she's terrified, she says *archive is technically a filing system*.
- **The vow:** her silence is a vow, not a lie — the kitchen-drawer envelopes are spoken for, and she will not make her mother re-deal them.
- **Campus life:** the SDSU ASCE Student Steel Bridge team is the first place she became known outside her family. In her freshman spring they won their regional competition, then finished anonymously enough at nationals to produce a durable body of team excuses. The group chat calls them regional champions without irony and treats her sophomore return as settled fact.
- **Her part in the bridge:** she was the freshman keeper of the competition rules, caught a prohibited connection before fabrication made it expensive, and earned a place in the timed assembly. The older students knew more engineering and designed most of the bridge; she mattered because she read closely, spoke in time, and practiced until her hands knew the build. This year they expect her to own rules compliance and part of the connection design.
- **Superpower (planted early, fired late):** she reads boring documents — steel-bridge rules in Brookings, a vending ordinance in June, an annuity contract in August. The first two teach her, a little too successfully, that systems yield when she reads their definitions more carefully than everyone else.
- **Money wound:** needing help feels like failure.
- **Right about:** work has value, and asking can cost dignity. **Wrong about:** that work is the only lever, and that grinding substitutes for speaking.
- **After the crash:** she blames Max — externalizing as one more form of hiding — until she hears herself mid-accusation at Estelle's.
- **Arc:** from carrying the number alone to saying it out loud; from one money physics to three; from prosecuting other people's failures to owning her own thumb. As the gap closes around her she mutes the bridge-team chat and lets people who expect her mistake silence for ordinary summer absence. Her victory is not that school gets paid for; it's that she stops disappearing from a life already in motion.

### Estelle Carter, 78 — grandmother, hidden investor, collector

Black; family came up from Arkansas in the Great Migration for packing work; finished eighth grade, then the John Morrell plant at seventeen; widowed young in the mid-1970s, then erased — banks would not deal with her, legally until 1974, in practice longer. Taught herself everything from library books and the business pages, and never felt entitled to say any of it out loud: a woman who stopped school at eighth grade does not lecture; she could always *do* it, never *say* it.

**Her secret timeline:**

- **1976** — first fund account in her own name: a salesman's load fund, the slip filled out in handwriting practiced on scrap paper.
- **1982** — reads about IRAs in the paper and maxes hers, twenty years running.
- **1985** — $1,000 into Deere & Co. at the bottom of the farm crisis; one paper certificate, never sold.
- **1987** — the Morrell strike, then Black Monday: her one panic, sold near the bottom.
- **1989** — back in via a no-load index fund, and she never sells anything again.
- **2009** — opens a 529 for each grandchild: $400 every January into an account bearing a two-year-old's name, with Keisha's portion planned as four annual $5,000 payments.
- **2013** — retires.
- **2021** — shifts the college money to safety just ahead of the crash.

Total: **$900,000, built the slow way** — plus a **$5,000 transfer she makes herself every late summer** against Keisha's bill. Keisha and Denise know Estelle paid $5,000 freshman year, but Estelle never said it would recur; both filed it as a one-time gift, while Estelle privately planned the same payment for all four years.

- **The collector:** fifty years of thrift-store finds curated in the garage like a museum, every object carrying a provenance story that names what she *paid* and never what it's *worth* — entry price public, valuation secret, the whole character in a storytelling tic. The find stories are her displaced autobiography and, it turns out, the teaching all along. She has never once held a sale — until page one. She cannot let go of what she holds: not the finds, not the certificate, not the truth.
- **The refused name:** she never let anyone call her Grandma — grandmas were old women, and she was busy. She is Stell or Miss Estelle; "Essie" is reserved, said by her exactly once, inside her own story. Keisha's unadorned "thanks, Grandma" at the loan signing is the payoff.
- **Guardrails:** the collection is curated, never squalor — and it is **never the piggy bank**; the gap closes through the 529 and labor, full stop. Her vulnerability to Gary is not intelligence — it's fifty years of being unlistened-to.
- **Money wound:** once erased, she built a life around never needing anyone again. **Wrong about:** survival is not the same as living.
- **Arc:** from hiding as safety to teaching money out loud, twice in one week. Understood, not absolved; she does not apologize well, and the book does not make her.

### Denise Carter, ~52 — mother, the middle strand

Black; Estelle's daughter; grocery-store shift supervisor — schedules, coverage, moods, shrink. Runs the household on labeled cash envelopes — GROCERIES, LIGHTS, CAR, SCHOOL — and is flatly excellent at every dollar she can hold; she taught Keisha the cash-drawer liturgy (count it twice, band the twenties, the way she closes the store every night).

- **The gap:** what she lacks is not sense but systems literacy — bursars, portals, aid letters, anything with a login — the structural condition of a first-generation college parent, and never a punchline. Nobody taught her, because Estelle never taught anyone.
- **The revision that powers the book:** she got the parent-portal email in June and read the $7,800 herself. She knows Estelle paid $5,000 freshman year but, because Estelle never discussed it, assumes it was a one-time gift. Denise makes one ordinary opening; Keisha says she is handling it, and Denise does not ask a second money question. She quietly begins her own rescue: the Friday supervisor shifts nobody wants.
- **The car:** the household car needs an approximately $800 repair. Denise has prepared — the CAR envelope covers most of it — but the balance consumes the margin she had assigned to tuition and forces the watch into her arithmetic. The emergency does not expose incompetence; it makes one well-planned dollar do two jobs.
- **The watch:** Estelle gave her the old men's watch when she made supervisor, saying only that it had belonged to her father. Denise wears it on important workdays, unaware that Estelle once sold it and secretly found it again years later. After the car repair breaks her plan, Denise takes it to a dealer once, intending to learn its value, and sells when the offer makes the tuition math possible.
- **Adaptation:** her lifelong habit is not-asking; performed certainty is her camouflage. She believes a competent mother anticipates need without making her child ask.
- **Private want:** she has protected Keisha's late-August move-in day on the store calendar. More than making a payment, she wants to be included in her daughter's return to Brookings, and cannot quite say so.
- **Arc:** from anticipating in silence to asking and staying in the answer. The small, concrete 529 question at the climax demonstrates that asking can be survived; her anger at Estelle demonstrates that speaking does not require instant forgiveness.
- **The runner:** she keeps meaning to visit the stand and never does — she's the supervisor covering everyone else's Friday — until one late-season stop: one cup, full price. Seeing the stand busy persuades her that Keisha can contribute more than she feared, but not enough for Denise to stake the bill on money they have never counted together.

### Sofía "Sofi" Ortiz, 16 — the watched one

Mexican-American; the neighbor girl — the Ortiz family has lived three doors down since before she was born, family in everything but blood. Supplies the agua fresca recipe (page one: a pitcher carried over for the sweating sorting crew — the stand is born in one collective gulp).

- **The invoice:** she has translated, interpreted, and helped for free her whole life; the recipe is the first contribution anyone tried to put a number on. She recognizes unpaid value faster than the older girls, invoices the recipe, and the amendment gives her a real per-batch royalty plus a vote — and the recipe never transfers with the business; it is hers.
- **Non-thematic want:** her learner's permit, over-seriously studied and casually denied — comic texture, not subplot.
- **Function:** the fourth generation by proximity, not blood — the pattern travels through anyone who's paying attention. The final image is hers.

### Lourdes Ortiz, ~40s — Sofi's mother, counter-wisdom

Mexican-American; cash, caution, liquidity, family obligation.

- **Not backward, not folklore:** her distrust has receipts — one bank account frozen years ago over a mismatched middle name; three weeks, four trips, one fee, zero apologies; told once, never as a lesson.
- **The invoice scene:** argues love's terms over the invoice (in this family you do not bill the people you love), then, handed the guardian pen, reads every line before signing.
- **JazzFest:** she's the one who grabbed the cash box when the crowd surged — the false alarm with real consequences.

### Sydney Ellis, 21 — chaos engine

White; the Ellis kids grew up four blocks over, family friends since grade school. Retail job, bills, a credit-card balance, and the feeling that everyone else is living louder. She spends out loud because pleasure feels like proof she exists — and she notices everyone's mood first.

- **Value discovery:** finds the Levitt concert crowds — the stand's real value discovery, made by the person everyone calls irresponsible.
- **The warning:** her warning to Keisha lands *mid-winning-streak* — warning someone off a bet that's up triple digits is the brave version.
- **The priced choice:** the Premier Center ticket sold in front of everyone, the card paid down, mourned a little — Keisha's dress rehearsal for costs said out loud.
- **Arc:** from spending to prove value to choosing joy with eyes open. The book is not anti-spending; Sydney is why.

### Max Ellis, 24 — fast-money foil, light love interest

White; Sydney's brother; a warehouse job he hates, where the shift genuinely runs whether he's on it or not. Smart, scared, charming, seduced by speed; dreams of retiring by thirty-five; finance-bro register applied to cup sizes and flirting. Shows up at the garage sale flashing one green screenshot and keeps showing up unneeded.

- **The pressure valve:** he becomes Keisha's safe confession — he won't worry, won't sacrifice, won't tell her mother — which is exactly what makes her silence sustainable.
- **The mechanics:** teaches her to open her own brokerage account and mirror his trades — her money, her thumb, her loss; no handover, no debt.
- **The crash:** crashes on the page, crawls back to the warehouse, opens the boring account publicly — **and doesn't really learn:** by the coda he has "diversified" into a new conviction ticker, boring account holding $85.
- **The apology:** bad — vague, weather-ish; she forgives him anyway, having learned that waiting for the perfect apology is one more way of not moving.
- **Boundary:** he stays entirely outside the family plot, forever. Ends warm and undefined: a boy at correct valuation, a laugh across a split check.

### Emily Larsen, 18 — math brain

White; Norwegian-Lutheran, fourth-generation Sioux Falls; a home that is kind, solvent-seeming, and has never once discussed money out loud — concern is expressed in hotdish. Sydney's opposite number: one money culture performs, one never speaks; both are broke in ways nobody names.

- **The instrument:** terrifying precision, spreadsheets with absurd filenames; owns the book's teaching instrument, the triptych — Keisha's fryer per-hour, the stand per-hour, and Max's screenshot on one table, no lecture attached.
- **The cracked phone:** holds the banking app she refuses to open — until she quietly does, weeks before anyone notices; Keisha discovers the stray transfer notation late, evidence that the door opens quietly.
- **Aftermath:** institutes the two-person count after JazzFest.
- **Money wound:** exact truth feels more dangerous than guessing — she trusts numbers except the one that belongs to her.

### Gary — the advisor

*Sunrise Retirement Solutions.* Pleasant, patient, remembers Estelle's late husband's name, laughs at her jokes; found her through the senior-center channel and planted the "simplify your estate" idea behind the garage sale Denise thinks she won.

- **The contract:** surrender charges, a 7% commission dressed in jargon, a signing "incentive" expiring on a circled date — legitimate advice never comes with a countdown.
- **Screen time:** three scenes total, no raised voice; defeated by being *read*, and leaves politely, because his species always does.
- **The ladder:** third rung of the predator ladder — the 1976 fund salesman, the concessionaire, Gary: three men across three eras pricing women's work and money at scrap value.
- **Scale:** Tuesday-sized, always — real financial predation is boring and patient, and depicting it accurately is the lesson.

### The concessionaire

Food-truck owner, liked by the girls all summer; offers $600 for booth and "brand" — nine days after an anonymous permit complaint nobody can pin on him. Shrugs when asked: "that's business." Buys a cup. Stays a market fixture; hints at the good corner for next June. One shade dark, no darker.

## The spine — five acts

### Act 1 — The Garage

Denise has finally talked Estelle into holding her first-ever garage sale. For a week, Keisha and her friends sort fifty years of Estelle's thrift-store finds while Estelle tells the story behind every object and keeps pulling things back out of the donation boxes. Denise wears the old men's watch Estelle gave her when she was promoted to supervisor; Estelle pays the watch a little too much attention, which everyone reads as her usual possessiveness about objects.

- **The envelope.** Sorting, Keisha finds a 1965 pay envelope from the John Morrell meatpacking plant — pay stub and a folded twenty-dollar bill still inside. Estelle takes it back and says it is not for sale.
- **→ Essie: 1965.** Flashback (Estelle's memory, triggered by the envelope): Estelle at seventeen, working at the plant, secretly folding one twenty back out of her pay — the first money she ever hid.
- **The stand is born.** Sofi brings over a pitcher of her family's agua fresca for the sorting crew. It is so good the girls decide to sell it at the Falls Park farmers market, which has no decent drink stall.
- **Max arrives.** Max shows up at the sale with nothing to do, showing everyone a screenshot of a winning stock trade on his phone. Keisha finds him annoying, and notices him anyway.
- **The bill.** Keisha's tuition bill arrives: **$7,800, due late August**. Estelle paid $5,000 freshman year, but never said there would be more; Keisha assumes the gift was one-time money from a poor woman and will not ask again. She decides on the spot to cover the new bill herself — her mother's cash envelopes are already stretched thin, and she won't add to that load. In the same hour, her college bridge-team group chat is planning the fall season and assuming she'll be back. She doesn't reply.
- **→ Denise strand 1: the portal.** Interlude (Denise's point of view): Denise already saw the $7,800 bill in a parent-portal email in June. She too assumes Estelle's freshman-year $5,000 was a one-time gift. Denise makes one ordinary opening; Keisha says she is handling school, and Denise lets the answer stand because these women do not ask second questions about money. Then she quietly starts her own rescue: she picks up the unwanted Friday supervisor shifts at the grocery store and blocks off Keisha's move-in day on the work calendar.
- **The stake.** Estelle invests $30 in the stand for a 30% share. Her thirty dollars are the stand's starting cash.

**Act out:** the deal is signed. Keisha and Denise each believe the full $7,800 is uncovered; Estelle knows her next $5,000 payment is coming but not that the other two are separately panicking over the whole bill. Three rescues are now running blind.

### Act 2 — The Stand

The stand fails on day one over a permit, gets legal thanks to Keisha's close reading of city rules, and finally finds its real customers — while Keisha opens a brokerage account and starts gambling her savings.

- **Day-one defeat.** First market Saturday: the girls are turned away at setup because they don't have a vendor permit, and processing takes three weeks. The ice melts in the parking lot. Estelle's $30 is gone with nothing to show for it.
- **The ordinance.** Keisha spends the week reading the city's vending ordinance line by line — the same way she reads competition rules for her college bridge team — finds a classification and a temporary authorization that fit the stand, and gets it legal before the next weekend. She draws a lesson from this that will hurt her later: any system pays off if you just read its rules more carefully than everyone else.
- **The flop, and the liturgy.** The stand's legal debut still flops — ten customers, and church ladies asking how many weeks until she leaves for school. That evening Denise counts the small take with Keisha the way she closes the store register: count it twice, band the twenties. Emily is there, her cracked phone face-down on the counter, and hides a bank-transfer alert when it lights up. (The reader can see that Emily has secretly started using the banking app she claims she can't bring herself to open. Keisha doesn't notice; this pays off in Act 5.)
- **Value discovery.** Sydney figures out where the real customers are: the big evening concert crowds at the Levitt — young, hot, thirsty, with no drinks for sale nearby. The stand takes off.
- **The buy-back.** Keisha spots Estelle at a neighbor's garage sale buying back — at a higher price — an item Estelle herself just sold. Keisha shrugs it off. (The point, which pays off at the climax: Estelle cannot let go of anything she owns.)
- **The valve opens.** Keisha tells Max about the $7,800. Not because she trusts him — because he's safe: he won't worry about her, won't sacrifice anything for her, and won't tell her mother. Having one person to tell makes the secret easier to carry, which keeps it going.
- **The account.** Fresh off beating city hall by reading the rules, Keisha decides the stock market is the same kind of system. Max walks her through opening her own brokerage account, and she starts copying his trades with her own savings — her money, her decisions, no debt to him. His winning streak is pure luck in a confident voice. The reader can see that; Keisha can't.

**Act out:** the account is open and rising. Everything from here rides on a lucky streak that has to end.

### Act 3 — The Boom

The stand and Keisha's stock position both climb — and every win quietly sets up a problem.

- **The offer.** A food-truck owner (the concessionaire) offers the girls $600 for the stand and its "brand." Emily notices the offer came nine days after someone filed an anonymous complaint about their permit. Nothing can be proven; he shrugs: "that's business."
- **The vote.** The girls vote by paper slip whether to sell: 3–1 against. Nobody knows who voted *sell*. It was Keisha.
- **The invoice.** Sofi presents the group with an invoice for her recipe. Her mother Lourdes objects — in this family you don't bill the people you love — but when she's handed the pen to sign as Sofi's guardian, she reads every line first. The amended partnership gives Sofi a per-batch royalty and a vote, and the recipe stays her property.
- **→ Essie: 1976.** Flashback, triggered by watching the girls amend a contract: 1976, a patient fund salesman, and Estelle opening her first investment account — her signature practiced beforehand on scrap paper.
- **→ Denise strand 2: the Friday shift.** Interlude: Denise at the grocery store, where she is visibly excellent — solves a staffing problem, catches a register discrepancy, turns down a shift on Keisha's move-in weekend but takes yet another Friday. The extra-shift money is finally adding up to something that could matter for tuition. Then, after closing, the family car won't start.
- **The car.** The repair costs about $800. Denise's CAR envelope covers most of it; the rest quietly comes out of the Friday-shift money she was saving for tuition. Keisha offers to chip in; Denise refuses because "you need every dollar for school"; neither one says what she is actually saving for. Watching a real emergency strain her mother's envelope system convinces Keisha she was right to keep her bill out of the household.
- **The liquidity trap.** Keisha's school housing deposit comes due, but her savings are now all in the stock — which is climbing, so she refuses to sell any of it. She pays the deposit out of her fryer wages instead, and then, short on cash, covers a $60 ice bill by borrowing from the stand's cash box without telling anyone, planning to put it back before the next count. One bad decision has now caused a second, and the borrow breaks the one money rule her mother explicitly taught her. Meanwhile the bridge-team chat assigns her the rules binder and part of the design for fall; riding paper profits, she confidently says yes to everything.
- **The mail.** On an errand, Keisha handles Estelle's mail and sees an envelope from an investment company. She files it as ordinary retirement mail from a woman without much to retire on.
- **→ Essie: 2009.** Flashback: January 2009, Estelle opens a college account in two-year-old Keisha's name, makes the first $400 deposit, and lays out four annual $5,000 payments for the future. (The reader now knows last year's apparent one-time gift belongs to a continuing system. Keisha still doesn't.)
- **JazzFest.** The stand's biggest night: record lines. Denise finally visits, buys one cup at full price, and takes the busy stand as evidence that Keisha can contribute meaningfully — but she will not stake the bill on money they have never counted together. The same night, Keisha's stock hits its peak, and she is about to sell — enough to nearly close the gap — when the cash box disappears in a crowd surge. An hour of panic; suspicion falls on the wrong vendor's kid. Keisha stops the accusation the only way she can: by demanding a full audit of the money, which means first confessing, in front of everyone, the $60 she borrowed. Then Lourdes walks up carrying the box — she grabbed it for safety when the crowd surged, and nobody passed the word. There was no theft; her confession stands anyway; and the selling moment has passed. Keisha lets the position ride. It never gets back to that price.
- **The supper table.** The three Carter women share an ordinary supper — hotdish, weather talk — each carrying her own rescue, Keisha checking her stock under the table. They comfortably discuss everything except money; the tuition bill is never mentioned.
- **→ Denise strand 3: the recalculation.** Interlude: Denise redoes her math. The car repair took too much, and the remaining Fridays can't rebuild the fund before the deadline. She takes off her father's watch, sets it next to the written arithmetic, and puts it in her purse — not yet to sell it, just to find out what it's worth.

**Act out:** Emily lays three numbers side by side on one table — what the fryer job pays per hour, what the stand pays per hour, and what Max's screenshot claims — and that same evening Sydney, the loudest spender in the book, flatly tells Keisha to get out of the stock while she's ahead.

### Act 4 — The Bust

The stock crashes, Keisha loses everyone she could talk to — and then, broke and out of options, she stumbles onto her grandmother's secret.

- **The press.** Keisha ignores both warnings and puts in more: she takes an approved advance on her stand earnings and adds it to the position. Each step feels reasonable to her at the time — first hope, then greed, then desperation.
- **The red morning.** The stock drops 80%. Her savings and the advance are gone together. Nobody did this to her: her account, her thumb, her loss.
- **→ Essie: 1987.** Flashback: the Morrell plant strike, then Black Monday 1987 — Estelle's one panic, selling near the bottom of the crash. The boring Deere stock certificate survived only because it was paper in a drawer, too slow to sell in a panic.
- **Max's public bottom.** Max is wiped out too. He goes back to the warehouse job he swore he'd escaped and — because his bragging was public — publicly asks Emily how to open a boring index-fund account.
- **The freeze.** Keisha cuts Max off completely and rewrites the summer as a story where the crash was his fault. She mutes the bridge-team chat too, right as they post the fall schedule and ask her to bring the rules binder. Now she has no one to talk to at all, and the secret becomes unbearable.
- **→ Denise strand 4: the sale.** Interlude: on Tuesday, Denise takes the watch to a reputable dealer, meaning only to learn its value. She asks good questions, gets the offer in writing, hesitates once — and sells it in the same visit, because the money makes her tuition math work. The receipt goes in her purse, the proceeds are set aside untouched for the bill, and outside she checks the time on her bare wrist.
- **The detective work.** With no money moves left, Keisha goes to Estelle's to help sort a box of paperwork, and starts finding things that make no sense in a poor woman's garage: a dividend statement; a John Deere stock certificate decades old; paperwork from the investment company whose envelope she handled — and, newest of all, a glossy folder from Sunrise Retirement Solutions with a salesman's business card and a date circled in someone else's handwriting, eleven days away.
- **Reading Gary.** Keisha arranges to be at Estelle's when Gary the advisor visits, and watches her grandmother light up — someone is finally listening to her talk about money, for the first time in fifty years. That's the hook: loneliness, not stupidity. Keisha borrows the contract overnight and reads it the way she read the vending ordinance. It's a bad deal dressed in jargon: surrender charges, a 7% commission, a signing "incentive" that expires on the circled date. In the paperwork she also finds "simplifying your estate" in Gary's handwriting — the garage sale that opened the book was his idea planted months ago, not Denise's victory. Estelle let Denise believe the nagging had finally worked.
- **The shame visit.** Before Keisha can decide what to do, Estelle comes to her — she cannot stand a witness to being nearly conned. She trades her story for Keisha's silence: eighth-grade education, the plant, widowhood, banks that wouldn't deal with her, teaching herself investing from library books, building wealth back slowly over fifty years — and a 529 college account she has kept in Keisha's name since Keisha was two. Last year's $5,000 was not an improvised sacrifice but the first of four annual payments; the next one was always coming. The one thing she refuses to say is how much money she has: she has never spoken the total aloud to anyone, and won't now. She calls herself "Essie" exactly once, inside her own story.
- **The mirror.** Keisha, mid-accusation — "you built an entire system of secret help so you would never have to have one honest conversation" — hears the words describe herself. She confesses her whole summer to Estelle: the hidden bill, the borrowed $60, the trading account, the crash.

**Act out:** Estelle names her price for all this honesty — Denise must never know any of it, and Keisha must never make her say the total out loud.

### Act 5 — The Table

Keisha has eleven days — Gary's circled date — to get everything said out loud before Estelle signs a bad contract.

- **The negotiation.** Keisha refuses to keep Estelle's secret, and also refuses to go behind her back to Denise. That leaves one path: Estelle tells Denise herself, at the family table, by Sunday. Keisha wins the argument using Estelle's own logic: a fortune nobody knows about is a fortune nobody can help defend — hiding stopped being safe the day Gary found her.
- **→ Essie: 2021.** Flashback: 2021, Estelle moves the college money into safe investments just before the market drops. One phone call, one confirmation slip — she has been actively watching over this plan the whole time.
- **Two rehearsals.** In the days before the dinner, Keisha watches two friends do small versions of the hard thing. Sydney sells her Premier Center concert ticket in front of everyone to pay down her credit card, and lets herself be sad about it. And Keisha finally notices the old transfer records showing Emily opened her bank account weeks ago and simply never announced it.

**The kitchen-table dinner** — one scene, three confessions in a chain:

1. **Keisha goes first:** the hidden bill, the borrowed $60, the trading account, the crash — with every cost stated. Mid-confession, Estelle needles her: *you told the Ellis boy before your own mother.*
2. **That needling backfires and forces Estelle's turn.** She does the thing she swore she never would: says the number out loud — **$900,000**, built over fifty years — plus the 529 in Keisha's name since age two and the four-year payment plan, with Gary's folder on the table as proof of why hiding has become dangerous. (The reader has known since the 2009 flashback that the $5,000 was a continuing plan; the *size* of the fortune is the one fact the book saved for this moment.)
3. **That breaks Denise open.** She leaves the room and comes back with three things: her phone showing the parent portal she's been logged into since June, the dealer's receipt, and the untouched proceeds. *I've known all summer. I thought last year was one time too. I sold Daddy's watch Tuesday.*

Then the blow the whole scene was built for: Estelle recognizes the dealer's name on the receipt. The watch was her late husband's — the one thing poverty forced her to sell decades ago, and the one thing she ever secretly bought back, years later, before giving it to Denise as "your father's watch" without ever telling that story. Denise, trying to rescue her daughter, has unknowingly sold the one object in the family that had already been lost once. Three women paid separately for the same fear, and this is the only cost that can't be refunded.

Before the scene ends, Denise asks Estelle one small, concrete question about the 529 — out loud — and waits for the answer: the exact thing her whole life of not-asking was built to avoid. She also names the damage: *you let me learn everything the hard way because that was how you learned it.* Estelle has to sit with that; she doesn't get to apologize her way out, and the book doesn't force forgiveness.

- **Gary's appointment.** A short scene, not a showdown: Gary arrives to find three women and his contract covered in margin notes. Estelle reads him two of his own surrender clauses and asks one question about his commission. Keisha and Denise don't say a word — they don't need to; his whole pitch was designed for a woman alone. He leaves politely.
- **The transfer.** Estelle's $5,000 posts to the bursar days before the due date — as it was always going to. That leaves $2,800 as Keisha's true share, and her summer covers it: fryer wages, her share of the stand's profits, the little left after selling the crashed stock, and a campus library job lined up for fall.

**Coda** — every cost gets written down and paid on real terms:

- Estelle lends Keisha laptop money as a real loan — written terms, token interest, both signatures — her second time teaching money out loud in a week, and easier already. Keisha says "thanks, Grandma": the first time in her life anyone has been allowed to call Estelle that.
- Keisha repays the lost stand advance out of her library-job paychecks.
- Estelle — the family's great finder of lost objects — hunts for the watch, but the dealer has already sold it on. Some prices stay paid. The sale money stays visible on the family's new shared ledger instead of disappearing with the object.
- Max apologizes badly and vaguely; Keisha forgives him anyway, having learned that waiting for the perfect apology is one more way of standing still. They split a check; he's already excited about a new "sure thing" stock. Warm, undefined, correctly valued.
- Sofi gets a custodial investment account, with the learner's-permit errand next on the list; the concessionaire hints the stand can have the good corner next June.
- Move-in day — the day Denise blocked off in June: Keisha drives the repaired car to Brookings with Denise beside her, bedding and the bridge-team rules binder packed, rating the I-29 overpasses as they go. Next year's tuition gap and Estelle's planned $5,000 contribution are written on the family ledger where everyone can see them, instead of hidden in separate heads. The bridge chat asks who has the binder; *I have it*, she answers, and nobody makes a fuss, because they never imagined she'd left. Sofi watches the car go the way Keisha once watched Estelle — the family pattern still passes down, but now it talks.

## Design rules

1. **Every bang buys a release** — no crisis beat may stand without relief within a beat or two, but the relief need not be quiet: a lull, a laugh, or a card cashing all count. The book breathes through comedy and resolution, not only stillness. The supper chapter is the model (highest suspense in the book, released through hotdish and weather); the crash must not stand alone.
2. **Villains stay Tuesday-sized** — Gary never raises his voice; the concessionaire stays liked and unproven; real predation is boring and patient, and depicting it accurately is the lesson.
3. **The comedy quota is non-negotiable** — group chat, church ladies, find-story provenance, the returned cash box. Funny is the delivery mechanism: the reader finishes because she was laughing and discovers she can explain surrender charges at brunch.
4. **Entertain first, then educate** — no drama-only scenes. The lesson ledger: say the number out loud; three physics of money (wages, ownership, compounding) plus speed, the counterfeit fourth; read before you sign; never risk money you can't lose; paper gains aren't money; liquidity — money in the market isn't money on Tuesday; price your work; the float is sacred; small confessions are survivable; asking is a financial skill; isolation is what predators target — disclosure is armor; slow and boring survives; consequences don't automatically produce wisdom (Max); help with terms is care, not rescue.
5. **The collection is never the piggy bank**, and Max never touches the family plot.
6. **Keisha's mistakes stay locally rational** — hope, then greed, then desperation; the book is anti-hiding, never anti-mistake.
7. **The bridge metaphor stays structural, never spoken** — no building bridges, carrying loads, or strong foundations in dialogue or narration. Keisha's engineering remains technical, funny, material, and allowed to be wrong outside its domain.
8. **Campus life is a pressure line, not a subplot** — three summer touches and one coda payoff; no named teammate arc, competition flashback chapter, or fall-contest plot. The team assumes continuity. Keisha is the only person entertaining her disappearance.
