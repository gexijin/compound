# Full Price — Working Design Document

**NOTE** This document is updated more often than the cast doc. Finished chapter drafts are canon over both — where a draft and this document disagree, the draft wins.

**The book:** an adult book-club novel, funny and fast, that quietly educates and encourages young women to handle money. Keisha Carter, 19, first in her family to enroll in college, spends one Sioux Falls summer (2026) trying to close a $7,800 tuition gap entirely alone — grinding fryer shifts, running an agua fresca stand, and copying a neighborhood friend's stock trades — while her mother and grandmother, unknown to her and to each other, are responding separately to the same bill. Three rescues, one number, three women paying separately for the same fear. The family disease is not lying; it is *solitary rescue* — loving people by carrying things alone, in a family that accepts money more easily than it discusses money — and the climax is three women saying their numbers out loud at one kitchen table — said in time, because the same silence that ran three blind rescues has also let a patient salesman spend months courting the family fortune.

**Voice and register:** first-person present tense, Keisha, no hindsight — funny, specific, observant, evasive; panic converted into systems. Comps: *Margo's Got Money Troubles* (broke-girl voice, zero self-pity), *Come and Get It* (money as exact social texture — real prices, real brands, no authorial judgment), *Black Cake* (the matriarch's history in installments) — on a Sheldon chassis: short chapters, hard outs, a reader who always holds one devastating card the heroine doesn't. A third card runs inside Keisha's own strand: the reader identifies Gary's presence acts before she does — the Tuesday gentleman, the swag, the supper-table sentence — so his Act 4 discovery is drafted as **convergence** (reader and heroine finally aligning), never revelation. The dramatic irony comes from two interleaved strands in close-third past tense:

- **The Essie strand** — four of Estelle's memories (1965, 1976, 1987, 2009), each detonated by something in the present-day scene, so the past reads as detonation, not flashback. Rules: one scene, ends on an act, no lesson, no hindsight.
- **The Denise strand** — four short interludes revealing the second parallel rescue as it runs: the portal, the Sunday shift, the recalculation, the sale. The portal lands as a hard cut directly after the Act 1 chat detonation — the reader learns Keisha's silence was pointless thirty seconds after watching her choose it. (Header canon, per the drafts: "Denise, Week N.")

**The summer calendar** is the connective tissue: Falls Park market Saturdays, Levitt concert crowds Thursday and Friday nights, Keisha's weekday fryer shifts — all counting down to the bill's late-August due date.

**The clock.** Time is tracked by week-of-summer plus day-of-week; weeks run Saturday→Friday, each opening on a market Saturday (Wk 1 Sat = the sale, mid-June; drafting calendar is 2026 actual, Wk 1 Sat = June 13, all weekdays verified). Keisha's narration counts weeks remaining, never dates. Exactly three literal calendar dates exist in the book, all institutional, all arriving in someone else's type or ink: the bursar's due date (**Fri Aug 28**), Gary's circled Monday (**Aug 24**), and move-in day (**Sat Aug 29**). Granularity tightens with the noose: in the narration, Acts 1–2 live at week level (days exist only as rhythm — market Saturday, the standing Tuesday), Act 3 starts naming weekdays, Act 4 counts in days, Act 5 resolves into one stacked, dated week. Essie's strand keeps its year-dated headers — the past gets fixed dates because it has already happened; the present tense never knows its own date that confidently.

### Act 2 — The Stand · Wks 2–5 · Chs 4–6 (Chs 4–5 **DRAFTED**)

The stand fails on day one over a permit, gets legal thanks to Keisha's close reading of city rules, and finally finds its real customers — while Keisha opens a brokerage account and starts gambling her savings.

**Story so far, as drafted.**

*Ch 4 — The Permit (Wk 2 Sat → Wk 3 Sat).* Day-one defeat at Falls Park: no vendor permit, three-week processing, thirty pounds of ice melts in the parking lot — Emily's ledger entry: revenue zero, ice $30, note *permit*. The second-job runner executes: both starred listings write back and both want the exact hours the fryer and the market already own (clothing store wants Saturdays; the warehouse pays $19.40 overnight but ends at six, when market setup starts); the manager's ceiling in one line — "Part-timers stay under thirty. Nothing personal" — and 29.5 hours scheduled. The ordinance read across shifts' breaks: fourteen pages, the temporary-vending paragraph past the ice cream trucks (sponsorship of a licensed market, over-the-counter authorization), two clerks saying "huh," the $10 permit, laminated. The dangerous lesson stated on the steps: everything that has said no this summer is a document, and documents can be out-read. Estelle's Tuesday count: **$16**, defeated; the lamp repriced to $1 ("owned by a company losing money"). Heron war resumes (the retiree's "You'll get there"). The relief priced in narration: said out loud it's just a number — easier to carry, which is exactly what keeps the carrying going. The split pitch: Max is half ticker, half bitcoin. **She mirrors the ticker only and declines the coin** — the ticker is a valve-and-housing company with filings ("we have never been profitable and may never achieve profitability" — the most honest sentence a document has said to her all summer; two customers are most of revenue, admitted on page 61); the coin publishes nothing, so she has no advantage ("Reading is my advantage"). The second-lever framing paid: the account is the second job she's been hunting since June, no ceiling anywhere in the paperwork — it just pays in *maybe*. **Remaining:**

- **[Ch 6 · Wk 5] The boom begins.** Four beats, one chapter:
  - **The projection.** The stand grows about 25% a week; Emily uses the rate to project supply buys — then, extending the same trend line, announces flatly that they will all be millionaires by Christmas. She means it; everyone else laughs. The earnest forecast is also a plant: the book's first quiet lesson in why curves don't run forever.
  - **[Thu–Fri nights] Value discovery.** Sydney figures out where the real customers are: the big evening concert crowds at the Levitt — young, hot, thirsty, with no drinks for sale nearby. The stand takes off.
  - **The invoice.** Sofi presents the group with an invoice for her recipe. Her mother Lourdes objects — in this family you don't bill the people you love — but when she's handed the pen to sign as Sofi's guardian, she reads every line first.   - **The drop-off (thirty seconds — Gary's first of three scenes).** That week, a sedan drops Estelle home from the center: a wave, a pleasant word through the window, gone. Sorting a junk drawer, Keisha files a glossy Sunrise pen without reading it. The reader connects 1976 salesman → Tuesday gentleman → that logo; Keisha reads past all three.

**Act out:** the account is open and rising. Everything from here rides on a lucky streak that has to end.

### Act 3 — The Boom · Wks 6–8 · Chs 7–10

The stand and Keisha's stock position both climb — and every win quietly sets up a problem.

- **[Ch 7 · Wk 6, Thu night] The walk.** A record Levitt Thursday; teardown ends near midnight, the hatchback loaded, and instead of driving home they drift the few blocks to Falls Park in the dark — her territory with a meaning he doesn't know, the heron's water with nothing to hunt in it. The coin argument starts as standing material and softens mid-sentence; she tells him about the heron — three summers, the retiree, the $3,000 lens — which for Keisha is nearly the whole vault; his hand and hers on the railing, and she doesn't move it. No kiss, no label, no witnesses — midnight after teardown puts them past the last of the market-and-Levitt eyes, and the scene says so on the page. The tic goes silent for the length of the scene: the one chapter where she rates nothing. *(Chapter money out: none — the metronome's single deliberate rest; the reader, trained on chapters that close on a dollar figure, hears the silence.)* → **Denise, Week 6 (tail): the Sunday shift.** Denise at the grocery store, where she is visibly excellent — solves a staffing problem, catches a register discrepancy, turns down a shift on Keisha's move-in weekend but takes yet another Sunday. The extra-shift money is finally adding up to something that could matter for tuition. Then, after closing, the family car won't start.
- **[Ch 8 · Wk 6, Sun night → Wk 7] The car, the teasing, the trap.**
  - **The car.** The repair costs about $800. Denise's CAR envelope covers most of it; the rest quietly comes out of the Sunday-shift money she was saving for tuition. Keisha offers to chip in; Denise refuses because "you need every dollar for school"; neither one says what she is actually saving for. Watching a real emergency strain her mother's envelope system convinces Keisha she was right to keep her bill out of the household.
  - **The teasing (beat, not scene).** Market setup: Sofi saw Keisha and Max at the falls from her cousin's car — reported sunnily, as fact. Two beats: Sydney's sibling horror (*my brother?*), and Keisha deflecting with invented axes — *a 6 on ambient humidity, a 2 on birds* — confirming nothing. Nobody asks a second question; the non-denial stands. (Mirror of the Tuesday-gentleman beat, one generation down; the rhyme belongs to the reader.)
  - **The liquidity trap.** Keisha's school housing deposit comes due, but her savings are now all in stocks — which are climbing — so she refuses to sell any of them. She pays the deposit out of her fryer wages instead and, short on cash, covers a $60 ice bill by borrowing from the stand's cash box without telling anyone, planning to put it back before the next count. One bad decision has now caused a second, and the borrow breaks the one money rule her mother explicitly taught her. Meanwhile the bridge-team chat assigns her rules compliance and part of the connection design for fall — riding paper profits, she confidently says yes to everything — including an August design call where silence means someone else takes rules compliance.
- **[Ch 9 · Wk 7, Fri night] JazzFest.** The stand's biggest night: record lines. Denise finally visits, buys one cup at full price, and takes the busy stand as evidence that Keisha can contribute meaningfully — but she will not stake the bill on money they have never counted together. The same night, Keisha's stock hits its peak, and she is about to sell — enough to nearly close the gap — when the cash box disappears in a crowd surge. An hour of panic; suspicion falls on the wrong vendor's kid. Keisha stops the accusation the only way she can: by demanding a full audit of the money, which means first confessing, in front of everyone, the $60 she borrowed. Then Lourdes walks up carrying the box — she grabbed it for safety when the crowd surged, and nobody passed the word. There was no theft; her confession stands anyway; and the selling moment has passed. Keisha lets the position ride. It never gets back to that price. After that night, the stand's cash runs on Emily's two-person count, and Estelle's drop-by recounts of the box stop being a joke. → **Essie, 2009 (tail).** January 2009: Estelle opens a college account in two-year-old Keisha's name, makes the first $400 deposit, and lays out four annual $5,000 payments for the future. (The reader now knows last year's apparent one-time gift belongs to a continuing system. Keisha still doesn't.)
- **[Ch 10 · Wk 8] The warnings, Verna, the supper table.**
  - **[Sat] The warnings.** At market close, Emily lays three numbers side by side on one table — what the fryer job pays per hour, what the stand pays per hour, and what Max's screenshot claims. That evening Sydney, the loudest spender in the book, flatly tells Keisha to get out of the stock while she's ahead — the first time she has ever bet against her brother's entire religion, and this summer she half-knows what else is riding on him.
  - **[Sun] Verna, on the church sidewalk.** Sunday after service, Verna Iverson warmly praises her "nice advisor" to Estelle in Keisha's earshot — he was so nice he "even handled that fee business" last year when she helped her son with his down payment. She credits Gary against "what happened with my computer" — a real person, across a real table, after that voice on the phone; it is the one story she tells without a dollar amount in it. Sweet to everyone present, horrifying to the reader; meaningless to Keisha in the moment. (Her one on-page appearance. Verna never learns what the fee was; Keisha does the math on the overheard fact in Act 4, after reading the contract's surrender schedule, and decodes the computer story there too; both are cited in Act 5.)
  - **[Sun evening] The supper table.** The three Carter women share an ordinary supper — hotdish, weather talk — each carrying her own rescue, Keisha checking her stock under the table. The hotdish is the potluck recipe — the one carrying Denise's decades-long, never-acknowledged rivalry with Sister Deloris Hayes: first pan emptied wins, everyone knows it, no one says it. Somewhere in the weather, Estelle mentions that her fellow from the center has been "helping her simplify." (Drafting note: the sentence is cover, not candor — she's deflecting a question about the sale leftovers or her Tuesdays, and "simplify" lands on Denise as vindication of the downsizing campaign, not as money talk. Estelle volunteers nothing; she redirects.) Nobody asks a second question. (The polarity flip: the reader, holding the 2009 card, watches the family disease hand the predator the room over hotdish — the 529 is no longer safe money offstage; it is money nobody knows to defend.) They comfortably discuss everything except money; the tuition bill is never mentioned.
  - → **Denise, Week 8 (tail): the recalculation.** Denise redoes her math. The car repair took too much, and the remaining Sundays can't rebuild the fund before the deadline. She checks the other doors the way she reads a register tape: no credit card — not one dime, not once, the pride itself; a file so thin the one car loan years back came at a brutal rate, paid off early, of course; a rented house with nothing to borrow against. Every door is closed for the exact reasons she is proud of, and no one ever says so — the counterfactual stays the reader's math. The chain is the only liquid thing she owns. She unclasps it, sets it next to the written arithmetic, and puts it in her purse — not yet to sell it, just to find out what it's worth. (This is the one place the plot prices her no-credit rule, hard.)

**Act out:** the chain is in the purse, the warnings are on the record, and the position is still riding.

### Act 4 — The Bust · Wks 9–10 · Chs 11–12

The stock crashes and Keisha loses everyone she could talk to — until Estelle notices the flinch, asks the family's forbidden second question, and opens her own papers in response.

- **[Ch 11 · Wk 9] The crash, whole.**
  - **[Mon] The press.** Keisha ignores both warnings and puts in more: she takes an approved advance on her stand earnings and adds it to the position. Even Max hesitates at that — *desperation money never wins*, a gambler's proverb he genuinely means and has never once followed — the third warning in a row, ignored like the other two; and this one costs him something to say, because since the falls he is not hesitating at a customer of his confidence but at Keisha, whose hand he held and didn't rate. (It leaves him an *I told you* he never uses.) Each step feels reasonable to her at the time — first hope, then greed, then desperation.
  - **[Thu] The red morning.** The stock drops 80%; the coin wobbles and drifts, unremarked by anyone — Max was half right, and half right is all the religion needs; only the reader clocks which half she mirrored. Her savings and the advance are gone together. Nobody did this to her: her account, her thumb, her loss.
  - **[Fri–Sun] Max's public bottom.** Max is wiped out too. He goes back to the warehouse job he swore he'd escaped and — because his bragging was public — publicly asks Emily how to open a boring index-fund account. The same week the "funds recovery" DMs arrive — his money back for an upfront fee; a loss is a lead — and he shows Emily the best one as a joke that isn't.
  - **The freeze.** Keisha cuts Max off completely and rewrites the summer as a story where the crash was his fault. It is also a breakup — of a thing that never got named, so there is nothing she has to announce: no label to retire, no conversation owed, the whole relationship dissolved in the family's native silence. She has finally had something end, and she conducts the ending the only way she knows — by not saying it. She mutes the bridge-team chat too, right as they post the fall schedule and ask her to bring the rules binder — with the August design call now days away, her silence has a price she could compute and won't. Now she has no one to talk to at all, and the secret becomes unbearable.
- **[Ch 12 · Wk 10, Tue–Thu] The second question, and the papers.**
  - → **Denise, Week 10 (opener): the sale.** On Tuesday, Denise takes the chain to a reputable dealer, meaning only to learn its value. She asks good questions, gets the offer in writing, hesitates once — and sells it in the same visit, because the money makes her tuition math work. ### Act 5 — The Table · Wk 11 · Chs 13–15
