# Full Price — Working Design Document

*The single source of truth during the design phase: cast, spine, and rules in one self-contained document. The chapter plan gets built on top once this is locked. Money figures appear where the story needs them and are subject to a separate fact-checking pass.*

**The book:** an adult book-club novel, funny and fast, that quietly educates and encourages young women to handle money. Keisha Carter, 19, first in her family to enroll in college, spends one Sioux Falls summer trying to close a $7,800 tuition gap entirely alone — grinding fryer shifts, running an agua fresca stand, and riding a boy's stock tips — while her mother and grandmother, unknown to her and to each other, are quietly solving the same problem. Three rescues, one number, three women paying separately for the same fear. The family disease is not lying; it is *solitary rescue* — loving people by carrying things alone — and the climax is three women saying their numbers out loud at one kitchen table.

**Voice and register:** first-person present tense, Keisha, no hindsight — funny, specific, observant, evasive; panic converted into systems. Comps: *Margo's Got Money Troubles* (broke-girl voice, zero self-pity), *Come and Get It* (money as exact social texture — real prices, real brands, no authorial judgment), *Black Cake* (the matriarch's history in installments) — on a Sheldon chassis: short chapters, hard outs, a reader who always holds one devastating card the heroine doesn't. The dramatic irony comes from two interleaved strands in close-third past tense:

- **The Essie strand** — five of Estelle's memories (1965, 1976, 1987, 2009, 2021), each triggered by a present-day object Keisha just touched, so the past reads as detonation, not flashback. Rules: one scene, one artifact, ends on an act, no lesson, no hindsight.
- **The Denise strand** — four short interludes revealing the second parallel rescue as it runs: the portal, the Friday shift, the recalculation, the sale.

**The summer calendar** is the connective tissue: Falls Park market Saturdays, Levitt concert crowds Thursday and Friday nights, Keisha's weekday fryer shifts — all counting down to the bill's late-August due date.

## Cast

### Keisha Carter, 19 — narrator

Black; born and raised near-north Sioux Falls; sophomore-to-be in civil engineering at SDSU in Brookings — ordinary infrastructure under stress, a fascination entirely hers and comic in its specificity. She rates the I-29 overpasses on expansion joints, drainage, sightlines, and the quality of ugly repairs; has opinions about culverts; and rejects on principle the claim that bridges are interesting because they *bring people together*. **First person in her family to enroll in college.**

- **Job and money physics:** a fast-food fryer job — weekday lunch shifts, never market days — the wage physics she grew up inside. Hours-for-dollars is the only lever she has ever seen pulled; equity feels like pretend money.
- **Coping style:** she turns panic into systems; she doesn't say she's terrified, she says *archive is technically a filing system*.
- **The vow:** her silence is a vow, not a lie — the kitchen-drawer envelopes are spoken for, and she will not make her mother re-deal them.
- **Campus life:** the SDSU ASCE Student Steel Bridge team is the first place she became known outside her family. In her freshman spring they won their regional competition, then finished anonymously enough at nationals to produce a durable body of team excuses. The group chat calls them regional champions without irony and treats her sophomore return as settled fact.
- **Her part in the bridge:** she was the freshman keeper of the competition rules, caught a prohibited connection before fabrication made it expensive, and earned a place in the timed assembly. The older students knew more engineering and designed most of the bridge; she mattered because she read closely, spoke in time, and practiced until her hands knew the build. This year they expect her to own rules compliance and part of the connection design.
- **Superpower (planted early, fired late):** she reads boring documents — steel-bridge rules in Brookings, a vending ordinance in June, an annuity contract in August. The first two teach her, a little too successfully, that systems yield when she reads their definitions more carefully than everyone else.
- **Money wound:** needing help feels like failure.
- **Right about:** work has value, and asking can cost dignity. **Wrong about:** that work is the only lever, and that grinding substitutes for speaking.
- **After the crash:** she blames Max — externalizing as one more form of hiding — until she hears herself mid-accusation at Estelle's.
- **Arc:** from carrying the number alone to saying it out loud; from one money physics to three; from prosecuting other people's failures to owning her own thumb. As the gap closes around her she mutes the bridge-team chat and lets people who expect her mistake silence for ordinary summer absence. Her victory is not that school gets paid for; it's that she stops disappearing from a life already in motion.

### Estelle Carter, 78 — grandmother, hidden investor, collector

Black; family came up from Arkansas in the Great Migration for packing work; finished eighth grade, then the John Morrell plant at seventeen; widowed young in the mid-1970s, then erased — banks would not deal with her, legally until 1974, in practice longer. Taught herself everything from library books and the business pages, and never felt entitled to say any of it out loud: a woman who stopped school at eighth grade does not lecture; she could always *do* it, never *say* it.

**Her secret timeline:**

- **1976** — first fund account in her own name: a salesman's load fund, the slip filled out in handwriting practiced on scrap paper.
- **1982** — reads about IRAs in the paper and maxes hers, twenty years running.
- **1985** — $1,000 into Deere & Co. at the bottom of the farm crisis; one paper certificate, never sold.
- **1987** — the Morrell strike, then Black Monday: her one panic, sold near the bottom.
- **1989** — back in via a no-load index fund, and she never sells anything again.
- **2009** — opens a 529 for each grandchild: $400 every January into an account bearing a two-year-old's name.
- **2013** — retires.
- **2021** — shifts the college money to safety just ahead of the crash.

Total: **$900,000, built the slow way** — plus a **$5,000 transfer she makes herself every late summer** against Keisha's bill, unexplained, unclaimed, the "credit line" nobody audits.

- **The collector:** fifty years of thrift-store finds curated in the garage like a museum, every object carrying a provenance story that names what she *paid* and never what it's *worth* — entry price public, valuation secret, the whole character in a storytelling tic. The find stories are her displaced autobiography and, it turns out, the teaching all along. She has never once held a sale — until page one. She cannot let go of what she holds: not the finds, not the certificate, not the truth.
- **The refused name:** she never let anyone call her Grandma — grandmas were old women, and she was busy. She is Stell or Miss Estelle; "Essie" is reserved, said by her exactly once, inside her own story. Keisha's unadorned "thanks, Grandma" at the loan signing is the payoff.
- **Guardrails:** the collection is curated, never squalor — and it is **never the piggy bank**; the gap closes through the 529 and labor, full stop. Her vulnerability to Gary is not intelligence — it's fifty years of being unlistened-to.
- **Money wound:** once erased, she built a life around never needing anyone again. **Wrong about:** survival is not the same as living.
- **Arc:** from hiding as safety to teaching money out loud, twice in one week. Understood, not absolved; she does not apologize well, and the book does not make her.

### Denise Carter, ~52 — mother, the middle strand

Black; Estelle's daughter; grocery-store shift supervisor — schedules, coverage, moods, shrink. Runs the household on labeled cash envelopes — GROCERIES, LIGHTS, CAR, SCHOOL — and is flatly excellent at every dollar she can hold; she taught Keisha the cash-drawer liturgy (count it twice, band the twenties, the way she closes the store every night).

- **The gap:** what she lacks is not sense but systems literacy — bursars, portals, aid letters, anything with a login — the structural condition of a first-generation college parent, and never a punchline. Nobody taught her, because Estelle never taught anyone.
- **The revision that powers the book:** she got the parent-portal email in June and read the $7,800 herself. She waits for Keisha to tell her, making several openings; a first-year portal tangle and Keisha's *I'll handle school stuff* have taught her to read the silence as a boundary. When Keisha says everything is handled, Denise quietly begins her own rescue: the Friday supervisor shifts nobody wants.
- **The car:** the household car needs an approximately $800 repair. Denise has prepared — the CAR envelope covers most of it — but the balance consumes the margin she had assigned to tuition and forces the watch into her arithmetic. The emergency does not expose incompetence; it makes one well-planned dollar do two jobs.
- **The watch:** Estelle gave her the old men's watch when she made supervisor, saying only that it had belonged to her father. Denise wears it on important workdays, unaware that Estelle once sold it and secretly found it again years later. After the car repair breaks her plan, Denise takes it to a dealer once, intending to learn its value, and sells when the offer makes the tuition math possible.
- **Adaptation:** her lifelong habit is not-asking; performed certainty is her camouflage. She believes a competent mother anticipates need without making her child ask.
- **Private want:** she has protected Keisha's late-August move-in day on the store calendar. More than making a payment, she wants to be included in her daughter's return to Brookings, and cannot quite say so.
- **Arc:** from anticipating in silence to asking and staying in the answer. The small, concrete 529 question at the climax demonstrates that asking can be survived; her anger at Estelle demonstrates that speaking does not require instant forgiveness.
- **The runner:** she keeps meaning to visit the stand and never does — she's the supervisor covering everyone else's Friday — until one late-season stop: one cup, full price. Seeing the stand busy persuades her, wrongly, that Keisha may truly have the rest handled.

### Sofía "Sofi" Ortiz, 16 — the watched one

Mexican-American; the neighbor girl — the Ortiz family has lived three doors down since before she was born, family in everything but blood. Supplies the agua fresca recipe (page one: a pitcher carried over for the sweating sorting crew — the stand is born in one collective gulp).

- **The invoice:** she has translated, interpreted, and helped for free her whole life; the recipe is the first contribution anyone tried to put a number on. She recognizes unpaid value faster than the older girls, invoices the recipe, and the amendment gives her a real per-batch royalty plus a vote — and the recipe never transfers with the business; it is hers.
- **Non-thematic want:** her learner's permit, over-seriously studied and casually denied — comic texture, not subplot.
- **Function:** the fourth generation by proximity, not blood — the pattern travels through anyone who's paying attention. The final image is hers.

### Lourdes Ortiz, ~40s — Sofi's mother, counter-wisdom

Mexican-American; cash, caution, liquidity, family obligation.

- **Not backward, not folklore:** her distrust has receipts — one bank account frozen years ago over a mismatched middle name; three weeks, four trips, one fee, zero apologies; told once, never as a lesson.
- **The invoice scene:** argues love's terms over the invoice (in this family you do not bill the people you love), then, handed the guardian pen, reads every line before signing.
- **JazzFest:** she's the one who grabbed the cash box when the crowd surged — the false alarm with real consequences.

### Sydney Ellis, 21 — chaos engine

White; the Ellis kids grew up four blocks over, family friends since grade school. Retail job, bills, a credit-card balance, and the feeling that everyone else is living louder. She spends out loud because pleasure feels like proof she exists — and she notices everyone's mood first.

- **Value discovery:** finds the Levitt concert crowds — the stand's real value discovery, made by the person everyone calls irresponsible.
- **The warning:** her warning to Keisha lands *mid-winning-streak* — warning someone off a bet that's up triple digits is the brave version.
- **The priced choice:** the Premier Center ticket sold in front of everyone, the card paid down, mourned a little — Keisha's dress rehearsal for costs said out loud.
- **Arc:** from spending to prove value to choosing joy with eyes open. The book is not anti-spending; Sydney is why.

### Max Ellis, 24 — fast-money foil, light love interest

White; Sydney's brother; a warehouse job he hates, where the shift genuinely runs whether he's on it or not. Smart, scared, charming, seduced by speed; dreams of retiring by thirty-five; finance-bro register applied to cup sizes and flirting. Shows up at the garage sale flashing one green screenshot and keeps showing up unneeded.

- **The pressure valve:** he becomes Keisha's safe confession — he won't worry, won't sacrifice, won't tell her mother — which is exactly what makes her silence sustainable.
- **The mechanics:** teaches her to open her own brokerage account and mirror his trades — her money, her thumb, her loss; no handover, no debt.
- **The crash:** crashes on the page, crawls back to the warehouse, opens the boring account publicly — **and doesn't really learn:** by the coda he has "diversified" into a new conviction ticker, boring account holding $85.
- **The apology:** bad — vague, weather-ish; she forgives him anyway, having learned that waiting for the perfect apology is one more way of not moving.
- **Boundary:** he stays entirely outside the family plot, forever. Ends warm and undefined: a boy at correct valuation, a laugh across a split check.

### Emily Larsen, 18 — math brain

White; Norwegian-Lutheran, fourth-generation Sioux Falls; a home that is kind, solvent-seeming, and has never once discussed money out loud — concern is expressed in hotdish. Sydney's opposite number: one money culture performs, one never speaks; both are broke in ways nobody names.

- **The instrument:** terrifying precision, spreadsheets with absurd filenames; owns the book's teaching instrument, the triptych — Keisha's fryer per-hour, the stand per-hour, and Max's screenshot on one table, no lecture attached.
- **The cracked phone:** holds the banking app she refuses to open — until she quietly does, weeks before anyone notices; Keisha discovers the stray transfer notation late, evidence that the door opens quietly.
- **Aftermath:** institutes the two-person count after JazzFest.
- **Money wound:** exact truth feels more dangerous than guessing — she trusts numbers except the one that belongs to her.

### Gary — the advisor

*Sunrise Retirement Solutions.* Pleasant, patient, remembers Estelle's late husband's name, laughs at her jokes; found her through the senior-center channel and planted the "simplify your estate" idea behind the garage sale Denise thinks she won.

- **The contract:** surrender charges, a 7% commission dressed in jargon, a signing "incentive" expiring on a circled date — legitimate advice never comes with a countdown.
- **Screen time:** three scenes total, no raised voice; defeated by being *read*, and leaves politely, because his species always does.
- **The ladder:** third rung of the predator ladder — the 1976 fund salesman, the concessionaire, Gary: three men across three eras pricing women's work and money at scrap value.
- **Scale:** Tuesday-sized, always — real financial predation is boring and patient, and depicting it accurately is the lesson.

### The concessionaire

Food-truck owner, liked by the girls all summer; offers $600 for booth and "brand" — nine days after an anonymous permit complaint nobody can pin on him. Shrugs when asked: "that's business." Buys a cup. Stays a market fixture; hints at the good corner for next June. One shade dark, no darker.

## The spine — five acts

### Act 1 — The Garage

Denise has finally won the decade-long clutter argument: Estelle's first-ever garage sale is a week out, and the girls are conscripted to sort — Estelle narrating the provenance of every find (the $5 table, forty tellings) and un-donating boxes as fast as they're packed. Denise is wearing the old men's watch Estelle gave her when she made supervisor; Estelle's slightly excessive attention to it reads as one more case of object possessiveness.

- **The envelope.** Keisha finds the 1965 John Morrell pay envelope, stub and folded twenty still inside. Before anyone can price it, Estelle takes it back: "that's not for sale."
- **→ Essie: 1965.** That night, the memory: seventeen, the plant, count and divide, one twenty folded back in, her first hidden keep.
- **The stand is born.** Mid-prep, Sofi carries over a pitcher of her family's agua fresca for the sweating crew, and the stand is born in one collective gulp: the Falls Park market has no drink stall worth the name.
- **Max arrives.** He turns up at the sale itself — hauling nothing, needed by no one, flashing one green screenshot — and Keisha files her first annoyed noticing.
- **The bill.** The bursar bill lands: **$7,800, due late August**, and the vow forms — the envelopes in that kitchen drawer are already spoken for; she carries it alone. Before she can absorb the number, the bridge-team chat produces a photo of the dented regional trophy, another argument about nationals, and the assertion that Carter still has the annotated rules binder. Someone tells her to bring it back. She does not answer; nobody has asked a question.
- **→ Denise strand 1: the portal.** She reads the parent-portal email in June and gives Keisha time and several openings to tell her. A brief memory of last year's portal tangle — and Keisha's *I'll handle school stuff* — makes restraint feel like respect. Keisha says everything is handled. Denise takes the unwanted Friday shifts and marks move-in day unavailable on the store calendar.
- **The stake.** Estelle stakes the stand — $30 for 30% — and her thirty dollars seed the float.

**Act out:** the deal signed; three rescues running blind.

### Act 2 — The Stand

The stand is killed by a permit, revived by an ordinance, and finally discovered by its real market — while every rescue and every secret picks up speed.

- **Day-one defeat.** First Saturday: turned away at setup — nobody knew there was a vendor permit; processing takes three weeks; the ice melts in the parking lot and Estelle's thirty dollars die on day one. Total defeat.
- **The ordinance.** Keisha spends the week inside the municipal vending ordinance the way she once lived inside the steel-bridge rules — definitions, diagrams, exceptions, cross-references — finds the right classification and the temporary authorization, and gets them legal before the next weekend. It is her first experience outside school of an institution saying yes because she asked precisely, the plant for everything Act 4 asks of her, and one more reason she mistakes the market for a system that will reward the same kind of reading.
- **Near-miss #1.** Walking home from the win, Denise opens her mouth about the portal email, and Keisha's "everything's handled, Mom — even city hall" arrives one beat first.
- **The flop, and the liturgy.** The legal debut still flops — ten customers, church ladies counting her weeks to move-in. The cash-box handover, and Denise's drawer liturgy that evening — Emily hovering at the counter, her cracked phone face-down until it lights with a transfer alert she palms out of sight: the reader clocks that the banking app she swears she can't open has been open for weeks, a card Keisha won't turn over until the end.
- **Value discovery.** Sydney finds the Levitt concert crowds — young, hot, thirsty, unserved — and the stand takes off.
- **The buy-back.** Keisha catches Estelle at a neighbor's sale buying back a piece from her own at a markup: the woman cannot let go of what she holds. Keisha files it away without understanding; the reader keeps it whole, a card that comes due at the table.
- **The valve opens.** In Max's keep-showing-up pattern, Keisha tells him the number — not trust, safety: he won't worry, won't sacrifice, won't tell her mother.
- **The account.** The permit win is what flips her on his standing offer: the market looks like one more system with rules, and she just beat a system by learning its rules. Two phones at a teardown table, flirtation as curriculum: he walks her through opening her own account — her money, her thumb, mirroring his trades. He talks edge and conviction, but it's borrowed heat — a tip half-heard, certainty with nothing under it. Dazzled by a system she thinks she can beat, Keisha misses what the reader catches clean: this is luck in a confident voice, and it ends one way.

**Act out:** the account opens; the boom begins as a green glow under everything — a glow the reader already reads as a countdown.

### Act 3 — The Boom

Everything is up — the stand, the position, the stakes — and every gain quietly arms a trap.

- **The offer.** The concessionaire's friendly $600 offer for booth and "brand"; Emily notices the anonymous complaint was filed nine days before it arrived — never proven, shrugged off, "that's business."
- **The vote.** Paper-slip vote: 3–1 against selling, and nobody knows whose slip said *sell*. Keisha does.
- **The invoice.** Sofi invoices the recipe; Lourdes argues love's terms at the Carters' kitchen table in front of the neighbors-who-are-family, then reads every line before signing as guardian. The amendment gives Sofi her royalty and her vote.
- **→ Essie: 1976.** Triggered by three girls amending a contract: the fund slip, the patient salesman, the signature practiced on scrap paper.
- **→ Denise strand 2: the Friday shift.** Inside the grocery store she is fully legible: solves a coverage problem, catches a drawer discrepancy, refuses a request for move-in weekend but takes another Friday. The extra-shift money finally looks sufficient to matter. After closing, the household car will not start.
- **The car.** The necessary repair is about $800. Denise's CAR envelope covers most of it; she quietly takes the balance from the Friday money she had assigned to tuition. Keisha offers limited help, Denise says she has it covered because Keisha needs every dollar for school, and neither follows the opening. Watching a real emergency meet a labeled envelope only hardens Keisha's vow: this is why she cannot put another number in that drawer.
- **The liquidity trap.** The housing deposit springs it — her savings are in the market, climbing, the *plan*, untouchable — so fryer wages cover the deposit (the bedding bag enters her room, unopened), and a $60 borrow from the stand float covers the ice supplier. The first bad decision quietly causes the second, and the borrow breaks the one money thing her mother ever explicitly taught her. Meanwhile the bridge chat plans the fall kickoff, assigns Keisha the new rules binder and a piece of connection design, and litigates last year's team shirts. With the position rising, she answers easily and volunteers for more; nobody congratulates her for returning because nobody imagines she might not.
- **The mail.** On a paperwork errand Keisha carries Estelle's mail — one envelope bearing the same institution as the credit line on her own portal — and files it unread.
- **→ Essie: 2009.** The January ritual, $400 into an account bearing a two-year-old's name. *The reader now holds the answer she just handled.*
- **JazzFest.** The season's biggest night, record line, Denise's one visit and one full-price cup — a busy stand she mistakes for proof that Keisha may truly have the rest handled — and the position peaking hard enough that the extrapolation says weeks, not years. She's reaching for the sell button when the cash box vanishes in the crush. An hour of panic; suspicion slides toward the wrong vendor's kid; Keisha stops it the only way available — full audit, which means confessing the $60 first, in front of everyone, so the count is honest. Then Lourdes appears carrying the box: grabbed in the surge, nobody told the right person. The theft was never real; the confession is irreversible; the sell window is gone. She lets it ride. It never comes back.
- **The supper table.** The quiet centerpiece: three women at one supper table, hotdish and weather talk, the reader holding all three cards while Keisha checks a green number under the table — near-misses #2 and #3 live here.
- **→ Denise strand 3: the recalculation.** The car repair has eaten the margin and the remaining Fridays cannot rebuild it in time. At home, Denise takes off her father's watch, sets it beside the written arithmetic, and finally puts it in her purse. No appraisal yet, only the decision to ask what it is worth.

**Act out:** Emily's triptych sits on one table — fryer per-hour, stand per-hour, Max's screenshot — and the same evening Sydney corners her: the loudest spender in the book saying *don't*, plainly, about a bet that's up triple digits.

### Act 4 — The Bust

The crash lands, the valve closes, and the summer's real detective story begins in a garage full of paperwork.

- **The press.** Keisha presses anyway: her approved stand-share advance added to the position, eyes open, twice warned — hope, then greed, then desperation, each locally rational.
- **The red morning.** Down 80%, savings and advance gone together; her account, her thumb, her loss.
- **→ Essie: 1987.** Strike paycheck, Black Monday, her one panic sale near the bottom — and the boring certificate that survived because it was slow.
- **Max's public bottom.** He crawls back to the warehouse — the half-heard *yes sir, whenever there's a shift* — and asks Emily, publicly, how to open the boring account, because his performance was public.
- **The freeze.** Keisha ices him completely: unanswered chats, cold teardowns, the whole loss loaded onto a story in which her thumb never appears. The bridge team posts its first shop-night schedule, asks her to bring the binder, and resumes the argument about the shirts; she mutes them too. Nobody there knows a disappearance is underway. The blame closes her only pressure valve — and a hider with no valve is a hider whose silence is finally unbearable.
- **→ Denise strand 4: the sale.** On Tuesday she takes the watch to a reputable dealer, intending to learn its value. She asks good questions, gets an offer in writing, hesitates once, and sells it in the same visit. The receipt goes into her purse, the proceeds stay intact for the bill, and outside she checks her bare wrist for the time.
- **The detective work.** Out of private moves, Keisha goes to Estelle's to help with a paperwork box, and the detective work begins almost by accident: a dividend statement that doesn't belong in a poor woman's garage; a John Deere stock certificate old enough to be furniture; letterhead matching last year's unexplained portal credit — and, newer than everything, a glossy Sunrise Retirement Solutions folder, a business card, a date circled in someone else's handwriting, eleven days out.
- **Reading Gary.** She engineers one look at him and watches her grandmother be *animated* at her own table — listened to about money for the first time in fifty years; the hook was never intelligence. She borrows the contract overnight and reads it the way she read the vending ordinance: surrender charges, the commission, the countdown. In the Sunrise paperwork, "simplifying your estate" in Gary's hand — the garage sale that opened the book was his fingerprint; Estelle let Denise believe the nagging worked.
- **The shame visit.** Estelle arrives before Keisha can act — being almost-had by that species twice in one life cannot have a witness — and trades the wound for silence: eighth grade, the plant, the banks, the erasure, the slip, a lifetime of building it back the slow way, the 529 in Keisha's name since age two — *she is the credit line; she has been paying all along.* The find stories were the teaching all along, and neither of them knew it. But the sum itself she will not say — how much there is is the one number she has never spoken aloud to a living soul, and she does not speak it now: *that's mine to say, and I've never once said it.* She calls herself Essie exactly once.
- **The mirror.** Mid-accusation — *you built an entire system of secret help so you would never have to have one honest conversation* — Keisha hears herself three ways: the hidden number, the prosecuted boy, the same wound at three scales. She gives her whole summer back — the vow, the float, the account, the crash.

**Act out:** Estelle asks the one price Keisha won't pay — keep all of it from Denise, and don't make her say the number out loud.

### Act 5 — The Table

The race is against shame, with a circled date.

- **The negotiation.** Keisha refuses secrecy and refuses to go behind Estelle's back, which leaves one path — Estelle at the table by Sunday on her own feet — and she wins with Estelle's own arithmetic: *a fortune nobody knows about is a fortune nobody can defend; hiding stopped being safe the day Gary found you.*
- **→ Essie: 2021.** The shift to safety, one phone call, one confirmation slip: she has been watching all along.
- **Two rehearsals.** In the same stretch, two rehearsals for costs said out loud: Sydney sells the Premier Center ticket in front of everyone, card paid down, mourned a little — the dress rehearsal Keisha didn't ask for — and a stray transfer notation reveals Emily opened her account weeks ago and never said: the door opens quietly.

**The kitchen-table chain reaction** — one scene, three collapses:

1. **Keisha goes first** — the vow, the float, the account, the crash, priced in full — and Estelle's needle lands mid-confession: *you told the Ellis boy before your own mother.*
2. **Which forces Estelle** to do the one thing she refused at the shame visit — **say the number out loud**: $900,000, built the slow way, and the 529 in Keisha's name since age two, the guarantee named for the first time in fifty years of kitchen, to the whole room at once, Gary's folder on the table as exhibit A. The reader has known she was paying since Essie 2009; the *scale* lands here, on the room and the reader together — the one card the book withholds on purpose, so the climax has something left to detonate.
3. **Which breaks Denise**, who leaves the room and returns with her phone — the parent portal, logged in since June — the dealer's receipt, and the untouched sale proceeds: *I've known all summer. I sold Daddy's watch Tuesday.*

And there, the blow the table was built for: Estelle, who can price every object she has ever touched, understands which watch Denise means and recognizes the dealer on the receipt — her late husband's watch, the one thing the erasure made her sell and the one thing she bought back in secret years later, handed to Denise as *your father's watch* without ever saying she'd reclaimed it. Denise, rescuing blind, sold the one object in the family that had already been lost once. Three women paid separately for the same fear, and only one cost cannot be recovered.

Then Denise does the thing her whole adaptation was built to avoid: asks one small, concrete question about the 529 line, out loud, and waits to be answered. The asking is not absolution. She names the injury Estelle's silence passed down — *you let me learn everything the hard way because that was how you learned it* — and Estelle has to hear it without a clean apology or immediate repair.

- **Gary's appointment** is a sentence, not a showdown: three women and a contract with margin notes; Estelle reads him two of his own surrender clauses and asks one question about his commission; Keisha and Denise say nothing at all, which is the point — he built the pitch for a woman alone. He leaves politely.
- **The transfer** posts days before the due date, the way it was always going to: $5,000 down, and the $2,800 that was truly hers closed by exactly the summer she had — shifts, stand share, the liquidated remainder, the job that said yes.

**Coda** — the costs get papered, which is the point of them:

- The laptop loan at real terms, token interest, signed — Estelle teaching money out loud for the second time in a week, easier already; "thanks, Grandma," unadorned.
- The lost advance repaid to the stand from circulation-desk shifts.
- The watch hunted a second time by the family's great finder of objects — the one buy-back she cannot make — and already moved on by the dealer; some prices stay paid. The sale proceeds remain on the new family ledger instead of vanishing with the object.
- Max's bad apology, forgiven anyway; the split check; the laugh at the new "diversified" ticker; warm and undefined.
- Sofi's custodial account opens, the learner's-permit errand waiting; the concessionaire hints at the good corner for June.
- Then the move-in day Denise protected: the bedding finally out of its bag, the annotated bridge rules and battered work gloves riding with it, Keisha at the wheel of the repaired car and Denise beside her, I-29 north and overpasses rated, next year's gap visible on a family ledger instead of in one girl's head. The bridge chat asks who has the binder. *I have it*, Keisha answers — no announcement, no relief, because they assumed she did — and Sofi stands at the fence, driver's manual against her side, watching Keisha the way Keisha once watched Estelle. ## Design rules (carry into the chapter plan)

1. **Every bang buys a release** — no crisis beat may stand without relief within a beat or two, but the relief need not be quiet: a lull, a laugh, or a card cashing all count. The book breathes through comedy and resolution, not only stillness. The supper chapter is the model (highest suspense in the book, released through hotdish and weather); the crash must not stand alone.
2. **Villains stay Tuesday-sized** — Gary never raises his voice; the concessionaire stays liked and unproven; real predation is boring and patient, and depicting it accurately is the lesson.
3. **The comedy quota is non-negotiable** — group chat, church ladies, find-story provenance, the returned cash box. Funny is the delivery mechanism: the reader finishes because she was laughing and discovers she can explain surrender charges at brunch.
4. **Entertain first, then educate** — no drama-only scenes. The lesson ledger: say the number out loud; three physics of money (wages, ownership, compounding) plus speed, the counterfeit fourth; read before you sign; never risk money you can't lose; paper gains aren't money; liquidity — money in the market isn't money on Tuesday; price your work; the float is sacred; small confessions are survivable; asking is a financial skill; isolation is what predators target — disclosure is armor; slow and boring survives; consequences don't automatically produce wisdom (Max); help with terms is care, not rescue.
5. **The collection is never the piggy bank**, and Max never touches the family plot.
6. **Keisha's mistakes stay locally rational** — hope, then greed, then desperation; the book is anti-hiding, never anti-mistake.
7. **The bridge metaphor stays structural, never spoken** — no building bridges, carrying loads, or strong foundations in dialogue or narration. Keisha's engineering remains technical, funny, material, and allowed to be wrong outside its domain.
8. **Campus life is a pressure line, not a subplot** — three summer touches and one coda payoff; no named teammate arc, competition flashback chapter, or fall-contest plot. The team assumes continuity. Keisha is the only person entertaining her disappearance.
