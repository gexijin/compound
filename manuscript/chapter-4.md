# FULL PRICE — Ch 4 · The Permit

## 1

The company's first Saturday begins at 5:15 a.m., in the half-dark, with a load plan.

Emily has drawn the hatchback from above in her notebook, every item outlined and labeled like an evidence photo: coolers here, table there, records wedged upright so they can't slide, the lamp riding in the footwell because nobody will say out loud that the lamp is coming. Sofi brewed the jamaica last night — two coolers' worth, dark red, limes floating like it's their job. The ice boards last, at 6:10, because ice is the only cargo with a deadline. Thirty pounds of it, bagged, paid for, working.

We are at Falls Park by 6:40. The market is already half-built: the kettle-corn guy threading his kettle through a tent leg, the bratwurst guy unstacking chairs, everybody moving like they've done this a hundred times, because they have. It smells like river and propane. I have a cash box, a float, a company, and a spot picked out on the walk down to the water where a hot person with no drink would have to walk past us twice.

We get as far as unfolding one table leg.

The market manager has a lanyard, a clipboard, and the walk of a woman who has ended better mornings than ours. "Booth assignment?"

"We're new," Sydney says, and gives her the smile that sold ten lamps.

"Vendor permit number?"

There is a silence with four people in it.

"We're pre-permit," Sydney tries. "It's a startup thing."

The manager flips one page on the clipboard, not because she needs to, but the way a referee touches the rulebook. "City vendor permit, then market vendor agreement. Permits go through the licensing board. Processing is about three weeks."

"Three *weeks*," I say. "The summer is ten of those."

"I know exactly how many weeks a summer is," she says, not unkindly, and moves her pen to the next line. "You can't sell on market grounds without one. That includes the parking lot, before you ask. Everybody asks."

Emily turns the notebook around and holds it up — the load plan, the labels, the ruler-straight lines. The manager looks at it a full second longer than she has to.

"Good drawing," she says, and walks off to end somebody else's morning.

So we stand in the parking lot next to thirty pounds of ice and watch the market open without us. Customers arrive. They look thirsty to me, but I've stopped being neutral on that subject. They walk right past the hatchback and buy kettle corn, wrong answer, from a man with a permit.

By nine the bags of ice have gone soft at the corners. By ten they are water with a rumor of ice in it, and the jamaica is warm, and we are drinking the inventory ourselves because the alternative is pouring it out. The kettle-corn guy comes over on his break and accepts a cup.

"Promotional," Sofi says, grimly.

He drinks it, says it's the best thing at the market, which it is, and the market knows it, and none of that is worth anything, because value that can't legally cross a property line isn't value. He tells us the ordinance is on the city website. He tells us everybody gets turned away their first year. "It's kind of a tradition," he says, warm, like that helps.

We load the hatchback in reverse order, per the diagram. On the drive home nobody talks except Sofi, who says, once, to the window: "It was the best thing at the market."

In the driveway Emily opens the laptop and enters the day into *agua_capital_do_not_lose*. Revenue: zero. Ice: $30. Under notes she types *permit* and closes the laptop gently, like a casket.

Thirty dollars of our investor's money is in a parking lot at Falls Park, being a puddle.

## 2

The restaurant does not care that I own a company now. The fryer needs me at eleven, the fryer gets me at eleven.

Between lunch and the afternoon lull I do what I have done all June: I look for the second job. The two listings I starred at the garage sale both wrote back. The clothing store wants weekend availability — Saturdays, specifically, all of them, the exact Saturdays the market owns. The warehouse pays $19.40 an hour to stack pallets overnight, which sounds like the answer until you read the shift: ends at six, and market setup starts at six, and I have not yet found the ordinance that lets me be two places at once.

I ask my own scheduler for more hours instead. She doesn't even open the calendar.

"Part-timers stay under thirty. It's policy. Nothing personal."

It is the politest wall I have ever hit. She schedules me twenty-nine and a half like she's doing me a favor, and by the rules on her screen, she is.

At the card table that night I run the math again, both directions, in case it works better backwards. It doesn't. Twenty-nine and a half hours at $14.50, every week, saving everything, lands where it landed at the garage sale. More hours don't exist, and the hours that exist pay what they pay. The lever I know goes to the floor and stops there. The gap wants a second lever, and the fryer only has the one.

I file that where I file things, and I read the ordinance instead.

The City of Sioux Falls Code of Ordinances, the chapter on peddlers, solicitors, and transient merchants, is fourteen pages, and I can tell nobody reads it, because it reads like a document nobody has ever been in a fight with. I read rules for a living — on the bridge team I'm the one with the binder, the one who finds out what *shall* means in a sentence where everyone else read *should*. Rules are where systems keep their honesty. Somewhere in the fourteen pages, the city has written down what it actually requires, as opposed to what the clipboard remembers.

It takes me three shifts' worth of breaks, sitting on an upturned crate by the walk-in, smelling like fry oil.

The vendor permit is real: application, licensing board, the board meets monthly, three weeks is optimistic. That's the front door, and the line at the front door is three weeks long. But farther down, past the section on ice cream trucks, there's a paragraph about temporary vending authorizations: a stand operating under the sponsorship of an already-licensed market or event, selling fewer than a stated number of days, can be authorized over the counter — the market countersigns, the city stamps, ten dollars. Same building. No board. The paragraph has been sitting there the whole time, holding the side door open, waiting for somebody to read that far.

I read it four times. Then I print it, because I have learned from my grandmother that paper wins arguments that phones lose.

Tuesday we're at the garage restocking, and my grandmother comes out to exercise her rights. She counts the box. It doesn't take long.

"Sixteen dollars," she says, in the voice of a woman reading a diagnosis.

Emily produces the ledger and itemizes: supplies, float, and the ice, $30, deceased. My grandmother listens to the whole accounting with her hands behind her back. Then she crosses to the inventory, picks up the lamp, clicks it on, clicks it off.

"This is a $1 lamp now," she says.

"It was $2 two weeks ago."

"That was before it was owned by a company losing money." She sets it down, and that's the whole visit — no rescue, no lecture, thirty percent of nothing noted for the record. At 5:40 she comes out of the house dressed, the earrings that click, and drives off to her Tuesday, and nobody asks.

The licensing office is a counter, a clerk, and a smell like toner. I put the printout down facing her, the paragraph flagged with one of Emily's ruler-straight sticky tabs. The clerk reads it once. Reads it again.

"Huh," she says.

She gets a second clerk. The second clerk reads it and says "huh" too, and then they find the form — it exists, it just lives in a drawer that doesn't get opened much — and I fill it out on the counter, and the market manager's countersignature takes one phone call, because the manager, it turns out, remembers the good drawing. Ten dollars. The treasury pays. The stamp comes down like a gavel.

Outside on the steps I hold the authorization in both hands, and I let myself think the thought all the way through, because it's earned: everything that has ever told me no this summer has been a document. The permit was a document. The thirty-hour ceiling is a document. The bill is a document — $7,800 of plain type with a date on it. And documents can be out-read. You just have to be the one person willing to sit on a crate by the walk-in and read to the end.

I get us a plastic sleeve for the authorization. Lamination is for companies with revenue.

## 3

Market Saturdays start below the falls now.

There's a heron that fishes the tailwater at dawn — a great blue, tall as a mailbox, patient as a bank. I have been trying to photograph this bird for three summers. The problem is the light, and the spray, and the distance, and my phone, which is four years old and photographs everything like a rumor. The other problem is standing forty feet to my left in a fishing vest, retired, with $3,000 of lens on a tripod.

"Got him Thursday," he says, and turns the camera around to show me the back of it. The heron in his screen has feather detail. It has an eye ring. It has a fish.

"Nice," I say, meaning several other words.

"You'll get there," he says, to my phone, and the phone and I absorb it together. Three summers, zero photographs. As an investment the heron is underperforming, and I keep the position anyway. Some positions you hold on principle.

At 6:55 I walk up to the market a legal woman. The manager checks the sleeve, initials her line, and says "huh" — the third city employee this week to meet the paragraph — and waves us in. We set up on the walk down to the water, between the kettle corn and a woman who sells honey. The table, the coolers, the records at a slant Sydney calls *browsable*, the lamp presiding. Sofi tapes the price to the cooler lid in her sales-clerk hand: $2. The first pitcher gleams. We are open.

The market, it turns out, does not know we're open.

Morning market people are coffee people. They walk down to the falls holding travel mugs, and they walk back up holding kettle corn, and between those two facts our cash box develops a long quiet middle. The church ladies come through in rotation and handle everything on the table like customs inspectors. One of them holds a 50-cent margarine tub of clothespins to the light, puts it down, and looks at me with real warmth.

"How many weeks till you leave for school, sweetheart?"

"Eight," I say.

"Eight," she repeats, nodding, like she's confirming my appointment, and buys nothing.

The bratwurst guy has two cups because it's ninety degrees and he stands over a grill for a living. A man in a bucket hat flips every record twice and buys one for $3 — Sons of the Pioneers, near mint, a fact only I appear to care about. A jogger takes a cup, drinks it at the table, and says the thing the kettle-corn guy said, best thing at the market, and I watch her say it and jog away without telling a single other person.

Emily keeps the tally. At close it reads: customers, ten. Cups, eleven — the bratwurst guy counts once as a person and twice as a sale. One record. One tub of clothespins, to the other church lady, who did not ask me about school.

"Front of house," Sydney announces, sandal on the cooler, "we did $26, which rounds to $30."

Emily turns the notebook around. $25.50.

"Which rounds," Sydney says, holding eye contact, "to $30."

We tear down. The heron guy walks by with his tripod over his shoulder and shows the honey woman the back of his camera, and her whole face does something delighted, and my jaw files a complaint I don't authorize.

That evening my mother closes us out at the kitchen table, because that's the law now: money came in, so money gets counted. Nobody voted. She doesn't ask why the take is small; the ceremony doesn't check the amount first. She counts the stack and slides it over; I count it back out loud. It took eleven minutes at the garage sale. Tonight it takes two, because $25.50 is mostly ones, plus a single twenty from the record man, who paid big and waited, unbothered, for $17 in change.

"Band the twenties," my mother says.

"It's one twenty."

"Then it'll be a fast band."

I band the twenty. One bill, the band lapping itself four times, the most protected twenty dollars in Minnehaha County. Emily's phone lights up on the counter next to the pencil box — a green banner, a little bank logo — and is face-down before it finishes buzzing.

"It's nothing," Emily says, to the pencil box.

Nobody asks a second question. My mother writes the number on the flap of the grocery envelope in her store printing, under a Saturday that went differently, and sets the pen down like a register drawer closing.

I check my app while she does it. Same number as this morning. Eight Saturdays left.

$25.50, banded.
