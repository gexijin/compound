# FULL PRICE — Ch 2 · The Chase (draft v1)

## 1

The woman in the sun visor is my favorite kind of customer. She buys three things out of the $2 box — a string of red beads, a brooch shaped like a pineapple, and the gold rope chain — and pays with exact change. Six dollars, no haggling, no stories. "Thank you," I say. "Have a good one."

She has a good one for about ninety seconds.

My grandmother surfaces from the garage with a box of Ball jars and stops halfway to the tables, watching the visor woman load her trunk at the curb. "What'd she find?" she asks me, friendly, over the box.

"I emptied half the jewelry box on her. Beads, two brooches, the rope chain."

"Which chain."

"The gold rope. The heavy one." I'm a little proud of the volume. "Two dollars."

My grandmother sets the Ball jars down in the grass, which is not a place she has ever set glass in her life, and goes.

Not toward me. Toward the car.

The car pulls from the curb doing the gentle five miles an hour of a person still checking her mirrors, and my grandmother goes after it down the middle of the street — 78 years old, June heat, apron strings straight out behind her. She doesn't yell. Yelling costs breath. The car doesn't see her. The car has no idea it is being chased.

I'm moving before I've decided anything. "Emily — drawer," and the cash box is out of my hands and into hers, twenties under the clip. On the end of the third table there's a bike, a teal ten-speed, $15 on a string tag. I haven't ridden since seventh grade. Turns out it's true, what they say.

I pass my own grandmother on my own merchandise, tag snapping at the handlebars, and catch the car at the corner stop sign, where I pull ahead, put a foot down, and hold up one hand like a crossing guard. Brake lights. The window comes down on a girl straddling a $15 bike, breathing hard.

"Ma'am. I'm so sorry. We sold you the chain by mistake."

My grandmother arrives thirty seconds later, walking now, walking like she'd been walking the whole time. She doesn't explain either. She takes two dollars out of her apron pocket — I catch the corner of the manila envelope riding along in there — and holds the bills through the window flat, like a toll.

The woman looks at the money, then at the two of us. "It was in the two-dollar box," she says.

Nobody argues. It's the truest sentence anyone says all day. She fishes the chain out of her purse, takes her two dollars, keeps the pineapple, and drives off at the speed limit.

My grandmother carries the chain back in her fist. I walk the bike. Neither of us says a word the whole block, which in this family counts as a debrief.

At the yard, Sydney takes one look at the fist and says, "Okay, wait," and goes into the house without asking, and comes back with the kitchen scale. My grandmother opens her hand. The chain pours off her palm onto the little steel tray like something melted, and the scale settles.

"Half an ounce," Sydney reads.

Emily already has her phone out. "Gold's at $4,300 an ounce." She holds the screen up and lets us do the rest ourselves. Half of 4,300.

"Twenty-one fifty," I say.

"Kitchen ounces aren't troy ounces," Emily says, thumbs still going. "Call it $2,000."

The table does the thing it did for the twenty — leans in without anybody moving.

"Miss Estelle." Sydney, gently, like she's defusing it. "What'd you pay for it?"

My grandmother straightens the crock pots. There are 4 crock pots. They were straight.

"Miss Estelle. What did you *pay*."

"$150," she says. "Nineteen eighty-four."

That's it. That's the whole file. I have heard this woman go four minutes on a butter dish. The chain gets six words and a year.

Emily does what Emily does, which is set two numbers next to each other and step back. "The envelope twenty sat 61 years and it's still a twenty," she says. "This sat 42 and turned $150 into $2,000."

"Mm," says my grandmother, who taught us all how much that syllable can carry.

For the record: my sticker, my thumb. Gold, on my grandmother's budget, is gold-tone. That was the whole theory. The theory is filed, and I don't price jewelry again today.

Then, instead of carrying the chain into the garage, into the museum, back into whatever drawer held it for 42 years, my grandmother crosses the lawn to my mother, who is re-taping the PRICED TO SELL sign that keeps letting go of its own promise, and holds it out.

"Here," she says. "Somewhere I can see it."

My mother's hands stop on the tape. "Mama—"

"Should've given you this when you made supervisor," my grandmother says, to the sign, and works the clasp herself behind my mother's neck, with those knuckles, on the first try.

Everybody marvels. Real gold, out of that garage. Nobody asks the question you'd think somebody would ask, and Emily — phone still in her hand, math still on it — says the thing that becomes the joke of the day:

"The woman who gives socks for Christmas owns actual gold."

Even my mother laughs. My grandmother allows it the way she allows weather. The socks are famous — store-brand crew, every grandchild, every Christmas, since before I could read — and the joke settles the whole business the way jokes do. Gold in the garage becomes a funny thing that happened today, like the bike, like the chase: a story with no question mark left in it.

Around noon my mother and I band the morning. It's a two-person job we have never once discussed. She counts a stack and slides it over; I count it back out loud and band it; twenties under the clip, ones fanned for speed. We don't talk during. We have never talked during. It is the one thing we do in perfect time, and it takes eleven minutes, and the morning comes to $487.

"$487," she says, and writes it on the flap of a grocery envelope. Above the envelope, at her collarbone, the chain catches the sun. $2,000, worn, unwritten.

---

The early-afternoon wave brings the serious hagglers, the ones who came after lunch on purpose. Mid-rush, my phone goes off six times in a row, which is a group chat, not an emergency.

It's the bridge team. Somebody posted the fall competition schedule. Somebody else is penciling names next to roles, and mine is already next to *rules compliance*, no question mark, and someone has typed *keisha has the binder anyway*, which is true. It's on my desk. The whole chat is people assuming I'll be there, the way you assume the ground.

Between customers I check the app. $1,503.72. Same as this morning, same as 9:40.

Here's the rest of the number's file. The fall bill is $7,800, due the last week of August. The portal has a sentence about it in plain type: unpaid balances are dropped from registration on a stated date, and the housing assignment is released. *Released* is a gentle word for it. Against that: $1,503.72, which is what a school year of careful looks like. The fryer pays $14.50 an hour and the ceiling is 30 hours, in writing. Ten more weeks of that, saving every dollar that doesn't buy gas, lands the whole summer somewhere around $5,000. $5,000 is not $7,800. It is not close enough to round.

Last August my grandmother paid $5,000 toward my first year, once, the way she does things: an amount and a silence. Nobody said there would be more, so there isn't. You don't ask her a second question, and you especially don't ask one with a dollar sign in it.

My mother is ten feet away, re-taping the sign again. Eleven minutes ago we counted $487 without a word and got it right to the dollar. The phone is in my hand. It would take one tap.

The envelopes in our kitchen drawer are labeled in her printing — GROCERIES, LIGHTS, CAR, SCHOOL — and they are already doing everything they can. And it's June. I have a whole summer. That's the part that makes the quiet feel affordable: ten weeks is a long time to be a person with plans.

I lock the screen. I don't answer the chat either.

"That's $4.50," I tell the waiting customer, and count her change back out loud.

## Denise, Week 1

The number had been in her phone since June.

It came the way school things came now, an email from the parent portal — the account she'd made at the kitchen table the winter Keisha applied, the two of them squinting at the aid letter. *Your fall statement is available.* Balance: $7,800, due late August. She read it twice on her break and once more in the car.

Mama's $5,000 last year had been a one-time thing. You could tell because nobody said otherwise.

She made one opening, by phone, that same week. "You set for school?"

"Handled," Keisha said.

"Good," Denise said, and that was the conversation.

Since then she'd been taking the Friday shifts nobody wanted. The first Friday's extra was already at the back of the kitchen drawer, in a new envelope she hadn't labeled, because labeling it would make it a conversation. Ten more Fridays sat between now and the bill, and Fridays added up. That was the whole plan, and it was a good one. On the store calendar, months out, she had blocked off the last Saturday of August in her own printing — OFF — because whatever else that week cost, she would be in the car when her daughter went back to Brookings.

Across the lawn her girl called out a total. Denise pressed the tape flat on the sign. Her hand went up once, on its own, to the new weight at her collarbone — warm from the sun, heavier than it looked.

She squared her tables. The email could sit where it sat. $7,800, and eleven Fridays.
