# FULL PRICE — Ch 11 · The Level

## 1

Monday morning the stock opens at $32.80, which is a dollar and twenty cents below my number.

I don't sell. Selling below my number feels like agreeing to lose money I already had in my hand Friday night, and I'm not doing that. It'll come back to the number. It went up for five straight weeks; it knows the way.

That's Monday. Tuesday it's $31.55. Wednesday it's $30.90. By Friday it's been sitting around $30 all week like a stubborn negotiator, and I have checked it from the fryer, from the car, from the market, from bed. I know the chart's shape better than I know my own class schedule. Every day it doesn't come back, my number gets one day more expensive to give up on.

Here's the honest ledger of the summer so far, and I keep it the way Emily would want me to. In June I put money in because it might work. In July I kept it in because it was working. There are ordinary words for both of those. I looked at them and felt fine.

Saturday at close, I ask the company for an advance.

I do it the proper way, just like we had outlined. Out loud, at the card table, both bookkeepers present: three hundred dollars against my share of August. Emily writes it in the book, reads it back, and counts it out with Sofi watching, and nobody asks what it's for. In this family and every family we've trained, nobody asks a second question. For once I am grateful for the disease.

Sydney watches the whole thing and says nothing. She said her piece in the car; she's not the type to repeat warnings unecessarily.

Max is the one who says something. He's loading coolers into the Vibe, and he waits until it's just us in the emptying lot, and when he says it he doesn't draw any charts in the air. He says it in a voice I've never heard him use, flat and quiet, like a man reading his own timecard.

"You're putting the advance in."

"The dip is a gift. Your guy's been saying it all week."

"My guy posts wins." He shuts the hatch and stands there. "Keisha. Desperation money never wins. That's not a chart thing. That's the realest thing I know."

"It's not desperation, it's an average-down. The company didn't change, the price changed."

He looks at me for a second, and whatever he was going to say next, he doesn't have the nerve to say it and we both know why, and it costs him something to stop. "Okay," he says, and gets in the car.

That's the third warning in eight days. One in writing, one in park, one in a parking lot with no chart hands. I can repeat all three word for word.

Monday morning, before my shift, I move the $300 in and buy ten more shares at $29.75. It fills in under a second. 136 shares. If it just climbs back to my number — not a miracle, just back — the whole bill is in reach again.

Money that has to win. There's a word for that. Max already said it, and I already decided it didn't apply to me.

## 2

Thursday the company puts out a press release at 7:40 in the morning.

I read it standing at the kitchen counter with a toaster waffle in my hand. It's four sentences long. Their largest customer — one of the two that make up most of their sales — is moving the work in-house. The company is "revising guidance," which is a phrase I now get to learn in public with everyone else.

Here's the part I want written down: I knew about those two customers. Page 61. One quiet sentence, six pages into the risk section, where only careful readers go. I found it, I underlined it in my head, and I filed it away for future use. Reading was my advantage. Reading told me exactly what could kill the company, and I put my savings on it anyway, and then I put the advance on it, because knowing a risk and believing a risk turn out to be two different jobs, and I only did the first one.

The market opens at 8:30 our time. I'm at the restaurant by 11, so I get to watch the open from my own kitchen: $6.10. By the time I clock in it's $5.92. On my first break I stand at the back counter, in the exact spot where I read all 187 pages, phone against the napkin dispenser, and watch it settle at $5.86.

Down 80 percent, in one morning, on one press release, about one customer, that one sentence on one page warned me about.

The account says $807.62.

Eighteen hundred dollars of real money went in — every dollar of savings I had in June, plus three hundred borrowed against work I haven't done yet. What's left wouldn't cover the housing deposit five times over. The signals group is a wall of men typing in capital letters, and the admin, who has posted every green day for two years, posts nothing at all until noon, when he posts a brand-new ticker and the words NEXT PLAY, and four hundred people say thank you.

I don't sell. I can't make my thumb do it, and anyway there's nothing left in there worth protecting. I put the phone in my apron and drop fries for five hours, and I want to say my mind was racing the whole time, but the truth is it was very quiet. 

Nobody did this to me. I don't want to admit it to myself, because I spend the rest of the day trying to find a version where somebody else did it, but the math checks out. It was my account. My name on the nineteen questions. My thumb on the buy button, all three times. Max never touched a dollar of it. My money. My thumb. My loss.

At supper my mother asks if I'm okay, and I say, "Fine. Long week." She looks at me one beat longer than usual.

Nobody asks a second question. For the first time all summer, I'm sorry about that.

## 3

Max got wiped out Thursday too. I know because everybody knows; his portfolio was always a public event, so the funeral is public too.

I learn the details at Saturday market, secondhand, standing close enough to hear: he had quit the warehouse two weeks ago. Told his shift the boring money could run itself without him from now on. Monday he asked for the job back, and the warehouse took him, because the warehouse always takes you back; that's what boring money does. He's on the 6 a.m. shift again, steel toes by the basement door.

He comes by at close. Not for me — he doesn't even look my direction, which tells me Sydney has briefed him on everything — but to stand in front of Emily's card table, in front of Emily, in front of everyone, and say:

"Walk me through the boring account. The index one. Like I'm Diego."

Emily doesn't gloat. Emily doesn't do gloating; she considers it a waste of breath. She takes out a fresh sheet and answers him one sentence at a time, and he asks real questions in a plain voice, no cup sizes, no moon, and writes the steps on the back of a receipt. It's the most serious I've ever seen him about anything, and it takes eleven minutes, and everyone at the table lets it happen without one joke.

At the end he holds up his phone to Emily. "Also, look at this one." It's a message — there've been a bunch, apparently, since Thursday. An account with a flag in its name and a photo of a man in a suit says it can recover his full losses, guaranteed, for a $500 processing fee up front.

"I know it's a scam," Max says. "I've been reporting these guys for years. Turns out when you lose money in public, you're not a person anymore, you're a list somebody sells." He says it like a joke. Nobody laughs, including him.

Here's what I do about all of it: nothing.

He texted me Thursday. *you ok.* Then Thursday night: *the level broke. nobody saw the cancel coming.* Then Friday: *keisha.* I read all three off the lock screen and answered none of them, because *nobody saw it coming* is a complete lie when I have a list of people who saw something coming — Emily in writing, Sydney in park, and a man with a proverb he recited to me and then watched me ignore.

There's a different version of events I find myself building over the weekend: a version of the summer where Max talked me into everything, where his group chat dictated my every move. It isn't true. I checked every box myself and read more pages than he's read in his life. But in his version of the summer — I mean mine, the one I'm building — I don't have to be in the room when the money died, and that version is easier to face.

So I stop answering, and there's nothing to break up, which is the convenient part. It never had a name, and when it ends, it's like it never existed in the first place.

Sunday night the bridge chat pings twice: the design call is set for the week before move-in, *bring the rules binder, K*, my name already penciled where my name goes. I look at the binder on my desk, tabbed and ready since May, property of a girl who thought reading was a weapon.

I mute the chat. If I'm not on that call, somebody else takes rules compliance, and there's a version of the fall where quiet costs me the seat I've held for two years. I could compute that cost to the dollar. I'm not computing anything this week.

Which leaves the count. Max knew the number and Max is gone, by my own doing. Sydney would have to hear what I did after her warning. Emily's book already holds my one correction, and I can't give it another. My grandmother would ask what's wrong in a way I can't respond to, and my mother is the reason for the entire situation. I carried this alone in June and pretended it was manageable.

It's not June anymore. I check the dark phone twice and open nothing.

$807.62, unbanded.
