# FULL PRICE — Ch 5 · The Account

## 1

The company's third Saturday starts below the falls at dawn, and the heron wins again.

He's standing in the shallow water below the third ledge, right where he always is, and for once the light is good. I raise my phone slowly. I tap the screen to focus. The camera takes a second to decide, and in that exact second the heron spreads his wings and flies off. What I end up with is a clear, well-lit photo of the water where a heron used to be. I have three summers' worth of photos like this.

The retiree with the $3,000 lens isn't even here today. The honey woman tells me he's at the dam this week, photographing pelicans. "He shows everyone," she says, tiredly.

The market is kinder to us this second week. The jogger from last Saturday comes back with two friends and tells them, right in front of our table, that this is the best thing at the market. Which is free advertising, and I'll take it. The bratwurst guy takes his two cups at 8:30 like it's his routine morning coffee now. A man buys three records and the 50-cent tub of clothespins. The church ladies rotate through, and one of them asks how many weeks until I leave for school. I say seven. She nods and moves on.

At close, Emily runs the tally: customers, nineteen. Cups, twenty-four.

"Front of house," Sydney announces, sandal up on the cooler, "we did $48 today, which rounds to $50."

Emily turns the notebook around. $47.75.

Max shows up for teardown because the coolers are heavy and he's free on Saturday afternoons. He does ten solid minutes of actual work, and then he can't help himself: the phone comes out. The screenshot is green, the line goes up, and the number at the top says **+$2,310**.

"The level held," he explains to anyone who will listen. "The price came down, tested the level, and bounced. That's confirmation." He draws the chart in the air with a flat hand, the way he always does.

Sydney takes Sofi to look at the honey. Emily heads to the library, because it's Saturday and that's where Emily goes on Saturdays. Then it's just me and Max, loading coolers into the trunk, and the parking lot slowly empties around us.

"Can I tell you a number?" I say.

"People tell me numbers all the time."

"This one has conditions. You can't fix it, you can't tell Sydney, and you can't do anything about it. I just want to say it to another person once."

He shuts the hatch and leans against it. "Go."

"$7,800. Tuition. Due the last week of August."

He's quiet for a second, thinking. He doesn't gasp. He doesn't ask why I haven't told my mother, which is the first question anyone else on earth would ask. He doesn't reach for his wallet like the number is an invitation.

"Okay," he says. "That's real money. But it's not impossible money. You've got a job, you've got a company—"

"I've got $1,503.72 and a waitressing job."

"—and you've got ten weeks," he says, like that solves everything.

Here is what I notice, standing in a parking lot with an empty cooler between us: the number sounds different out loud. I've been carrying it alone since June, and it has felt enormous the entire time. Said out loud to another person, it's just a number. People say numbers like this every day like it's nothing, and life goes on.

"Don't tell Sydney," I repeat.

"Tell her what?" Max says, grinning, and I know without asking again he'll keep my secret.

On the ride home the number is still mine. It weighs the same; it just isn't sitting as heavily on my chest anymore.

## 2

Tuesday we restock at the garage, and my grandmother comes out to count the box. This week the count takes longer, which is itself the good news.

"$63.75," she announces. Then, after a pause: "Better."

She walks over to the inventory, picks up the lamp, clicks it on, clicks it off.

"This is a $3 lamp," she says.

"It was $1 last week."

"Last week it belonged to a company that was losing money." She sets it down. At 5:40 she comes out of the house dressed, the earrings that click, and drives off without another word. Nobody says the word *gentleman* because Sydney isn't here to say it.

Max drops me home last because of the coolers. He parks the Vibe at our curb and leaves the engine running. The kitchen light is on — the window over the sink, where the drawer of envelopes lives. GROCERIES, LIGHTS, CAR, SCHOOL. I look at that light for a second. Then I take out my phone.

"Show me how you opened yours," I say. "The account."

Max does not need to be asked twice. He directs me to the app store like he's been waiting all summer for someone to ask.

The brokerage app has a cartoon rocket on its loading screen. Opening an account turns out to be nineteen questions, and I read every one before I answer it. Employment status: employed. Annual income: I type it in, and the app accepts it without comment. Investment experience: none. There's no box for *watched a neighbor's screenshot twice*. Then a risk quiz with three choices — conservative, moderate, aggressive — and no option that says *I have ten weeks*.

"I'm not, like, a fiduciary," Max says, somewhere around question fourteen. He says it like a joke, but the laugh comes a beat late, like part of him means it.

"Noted," I say, and keep typing. It's my phone. My name on the questions.

While the app verifies my identity, he shows me where his picks come from, and he presents this proudly, like he's revealing a trade secret. It's a group chat. Nine hundred members. A man whose username is in all caps posts screenshots of his wins, and the screenshots are green, all of them, as far back as I care to scroll.

"Where are the red ones?" I ask.

Max looks at me patiently. "Why would he post those?"

Some part of me knows that's a bad answer. It's the same part of me that found the paragraph about temporary vending permits. But I ignore it, because I'm not investing in a group chat. The group chat is just where Max gets his ideas. The actual thing he owns is a company — the rocket-adjacent one, the one that makes parts for the industry that's supposed to take off — and a company has to publish reports. Real documents, with numbers in them, that anyone can read. Reading is the one thing I know I'm better at than everyone else.

"I'm splitting mine," Max says. "Half the stock, half bitcoin. The bitcoin is insurance."

"What does bitcoin publish?"

"Publish?"

"Reports. Financial statements. Anything I could read."

"It's decentralized. That's the whole point."

"So there's nothing to read."

"There's nothing you *need* to read—"

"Then I have no advantage," I say. "Reading is my advantage. Send me the company's reports."

He sends me a link, shaking his head a little. The annual report downloads while we sit there: 187 pages. The city ordinance was fourteen pages, and buried in it was the paragraph that saved our whole company. This is the same kind of document, just bigger. Bigger should mean more buried treasure, not less.

"You're actually going to read it," Max says. Not a question. More like he's never seen it done before.

"Cover to cover."

"Unbelievable," he says, and it doesn't sound like criticism.

## 3

It takes me four shifts' worth of breaks, same as the ordinance. I lean on the back counter, smelling like a value meal, phone propped against the napkin dispenser, and read.

Most of it is a company explaining itself slowly. They machine valves and housings for engines, some of which end up on rockets. Two customers make up most of their sales, which the report admits on page 61, in one quiet sentence. The risk section runs six pages and includes the line "we have never been profitable and may never achieve profitability," which is the most honest thing any document has said to me all summer. That's the pattern I keep finding: the honest parts are always buried deep, where only careful readers get to them. Which is fine by me. That's the whole reason reading works.

Here's my math, and I run it twice, the way I was raised. The fryer has a ceiling, and the ceiling is in writing. The second job wants hours I don't have. The stand is growing, but it's growing in cups. This account is the second job I've been looking for since June — no schedule, no thirty-hour limit, nothing in the paperwork that says I can't. I checked. That was the first thing I checked. The only difference is that this job doesn't promise to pay you. It pays in maybe.

Friday after my shift, alone in the car in the McDonald's parking lot, I move $1,500 from savings into the account. The bank app asks if I'm sure. It's the first thing all summer that has asked. $3.72 stays behind in savings.

The buy screen wants a number of shares. The stock is at $11.84. I type 126, which comes to $1,491.84, and I sit there with my thumb over the button for a second. Max isn't in the car. Nobody is. My money. My thumb.

I press it.

By the market close on Friday, I'm up $45.36. I check it at a red light, and again in the driveway, and the number hasn't changed between the light and the driveway. I notice that I checked anyway.

Inside, my mother is at the kitchen table with the drawer open, doing the Friday thing: a folded bill into GROCERIES, a folded bill into LIGHTS, everything in its envelope. If I set my money on the table, she'd count it with me. That's the rule in this house: money came in, money gets counted. Count it twice, band the twenties.

But there's nothing to set down. My money isn't bills anymore. It's 126 small pieces of a valve company, riding around in my pocket, changing value while I sleep. You can't count it at a kitchen table. There's no band that goes around it.

"Goodnight, baby," my mother says, and presses the drawer shut.

"Night."

Upstairs, I check one more time. Same number as the driveway.

$1,545.36, unbanded.
