# FULL PRICE — Chapter 1 (draft v2)

## 1

Nine days ago I was in a fluid-mechanics final in Brookings. Today I'm running the cash box at my grandmother's first garage sale in 50 years of owning things, and between us, the cash box is the better exam. Count it back out loud. Twenties under the clip, ones fanned for speed. The drawer is right or the drawer is wrong — there is no drawer that's basically right. My mother taught me that on a milk crate at her store when I was 7, and it turns out you can get through a year of engineering school and still feel like a genius making correct change in the sun.

It's a good day. It's June, school is paid up behind me and waiting ahead of me, and I have a summer to fill with money. I have plans for this summer. The summer does not know this yet.

"A year of nagging finally worked," my mother tells Sister Ruth-Ann at 9, pleasant and carrying, the voice she uses at the store for *register 3 is open*. She tells Sister Deloris Hayes the same sentence at 10:15. Sister Deloris says, "Well, praise Him," in a tone you could return for store credit.

The driveway layout is Emily's, my best friend since ninth-grade homework at the branch library. There are signs at both ends of the block with arrows whose angles she calculated off traffic speed, and a price key lettered in mechanical pencil so straight it looks notarized. She's posted by the garage with the inventory sheet, making a small mark every time something sells — or, this being my family, every time something doesn't.

Because this sale is running two sales philosophies on the same tables, and neither one acknowledges the other, the way two weather systems don't.

Philosophy one is Sydney — grew up four blocks over, babysat me back when 13 watching 11 counted as babysitting, taught me to parallel park. She's working the front tables like it's a career she's inventing on the spot. She doesn't sell objects; she stages *vignettes*. By 9 a.m. she had grouped a macramé plant hanger, a fondue pot, and a ceramic owl on a card table and named it, to my face, "the reading nook collection."

"It's a fondue pot," I said.

"It's an *accent piece*," she said, and turned the owl a quarter inch, like adjusting a groom's tie.

Philosophy two is my grandmother, and my grandmother's philosophy is *no*.

Estelle Carter built the museum in that garage across 50 years of estate sales, and every object has a file: what it is, what fool let it go, what she paid — the entry price is public record; the value is classified. Now that the museum is out in the sun with strangers breathing on it, she has invented the sales pitch that faces backward. A woman in a Sturgis t-shirt picks up the cast-iron skillet, and my grandmother is there before the elbow finishes bending.

"Wagner. 1948. Off Alva Petersen's estate for $1, because her children flew in from Denver and priced the kitchen like a landfill. Sixty years of seasoning on that iron — you can't buy that finish, you can only inherit it or steal it." A beat. "$85."

The woman sets the skillet down with both hands, like it might go off, and says she's just looking. My grandmother says "Mm," which in her language is a paragraph, and pivots to defend the Pyrex.

So it goes, all morning, on parallel tracks: Sydney invents a lifestyle an object never had; my grandmother recites the life it actually had, plus a number with a moat around it. Twenty feet apart, same merchandise, and neither of them has looked at the other once. I'd bet the drawer neither of them will.

Between customers I check my banking app. Same number as 9:40. Good. Fine. Next customer.

The owl moves at 11, and I want it on the record that it was the best thing I've seen all summer. The browser is maybe 30, cargo shorts, the energy of a man furnishing an apartment out of spite after a breakup. Sydney reads him in one pass, the way she reads everybody — like a menu she's already eaten off of.

"So, owls are very in right now."

"Are they."

"Mid-century woodland. It's a whole thing. This guy anchors the reading nook collection, but honestly? He carries a bookshelf alone. He doesn't need the nook."

"How much is the nook?"

"The nook is $12. The owl à la carte is $5."

He pays $5 for the ceramic owl and carries it to his car in one arm like a rescue animal, visibly happier than when he came. Sydney turns to the driveway at large — to the *air* — and mouths *anchor piece*. Behind her, my grandmother talks a man out of a Folgers can of drill bits by telling him whose drill bits they were.

I rate the owl sale a 9 on execution and a 2 on repeatability, there being one lovesick man in cargo shorts per garage sale, and Sydney has used ours.

Then a visiting church lady sets a table lamp on the cash box, and while she digs for her wallet I look for the price and find a geological event. The sticker says **$40**. Under it, one corner curling, is a sticker that says **$95**. The $40 is round and patient and certain of itself, and I've deposited enough grocery lists to know my mother's handwriting at a glance. The $95 is my grandmother's — the 9 drawn like it's being paid by the stroke.

"It says $40," the lady says, watching my face.

"It says $40," I agree, and count her change back out loud, scanning the tables the whole time, because if the lamp has two stickers, the lamp is not a lamp. The lamp is a front line.

Once you see it you can't stop. The sewing cabinet: $120 under a $55. A milk-glass vase wearing three stickers deep — hers, my mother's, hers again, the top one pressed down at the corners with a thumbnail, which is my grandmother saying *and stay down*. And as my mother passes the linens table with an armload of coats, her free hand drops — one motion, smooth as a card trick — and when she's gone, the tablecloth that said $30 says $12.

Nobody says anything. Nobody has said anything all morning. Two grown women are conducting an entire negotiation in adhesive, at a volume of zero, in front of 40 strangers and God and Sister Deloris Hayes, and the customers just pay whichever number surfaced last. I band the twenties and keep my face like a drawer that balanced.

"A year of nagging finally worked," my mother tells a browsing couple, refolding a quilt with supervisor snap. That's five.

Sofi texts at 11:52: `omw with a pitcher bc you're all going to die out there. jamaica or cucumber-lime, voting closes at 1.` Sofi is 16, lives three doors down, and runs her life like a small, well-audited country. I vote cucumber-lime, check the app while the phone's out — holding steady, like a pot that knows it's being watched — and pocket it, because a customer is coming, and the customer has a handbag.

The handbag table appeared this morning: a box of purses my mother hauled out of the deep end of the garage during her relabeling campaign and priced flat at $3 before my grandmother's coffee was done. The woman picks a stiff brown one with a brass clasp and opens it to check the lining.

"Oh," she says. "Hon — there's something in this one."

She holds it out flat, lid open, like a clam. Inside: a manila envelope, soft at the corners, a window in the front, printed across the top — **JOHN MORRELL & CO., SIOUX FALLS, S.D.** Behind the window, a pay stub in purple type. And behind the stub, a $20 bill, folded small. Folded the way you fold something you mean.

"1965," the woman reads off the stub, delighted. "Can you *imagine*."

Her change is half-counted into my palm when my grandmother crosses the driveway. I never saw her start. She was 40 feet away defending the Pyrex, and I'd testify she never looked at this table — but she's here, hand on the bag, and the bag is closed.

"Not for sale."

Not sharp. Filed, the way a judge files something. The woman gets out half of *but the sticker* and stops, because my grandmother's hands are on the clasp, and her hands end arguments — line worker's hands, knuckles like tree roots, cold to the touch in July. I refund the $3 with my best register smile, and my mother watches all of it from across the yard over a stack of Tupperware, and says nothing, and sets the Tupperware down, and says nothing.

Sydney materializes, because Sydney can smell a scene through drywall. "Okay, what was in the *bag*."

And my grandmother does the thing she does — the thing I could sell tickets to, the thing that made me the grandchild on the cash box. She tells it. Except this time, complete, it goes:

"John Morrell. 1965. They paid you in envelopes then — cash money, every Friday, at a window." Her thumb turns the clasp. "You stood in a line for it."

That's the whole story.

I've heard this woman go four minutes on a butter dish. Every object in that garage has a fool who sold it, a price she paid, and one line delivered over the fool's grave — and the one with her own name in purple type gets three sentences with nobody in them. It has the shape of one of her stories the way the display cake at the store has the shape of a cake.

"Wait, was that *your* pay?" Sydney says, thrilled. "Is that your *actual*—"

"It's an envelope. They don't do it that way anymore."

She carries it — bag, stub, twenty and all — up the drive and sets it on the workbench inside the garage, in the shade, where the sale isn't. Then she comes back out and catches a man reaching for the skillet. "Wagner," I hear her open. "1948."

Sister Deloris Hayes buys the fondue pot at full sticker, which I take as a sign the afternoon is going to be excellent. I make change. I band a twenty.

Folded the way you fold something you mean. I keep the thought exactly one second — store policy — and call the next customer up.

## Essie, 1965

The envelope had her name typed in the little window, and the window had a shine to it; tilted in the light on the walk down from the pay office, it showed her back to herself, 17 and hatless, looking out of her own pay.

Friday at John Morrell, the whole plant moved toward that window at end of shift like water finding a drain. Estelle stood the line with her hands pushed into her coat pockets — not for the cold; the cold was already in them, eight hours of it, the trim floor kept the meat's temperature and the meat did not negotiate — but because a girl standing with her hands out looked like a girl waiting, and she had not been raised to look like that.

The paymaster said your name off the stub, slid the envelope under the grille, and was saying the next name before yours finished landing in your hand. It was heavier than it looked. Cash sat different than paper.

In the stairwell by the time clock there was a landing where the light had been out since spring. She stopped there. She hadn't decided to stop; her feet had decided around Wednesday, and Friday they executed.

She worked the flap loose without tearing it. Four twenties, four ones, coins in a corner fold; the stub with its purple figures taking their little bites down to net, which was the envelope, which was Friday, which went home whole to her mother's kitchen table like every Friday since the first.

Estelle took one $20 out.

She folded it in half, and in half again, and again, until it was the size of a stamp and thick as a knuckle. She worked her shoe loose at the heel, slid the bill flat under the insole, and stood on it. Then she pressed the flap smooth, put the envelope in her coat pocket, and walked out through the third door into the Friday cold — the whole way home on $20.
