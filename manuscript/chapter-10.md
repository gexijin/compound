# FULL PRICE — Ch 10 · The Doors

## 1

At Saturday's close, Emily puts a single sheet of paper on the card table, face up, where everyone stacking cups can see it, and says nothing about it. She just sets them down.

The sheet has three lines on it, in her ruler-straight printing.

*Fryer: $14.50 an hour.*
*Stand: $11.20 an hour.*
*The screenshot: +$2,310 ÷ 0 hours.*

The last line doesn't have an answer next to it, because you can't divide by zero. She's left the space blank on purpose.

Sydney reads it, snorts, and keeps stacking. Sofi reads it and nods like it confirms something she's known forever. I read it and feel the need to defend a number, which should probably tell me something.

"That screenshot's from June," I say. "It's more now."

"The hours are still zero," Emily says.

That's all she says. She folds the sheet into the notebook and we finish teardown, and I tell myself the line about zero hours is about Max, because it's easier if it's about Max.

That evening Sydney drives me home. She pulls up at the curb, and I have my hand on the door when she puts the car in park, which means the ride isn't over.

"The stock thing," she says. "Whatever you've got riding on my brother — the money part, I mean. You're up now, right?"

"Way up."

"Then get out."

I turn and look at her, because I need to check who's driving. Sydney has never once in my life told me to be careful. That is not our arrangement. She taught me to parallel park by yelling *commit*. She is the reason I've seen every good show that's come through this town. Her entire philosophy, as long as I've known her, is that the money is for the life and not the other way around.

"You sound like somebody's mom," I say.

"I sound like somebody who's watched Max be Max since he was eight." She's not laughing. She's completely flat, which on Sydney feels like a different person. "He's my brother and I'd fight anybody who says a word about him, and I'm telling you: everything with him goes up before it goes wherever it actually goes. You're ahead. People who are ahead get to leave. That's the whole prize."

"I have a number in mind," I say. "When it gets back there, I'm out."

"Back there," Sydney repeats, and looks at me for a second too long, and I realize she caught it — *back* — and is deciding not to ask. She puts the car in drive. "Okay. Goodnight."

So that's Saturday: two warnings, one in writing, one in waiting. I can repeat both of them back word for word. That turns out to be a different thing from hearing them.

## 2

Sunday after service the churchgoers perform their usual routine, lingering on the sidewalk for far too long despite the weather. It's ninety degrees and everyone stands in the sun saying goodbye for forty-five minutes.

I'm holding the empty hotdish pan — my mother's pan came back empty from the potluck, Sister Deloris Hayes's came back with a corner left in it, and nobody has said one word about it, and my mother has been in a fantastic mood for two hours — and I'm waiting for my grandmother, who is parked in a knot of church ladies with Miss Verna Iverson.

Miss Verna is telling a story I've half-heard before, about her computer. "After what happened with my computer, I don't do one thing over the phone anymore," she says. "Not one thing. If I can't sit across a table from a person, they don't get my business."

The church ladies murmur. This is settled doctrine on this sidewalk.

"That's why I thank the Lord for my advisor," Miss Verna says. "Gary. He comes to me. Sits right at my kitchen table with his folder, remembers everything I tell him — he asks about Daniel by name. When Daniel needed the down payment last year, there was some fee business with getting my money out — you know how they are — and Gary handled every bit of it. I never had to lift a finger. That's a real person, across a real table. You can't get that from a phone."

"Mm," my grandmother says.

"You'd like him," Miss Verna says.

"Mm," my grandmother repeats noncommitally, and asks after Verna's tomatoes, and the sidewalk moves on to tomatoes.

I catch myself thinking that Miss Verna looks happier telling that story than anyone has ever looked talking about money, and that whoever Gary is, he has figured out something about sitting at kitchen tables that the banks downtown never will. Then my mother waves the car keys at me over four heads, and I stop thinking about it, because the pan is hot and the parking lot is hotter, and tomorrow is Monday, and Monday is the day my number comes back.

## 3

Sunday supper is the three of us at our kitchen table: me, my mother, my grandmother, and the second hotdish — the one that never went to the potluck, because my mother makes two, one for the church and one for the family, and has done it that way since before I was born.

The conversation is weather, first. There's a storm supposed to come Tuesday, and all three of us have opinions about whether the tomatoes will make it, by which we mean my grandmother's tomatoes, which have not made it a single summer in my lifetime and are discussed every year like a promising rookie.

"The Canaries lost by one," my grandmother reports. "The scoreboard gave their man a hit in the fourth that was an error to anyone with eyes. My book has it right."

"You should mail them the book," I say.

"They have two of my letters."

My phone is in my lap, under the table. $4,292.16 — same as Friday night. The market is closed on Sundays. I know the market is closed on Sundays, but I check it twice anyway, once when the hotdish lands and once when my mother gets up for the pitcher, and the number sits there not moving, the way a number does when it's waiting for Monday. I have the sell order built. Quantity: 126. Monday, at Friday's number or better, I'm out. I've said it to myself enough times that it has stopped sounding like a plan I made after the moment passed.

"Mama," my mother says, somewhere between servings, "what's happening with the rest of the garage? The leftovers from the sale, all those boxes. You want me to list the big stuff?"

My grandmother doesn't look up from her plate. "It's being handled," she says. "My fellow at the center has been helping me simplify."

There's a beat where the word *fellow* sits on the table, and I watch my mother decide to be gracious and not tease her about the Tuesday suppers in front of me.

"Well," my mother says instead, and I can hear a year of nagging cashing itself out in her voice, "it's about time somebody got you to simplify. I've been saying it since last summer."

"You have," my grandmother agrees, pleasantly, and passes the beans.

And that's it. That's the whole exchange. We talk about the storm again, and the church roof fund, and whether the bus schedule is changing in the fall. Three women at one table, and the talk touches every single subject in Sioux Falls except one, and lands nowhere near a dollar amount, and the supper is honestly nice. It's the nicest supper of the summer. I do the dishes; my grandmother drives home before dark; my mother wipes the table and tells me she's proud of how the stand looked Friday, and I go upstairs with the phone already in my hand.

Monday. Friday's number or better.

## Denise, Week 8

After the girl went up and the house went quiet, Denise wiped the table a second time and sat down at it with a pencil and the back of an envelope, because that's where arithmetic happened in her house.

She wrote down what was in the unlabeled envelope. The car had bitten into it — the CAR envelope hadn't covered the repair, and the difference had come out of the tuition money, and she'd known it at the time and done it anyway, because a car that starts is not optional for a woman with her schedule. She wrote down the three Sundays left before the due date, times the hours, times time and a half. She added it twice, the way she was raised.

The total stopped short of the number in the portal. Not squinting-distance short. Short.

So she checked the other doors, the way she read a register tape: top to bottom, no skipping, no hoping.

A credit card. She had never had one. Not one dime of interest paid in her life, not once, to anyone — she'd watched thirty years of coworkers carry balances the way you carry a stone in your shoe, and she had never once been one of them.

A loan, then. She'd had exactly one, for the car, years back. The bank had looked at a woman with no credit card, no balances, no history — a lifetime of paying for everything in full, on time, in cash — and offered her a rate you'd quote to a criminal. She'd taken it, hated it, and paid it off early. Of course she had. A file that thin didn't get better offers by asking twice.

The house. You can't borrow against a house that isn't yours, and the house had never been hers. The rent had gone out on the first of the month, every month, since before Keisha was born.

That was all the doors. Each one shut, and each one shut for a reason she could name, and she sat with the pencil for a moment in the quiet of her own kitchen, not letting any particular thought finish.

Then she reached up and unclasped the chain.

It took her two tries; the clasp was small and her hands were tired. She laid it on the envelope, right next to the arithmetic, half an ounce of it, catching the kitchen light. She'd been in the yard the day the number went around — she knew what it meant. She'd carried that number with her every day since she'd found out.

She looked at it next to the total for a while.

Then she put it in her purse, in the zip pocket, and snapped the purse shut. Not to sell it. To find out. There was a difference, and she planned to stay on the right side of it as long as she could.
