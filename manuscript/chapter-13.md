# FULL PRICE — Ch 13 · The Contract

## 1

I finish the contract Friday morning before my shift, at the kitchen table, with coffee I don't usually drink.

Forty-one pages, and by the end of it I start writing down everything I remember, because it took me half the summer to learn to read this kind of document and one night to find out why it matters.

It's legal. That's the first thing to understand. Nothing in these pages is a crime. It's an annuity with a sunrise on the cover, and it's a bad deal dressed in vocabulary, and the vocabulary works.

The surrender schedule, page 19. If my grandmother puts her money in and wants it back out, there's a toll. Eight percent of whatever she takes in the first year. Seven the second year. It steps down one percent a year, which means the door out of this product is seven years long, and she is seventy-eight years old. The word for the toll is "surrender charge," and I read the definition four times, and it means what it sounds like it means.

His pay, page 33, in the compensation disclosure, one paragraph in small print: seven percent of everything she puts in, paid to him up front by the company. The company is not a charity. The seven percent comes back out of her, through the schedule, through the years. That's what the surrender charge is for. It's how they make sure she stays long enough to pay for Gary.

And the reason for the circle on the calendar card, page 4: a "signing bonus," five percent added to her account if the application is dated by Monday, August 24. After that, the bonus goes away. Nothing else in the contract changes on the 24th. The date exists so that a decision that deserves a year gets a weekend. Gary never said one word about it out loud. He didn't have to; the paperwork applies the pressure so his voice never carries any.

Then, on a worksheet clipped inside the back cover, in blue ballpoint, in the same hand that circled the date, under a printed line that says CLIENT GOALS: *simplifying your estate.* And under it, a note, dated in February: *household — collections, garage. Spring?*

I put the worksheet down and sit there while the coffee goes cold.

The garage sale. The one that opened this whole summer, the one my mother campaigned a year for and finally won. It's in his handwriting, five months before it happened. My mother thinks her nagging worked. My grandmother let her think it. The biggest family event of the year was a line item in a salesman's plan, and the woman who fought every price to the curb was complying with a program, and nobody at that sale knew they were at his event, including me, and I ran the cash box.

One more piece of arithmetic before work, because once you can read these things you can't stop. Miss Verna, on the sidewalk, sweet as anything: *some fee business with getting my money out — you know how they are.* Her son's down payment. A down payment is real money — fifty thousand, give or take. If Verna's money was sitting inside a schedule like page 19 and she pulled a down payment out early, the fee business was three or four thousand dollars. Call it four thousand, to reach her own money, and she still doesn't know that's what it was. She thanked God for the man who "handled" it.

And she went to that man in the first place because a voice on the phone had already robbed her, and after that she only trusted a person at her own kitchen table. That's the whole machine, and I finally see all of it at once. The scams aren't competition for Gary. They're his supply line. Every widow in this city who gets robbed by a phone learns the same lesson — trust a real person, across a real table — and Gary is the real person who comes to the table. The fear does his advertising. The senior center does his scheduling. Verna does his referrals, for free, out of gratitude.

Rung zero was the man who handled my great-grandfather— no. I'll say it the way my grandmother would count it: 1975, the settlement man. 1976, the load salesman, who at least printed his price where she could read it. 2026, Gary. Fifty years, three men, one profile: a woman alone with money nobody's watching.

Except the profile is out of date by one person, because this time somebody in the family reads.

## 2

Friday after my shift I drive to my grandmother's to return the folder, and I don't return it. I spread it out on her kitchen table instead, opened to page 19.

"Before Monday," I say, "you're going to read three pages with me."

She sits down. She doesn't argue about that part; reading has never once been the fight in this family.

I walk her through it the way I walked the clerks through the ordinance. The surrender schedule, year by year, out loud — eight, seven, six — and what each number would cost her at seventy-eight, seventy-nine, eighty. His seven percent, up front, page 33, read word for word. The bonus that dies Monday, and what a date like that is for. She follows every step; she's better at this arithmetic than I am and we both know it. That's never been the problem. The problem has never once been whether Estelle Carter can do the math.

"The load man in seventy-six charged eight-fifty a hundred," she says at the end, "and told me to my face. This one charges more and buries it on page thirty-three." She says it with something that is almost respect for the dead salesman. Then: "I hadn't signed anything, and I wasn't going to."

"Then what's the folder still doing in your kitchen? What's Monday still doing on your calendar?"

She doesn't answer that.

I take out the worksheet, and I put it in front of her, and I put my finger on *simplifying your estate,* February, his ink.

"The sale was his idea."

"It was time anyway," my grandmother says. "Forty years of things in that garage."

"Mom thinks she won. She nagged you for a year and she thinks the year worked, and you let her think it, and the whole time it was this." I keep my voice level because raising it would end the conversation, and this conversation doesn't get to end. "He's been inside this family since February. He's on the fridge. He's in the coffee can. He drove you home in front of all of us. The only person who ever saw him is the one nobody tells anything to — me — and I only saw him because you got caught being kind to me on a Wednesday."

And here my grandmother defends him. Once. I watch her do it and I understand that she has to, and what it costs.

"He remembered Nate's name," she says. "Sixty years, and the people at my own church get it wrong. Fifty years of men with papers, and not one of them ever asked me a second question. He asked. Every Tuesday, he asked, and he listened to every answer all the way to the end. You don't know what that's worth, because you're nineteen, and people still ask you things."

"He remembered Nate's name because he wrote it down," I say. "It's in a file, Grandma. The listening is the product. He sells it to Verna on Wednesdays."

She doesn't say anything. She looks at the worksheet, at her own life summarized in another man's ballpoint — *widow, tenure, house paid, collections, garage, spring* — and I watch the Tuesdays reprice themselves in front of her, every single one, the good blue blouse and the earrings that click, and I would give a lot to not be the person who did this, and I would do it again.

"Wednesday was about your money," she says finally. "This is mine. I've handled my own money since 1965, and I've handled it fine — better than fine — and I have never needed a soul."

"I know. That's the problem I'm here about." I put the pages back in order, because my hands need a job. "Grandma. You hid it for fifty years so nobody could take it, and hiding worked while the wolves had to find you by accident. They don't anymore. There's an industry now. It calls every phone in this city, and the ones it can't rob it frightens, and the frightened ones get walked by their friends straight to a nice man with a folder. He found you in February in a building you go to every Tuesday, warmly referred, because Verna loves him, because he handled the fee business, and Verna got tolled twice and doesn't know it either time — the phone took twenty thousand—"

"Twenty," my grandmother says quietly. Church ladies know everything.

"—and the rescue took four more, and she still doesn't know what the second toll was. That's what being alone with money looks like now. Nobody has to catch you anymore. You're a profile. A homeowning widow with fifty years of quiet money is what his Tuesdays are *for*. Hiding stopped protecting you the day he found you. The only thing left that protects you is people who know."

"You know," she says.

"I'm a college student with eight hundred dollars and a bus pass. I mean Mom." She starts to speak and I don't let her. "I'm not keeping this from her. I told you my everything on Wednesday, and I'm done being a keeper — I'm the worst-qualified keeper in this family, you've seen my books. And I'm not going behind your back to tell her either, because that's the same disease with my handwriting on it. So there's one door. You tell her. Yourself. Sunday, at the table, before Monday puts a pen in this kitchen."

My grandmother sits with that for a long time. The refrigerator hums. Somewhere in the garage there's a lamp neither of us has priced in a week.

What moves her, in the end, isn't my speech, and I know it. It's the worksheet, still facing her on the table, February, *spring?*, his handwriting in her life. She watched a man listen to her for six months, and fifteen minutes ago she found out what the listening was for, and there is no arguing anybody out of or into that. There's only sitting next to them while it lands.

"All right," she says.

That's all. She squares the folder, both hands.

"Sunday," I say. "I'll be there. You just have to say it once."

"You," my grandmother says, standing up, taking the folder with her, and here it comes, the toll for winning, "can wait three hours in wet grass for a bird. But thirty seconds to tell your own mother something — that, you couldn't find all summer."

She goes to put the folder away. I let the needle stay in, because it's fair, and because Sunday I pull it out myself.

## 3

Saturday runs the market like the summer's ending, because it is. Three beats from the last ordinary morning.

Sydney arrives without her October concert ticket. She sold it Thursday night, face value, to a coworker — the Premier Center show she's been narrating since April, floor section, the good screaming distance. The money went against her credit card the same hour so it couldn't change its mind. She announces all of this herself, at setup, to everyone, and then she lets herself be sad about it in public, which I have never seen her do about anything. "I wanted the night," she says, stacking cups. "I just want the card gone more. Both of those are true and they don't fit." Nobody rounds it up for her. She doesn't round it up for herself.

Mid-morning, Emily's phone buzzes on the card table, face up, and a green banner with a little bank logo sits there in the sun while she finishes a line in the notebook, and she glances at it, and keeps writing. That's the whole event. The phone isn't face-down anymore. Judging by the banner — *scheduled transfer complete* — it hasn't been for weeks; there's a rhythm to it now, money moving somewhere on purpose, every week, quietly. She never announced it. There was no moment. The door just opened at some point this summer, on her own terms, and today is only the day I finally notice. I don't say anything, because some doors you don't stand in front of clapping.

And at two o'clock, from my own kitchen table, I unmute the bridge chat and join the design call.

Twenty minutes. That's what it takes. The connection design questions are questions I know; I've had the binder since May and the rules memorized since June, and when the compliance question comes up — it's the gusset detail, it's always the gusset detail — I cite the section number from memory and read the sentence off the page, and somebody says "that's why she has the binder," and we move on. Nobody asks where I've been all summer. Nobody says *you went quiet.* The team treats me exactly the way they treated me in May, because from their side of the phone, nothing ever happened. I muted them for eleven days and priced the whole seat lost, and the seat never moved.

I sit in the kitchen after the call ends and let that one land all the way. The catastrophe I spent the summer managing was a Tuesday for every single other person in it. My mother was going to be fine. The team was going to be fine. The only person who ever wrote me out of my own fall was me, at this table, alone, in June.

Tomorrow the being-alone part ends, on purpose, out loud.

Sunday, six o'clock, my mother's table. My grandmother brings her papers. I bring every number I have.
