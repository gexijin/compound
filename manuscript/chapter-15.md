# FULL PRICE — Ch 15 · The Heron

## 1

Monday at two o'clock the gray sedan pulls up on schedule, because Gary is never late; being reliable is part of the product.

My mother is at the table. She asked a coworker to cover two hours of her shift — asked, out loud, on the phone, while I stood there listening to a thing I had never once heard her do — and now she sits at her mother's kitchen table in her work clothes with her hands folded, and she does not intend to say one word, and she doesn't. I'm next to her with my pen. My grandmother sits at the head, and in front of her, squared with both hands, is the contract.

We spent an hour on it after church. It has tabs now. The definitions are circled, the surrender schedule has arithmetic in the margin in my handwriting, page 33 is flagged in yellow, and the worksheet with *simplifying your estate* is clipped on top, face up.

Gary knocks, comes in warm, and sees all of it at once: three women, one table, no empty chairs, and his own paperwork annotated to the edges. If it lands anywhere on him, it doesn't show. He says good afternoon to each of us by name. He congratulates me on the school year starting.

"Sit down, Gary," my grandmother says, and he does, and she puts on her reading glasses.

She reads him two of his own clauses. She reads them the way she reads box scores, without commentary, in the plant-floor voice that carries: the schedule on page 19, eight percent stepping down to nothing over seven years, every number said in full. Then the paragraph underneath it, the one that says the charge applies to every dollar above the free amount, every year, no exceptions for hardship, health, or death of a spouse. She reads that one slower.

Then she takes the glasses off and asks her one question.

"Page thirty-three says the company pays you seven percent of whatever I put in, the day I put it in. Is that right?"

"That's the standard structure for this class of product," Gary says, pleasantly, because it is, and because he has answered this question before in this exact tone at other tables.

"Mm," my grandmother says. "Then we're done, and you can have your folder back. I'm keeping the worksheet."

And that's the whole fight. There isn't one. Every move in his kit needs a woman alone at her own table, and there are three of us, and the table is full of handwriting. He doesn't argue a clause, because the clauses are all true and we've read them. He doesn't mention the bonus that dies at midnight, because the bonus only works on people who haven't priced it. He gathers his folder, thanks my grandmother for her time with what appears to be complete sincerity, tells me Brookings is lucky to have me, and wishes my mother a good afternoon on his way out.

At the door he says, "You have my card if anything changes," and means it, and is gone. Through the window I watch the sedan pull away at the speed limit, unhurried, a man with other Tuesdays on his calendar and other referrals warming up. We didn't beat him. Nobody beats a channel. We just took one table off his route, and the route is long.

Before supper, the Canaries schedule comes off the fridge, gets a new magnet from the junk drawer — a pizza place that closed in 2019 — and goes back up straight. Nobody says anything about it. The sunrise magnet goes in the trash, under the coffee grounds, where my grandmother puts things she's finished with.

## 2

Tuesday morning I drive my grandmother downtown, to the shop between the title company and the sandwich place, because she asked me to and because she is the person in this family who finds things.

She has the dealer's card and the receipt, borrowed from my mother's purse with my mother's knowledge. She has, folded in an envelope, $1,980 plus something extra for the trouble, because she assumed the trouble would be a negotiation and she has never once lost one of those.

The dealer recognizes the receipt before he's read it. He's polite about it, and he doesn't make us ask twice, and he doesn't pretend. He opens a ledger, runs his finger down a column, and turns it around so she can see the line.

The chain went to the refiner Friday. Melted, with that week's lot, before any of us ever sat down at the table. It was gone before the dinner. It was gone while we were still lying to each other.

My grandmother looks at the ledger line for a moment. She thanks him for keeping a clean book — she means it; a clean book is the highest thing she says about anybody — and she puts the envelope back in her purse, and we leave.

In the car she doesn't say anything, and I don't either. There's nothing to negotiate with. The money still exists, untouched, in my mother's envelope. The receipt still exists. The object is a bar in a vault somewhere, indistinguishable from every other ounce, and no amount of finding will ever make it that chain again.

We don't stop at any counter on the way home, and nobody ever suggests a replacement, that day or after. Some prices stay paid. That one stays paid twice.

## 3

Midweek, the money moves.

The school's email arrives with the date in bold — **Move-in: Saturday, August 29, 7:00 a.m.–noon** — and under it, the housing assignment I paid $150 to keep. Wednesday my grandmother's $5,000 leaves the account she opened when I was two, and Thursday it shows at the bursar, two days ahead of the deadline, exactly the way it was always going to, whether or not I spent a summer on fire about it.

That leaves $2,800, and it's mine, and I can reach it.

I sell the wreckage first — all 136 shares, $813.06 back into my checking account, the cartoon rocket deleted from my phone in the parking lot where I installed it. Between that, the last fryer checks, and my share of the stand — counted out by Emily at the card table, two-person rule, out loud — my share is covered with a little to spare, and the campus library has me down for ten hours a week starting the second week of September, because the girl who reads might as well get paid for it.

I pay it Thursday morning at the kitchen table, on my phone, with my mother sitting across from me. We count it first — out loud, both of us, the whole stack of everything the summer earned — and then I press the button, and the portal thinks about it, and the balance page reloads:

**$0.00 — PAID IN FULL.**

My mother reads it upside down and nods once, the way she nods at a drawer that balances.

Her envelopes go back in the kitchen drawer. The unlabeled one is still full — the Sundays, the chain money, all of it, needed by nobody now — and for the first time since June it has no job. She hasn't decided what it is yet. She said so out loud, at the table, which two weeks ago would have been science fiction. Nobody rushes her.

## 4

Thursday night is the stand's last Levitt of the summer — Emily leaves for Augustana the same weekend I leave for Brookings, and a company where the reader and the bookkeeper are both out of town is a company on pause.

We sell out. Of course we sell out; we're good at this now. Sofi collects her royalty in front of everyone, $2 a batch, and announces the running total in her sales-clerk voice, and Sydney takes orders for a season that doesn't exist yet, and Lourdes counts her stockpot back into her own two hands.

Teardown ends at eleven. The others peel off the way they always do — Sofi's cousin, Sydney and Emily — until it's me and Max and the loaded Vibe, the same as every Thursday all summer, except that we haven't really spoken since the red morning, and both of us know tonight's the last load.

We get everything in. He shuts the hatch. And then, instead of walking around to the driver's side, he stands there, and when he talks it's the flat voice, the timecard voice, the one with no charts in it.

"One thing," he says. "Then I'll drive you home and we don't have to do a whole scene about it."

"Okay."

"I made guessing sound like knowing because I liked that you trusted me."

That's it. That's the whole thing. He doesn't say he lost too, though he lost more. He doesn't ask me to say it's okay. He doesn't reach for the one card he's holding — because he did tell me, in a parking lot, in plain words, and he has never once played it — and I watch him not play it, and that's worth more than the apology.

"It was my thumb," I say.

"It was your thumb," he agrees. "It was my mouth."

"Both those things are true and they don't fit," I say, which is Sydney's line, and he knows it's Sydney's line, and something in his face untightens for the first time all month.

"Thank you," I say. "That's the truest thing anybody's said all summer, and there was some competition."

We get in the Vibe and he drives me home with the windows down, past the falls, and neither of us names anything, because nothing needs a name to be worth what it's worth. At my curb he says, "Text me your heron when you get it," which is one small promise about a bird and no promises about anything else, and that's exactly the right size.

The Vibe still smells like hibiscus. It probably always will. That's the whole report.

## 5

Saturday, dawn, the car is packed by 6:10 — bedding, the milk-crate of kitchen stuff, and the bridge-team rules binder, tabbed and ready since May, riding on top where it can't be crushed.

My mother is in the driver's seat with the engine running and two coffees in the cupholders. She's been ready for this morning since June; there's a store calendar with a box around today to prove it. She protected this day before I ever knew she knew anything, back when we were both carrying the same number in separate pockets, and now the day is just a day: a mother driving her kid to school, with the tuition paid and the windows down.

"Ten minutes," I say. "One stop."

She doesn't ask which stop. She drives to Falls Park and pulls into the empty lot, and I walk down past the market spot where the vendors are unloading — the kettle-corn guy lifts his chin at me — and down the walk to the railing above the third ledge, where the water comes over white and the light at this hour is the good light.

The heron is there.

He's standing in the shallows below the ledge, exactly where he's always been, on the water I've watched at dawn and at midnight, and for three summers this is the moment where the camera takes a second to decide and the bird decides faster.

I raise the phone. I tap the screen. He stays.

I get the shot.

It's a little blurry — his head is sharp and one wing has motion in it, and a real photographer would call it a miss. It has no feather detail and no eye ring, and nobody will ever show it around on the back of a camera. It's mine. I stood on this railing with what I had, and I got it with what I had, and it cost nothing and proves nothing and it is completely, entirely mine.

I look at the bird for one more second. He goes back to fishing. He was never in this for me.

Then I walk up the hill to the car, where my mother is waiting with the engine running, and I get in, and we drive north.
