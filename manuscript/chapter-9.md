# FULL PRICE — Ch 9 · The Box

## 1

JazzFest takes over the Levitt lawn for the last weekend of July, and our letter says *event*, and JazzFest is nothing if not an event.

We brew seven batches. Sofi's kitchen has smelled like hibiscus for two days straight, and Lourdes comes with us Friday night, partly to get her stockpot back and partly because she has looked at our projections and decided her daughter's company is going to need adults. She takes one end of the pour line without being asked. Max hauls ice twice before the opener even starts.

By six o'clock the line at our table is never shorter than twenty people, and it stays that way. I pour, Sofi pours, Lourdes pours, Sydney works the line like it's a show of its own, and Emily's pen never stops moving. At some point the kettle-corn guy sends a bag down the row to us, no charge, which I understand is how vendors say congratulations.

My mother comes at a quarter to eight.

She's still in her work clothes. She's been saying she'll come see the stand since June, and every Saturday something at the store has eaten her morning, and now here she is on the biggest night we have ever had, standing in our line. Not at the side of the table, where family stands. In the line. She waits her turn behind nine strangers, and when she gets to the front she looks at the whole operation — the coolers, the tally, the cash box, her daughter — the way she reads a register tape.

"One, please," she says.

"Family rate is a dollar—"

"Full price." She puts two dollars in my hand. "You don't discount family when the line's this long."

She steps out of the way of the next customer, drinks it standing where she can see everything, and doesn't rush. "Your line moves quick," she says, which from my mother is a trophy. And then, quieter, watching Emily's pen: "You count all this?"

"Every night. Twice, just like you taught me."

"Good," she says. She throws the cup away in the correct barrel, tells me she'll leave the porch light on, and goes home before the headliner, because she opens the store in the morning. She was here maybe twenty minutes. I put her two dollars in the box myself, and I smile stupidly about the whole thing for the rest of the night.

## 2

At 7:15 that night, the stand is having the best hour of its life, and so is my account.

I've been checking it all day, because the stock has been climbing all week, and after my shift it crossed a line I had marked in my head without ever saying it. $4,292.16. I bought in at $1,500. If I sell all of it tonight — all 126 shares — then that number, plus the paychecks left in the summer, plus my share of the stand, puts the bill close enough to touch. Not covered. Close. All summer the math has refused to reach $7,800, and tonight, for the first time, it reaches.

I always told myself I'd sell when it was enough. It's enough.

The app trades until seven; I checked that weeks ago, then six more times since then to make sure. At 6:52 I'm standing behind the table with the sell screen open, quantity: 126, my thumb over the button, waiting for one break in the line.

The break never comes.

The gates at the far end open, the lawn compresses, and a whole crowd's worth of people decide at the same time to be somewhere else. It moves through the vendor row like weather. Our table gets bumped hard enough that a cooler slides, a stack of cups goes down, somebody's elbow catches Sofi, Sydney is shouting *one line, one LINE,* and for about four minutes it is all hands and no thinking. I put my phone in my pocket to catch the cups.

When the lawn settles, the cups are back, the coolers are half empty, and the cash box is gone.

The next hour is the worst hour of the summer. We search the table, the bins, the Vibe, the grass. Emily goes through everything twice in a straight line like a person mowing a lawn, and her face has no color in it. The neighboring vendors get involved, because a missing cash box is every vendor's nightmare, and involvement turns out to be its own problem: the funnel-cake vendor's wife saw a boy behind our table during the surge — the shaved-ice kid, maybe twelve, who has been running up and down the row all night being twelve — and by the third telling she didn't *see* him, she *knows*. Other adults start nodding. The kid is standing next to his father's cart with his ears turning red, and Sydney, who is scared and tired and out four hundred dollars of the company's night, says, "He was back there. I saw him back there too."

"How much was in it?" the funnel-cake woman asks, ready to make it official.

Emily doesn't want to say it and says it anyway, because numbers are the one thing she won't bend. "The tally says $713.50."

"Then that boy's got $713.50."

"It didn't have $713.50 in it," I say.

Everyone looks at me. Emily looks at me like I've said the earth is flat, because her tally has never been wrong in its life.

"The box had $653.50. The tally's off by sixty dollars." I make myself say the rest of it at normal volume, to the whole row. "Because Thursday morning I took three twenties out of it to pay for ice, and I didn't tell anyone, and I didn't put it in the book. I was going to pay it back tonight and nobody was ever going to know. So the book is wrong, and it's wrong because of me — and if the book can be wrong because of me, then nobody here is accusing a twelve-year-old of a number nobody actually knows. We audit everything first. Every dollar in, every dollar out, out loud, or nobody gets accused of anything."

Nobody says anything for a second. The funnel-cake woman looks less official. The kid's father puts his hand on the kid's shoulder.

And that is when Lourdes walks up carrying the cash box.

"The crowd came through," she says, like it's the most obvious thing in the world. "I took it to my car and locked it. I told the table."

"Nobody heard you," Sofi says.

"I said it in Spanish and in English," Lourdes says, "to the backs of six heads."

So there it is. No thief, no crime, a box that was safe in a Corolla the entire hour, and a row full of adults who nearly convicted a child because nobody passed the word. The kid gets an apology — Sydney walks over and makes it herself, out loud, to him and his father both, which I will not forget — and the vendors drift back to their carts like it never happened.

My confession can't be taken back, though, and I slowly see everyone else coming to terms with it.

"You took sixty dollars out of the box," Sydney says. Not loud, not angry.

"Thursday. For ice. I was short until payday, and I didn't want to say why."

She looks at me the way she looked at me over the cooler on Saturday, like I'm a door that's stopped opening of it's own accord. Emily opens the notebook against her knee, writes the correction in her straight hand — the date, the amount, my initial — and closes it without a word, which is worse than shouting at me.

It's 8:20 by the time anyone thinks about anything else. I remember to check my phone at 8:40.

The sell screen is still open. Quantity: 126. The session closed at seven. The button doesn't even work now; it's just a picture of a button.

Monday, I tell myself. It's a good company. It went up all week. I'll sell Monday, at tonight's number or better.

That's the plan I go to sleep on: it comes back to tonight's number, and then I sell.

## 3

The next saturday morning, we're setting up at the market at our usual time, only this time without the usual chatter.

Emily calls a meeting at setup that lasts forty seconds. "New rule. The box opens with two people, or it doesn't open. Every open gets counted, both ends, out loud, effective immediately." Nobody argues. I second it, which everyone lets me do without comment, which is a kindness.

Before I bag the ice I hand her three twenties from Friday's paycheck, and she counts them into the box out loud with Sofi watching, and enters it, and that particular sixty dollars is finally where it belongs, four days late, with an audience.

Mid-morning my grandmother comes by the market — she does that some Saturdays, on her way from an estate sale. She lifts the box, sets it on the card table, and counts it. She's been counting it since June; it's her right, as the owner of thirty percent of our company. It used to be the joke she did to make us roll our eyes.

She counts it all the way through, says nothing about the total, puts the band back on, and sets the box down.

Nobody laughs. It isn't a joke now, and everyone at the table knows exactly when it stopped being one.

$60, repaid, counted.

## Essie, 2009

The office was nearly empty in January, which suited her.

The paper had spent the fall using the word *crisis* on the front page, and by the new year half the men she'd worked beside were afraid to open the envelopes their statements came in. Estelle had read every statement to the last line, the way she read everything, and then driven downtown through the slush.

The young man at the desk had a sign with his name on it and a tie he hadn't grown into. She told him what she wanted: a college account, the kind where the money grows and the taxes wait, in the name of her granddaughter. Keisha Carter. Two years old.

He typed for a while. Then he felt he should say something, because of the year it was. "I just want to make sure — with the market where it is right now — you're comfortable putting money in?"

"Everything's on sale," Estelle said. "I've shopped sales all my life."

He didn't have a form response for that, so he typed.

She wrote the first check for $400 with the pen from her purse, not his, and her signature came out level, first try, the way it had since 1976. The confirmation printed and she read it twice before she folded it.

At home she took a sheet of lined paper and ruled it methodically. Four lines. On each line she wrote $5,000, and next to each one, no date — just the word *when*, four times, in her small print. When the girl goes. When she goes back. When she goes back. When she finishes.

She folded the page behind the confirmation, put both in the folder, and put the folder where her folders went, under everything, at the back, in the quiet, where her money had lived for forty-four years.

In the morning she bought socks.
