# FULL PRICE

** Don't read this if you don't want to know the entire plot **


Nineteen-year-old Keisha Carter is the first person in her family to attend college. When she discovers a $7,800 tuition shortfall due at the end of summer, she decides to solve it alone. What she does not know is that her mother, Denise, and grandmother, Estelle, have each discovered the same bill—and are secretly mounting separate rescues of their own.

## Act 1 — The Garage

The summer begins at Estelle’s enormous, chaotic garage sale in Sioux Falls. Keisha is happy, newly finished with finals, and working the cash box alongside her friends. Beneath her excitement, however, she keeps checking the $1,503.72 in her bank account and calculating how far it is from the tuition she owes.

The sale reveals that Estelle has spent a lifetime assigning value to things no one else understands. A forgotten pay envelope from 1965 still contains a twenty-dollar bill. A gold chain mistakenly placed in a two-dollar box turns out to be worth more than $2,000. Estelle gives the chain to Denise, saying she should have done so years ago.

During the sale, Keisha receives her fall engineering-team schedule and confronts the full arithmetic: her fryer job cannot close the tuition gap. She tells no one. Moments later, the reader learns Denise has already accessed the parent portal and seen the same bill. Denise quietly begins taking every available Sunday shift to pay it.

Keisha and her friends also stumble into a business idea: selling Sofi’s agua fresca at Falls Park. Estelle invests $100 for a share. At the same time, Keisha notices Max, the overconfident older brother of her friend Sydney, celebrating a profitable stock trade. The summer now presents her with two possible solutions: a fragile small business and the seductive promise of fast money.

## Act 2 — The Stand

The agua fresca stand fails immediately when the girls discover they need a permit. Keisha studies the city ordinance with the same intensity she brings to engineering rules, finds a legal workaround, and gets them operating. The victory teaches her a dangerous lesson: every system can be beaten if she reads its rules more carefully than everyone else.

Her search for a second job goes nowhere. Every employer wants hours already claimed by the fryer and the stand, and even maximum legal shifts will not produce enough money before tuition is due.

Keisha finally tells Max about the $7,800. He helps her open a brokerage account, and she begins copying his stock trades with her own savings. Max’s success is luck disguised as expertise, but Keisha believes the market is simply another system she can master.

Meanwhile, the stand finds its real customers among the evening concert crowds. Sales climb, Sofi negotiates ownership of her recipe, and the girls begin learning what their work is worth. In the background, Estelle’s new “gentleman friend,” Gary, appears through senior-center meetings, branded pens, and retirement-company materials. The reader recognizes him as a salesman long before Keisha does.

## Act 3 — The Boom

The stand and Keisha’s stock position rise together. Keisha grows closer to Max during late-night teardown shifts, but neither names the relationship. Her paper profits approach the amount she needs, strengthening her belief that secrecy has worked.

Every success, however, creates another vulnerability. When Denise’s car needs an $800 repair, Denise quietly uses money she had been saving for tuition. Keisha, whose savings are now trapped in stocks, borrows $60 from the stand’s cash box to cover supplies. When the box briefly disappears during their busiest night, Keisha must confess the unauthorized loan in front of everyone.

At the same time, the reader learns Estelle opened a college account for Keisha when she was two and has planned four annual $5,000 payments. Keisha is gambling to solve a problem that is much smaller than she believes.

Denise recalculates her own rescue and realizes her remaining shifts will not be enough. The only valuable asset she owns is Estelle’s gold chain. She places it in her purse.

At a family supper, Keisha, Denise, and Estelle sit together while each privately carries a plan to pay the same bill. Estelle casually mentions that Gary has been helping her “simplify.” No one asks what that means.

## Act 4 — The Bust

Ignoring warnings from her friends and even Max, Keisha invests more. The stock collapses by eighty percent, wiping out her savings and an advance on her stand earnings. Humiliated, she blames Max, cuts him off, mutes her college team, and retreats further into silence.

Denise sells the gold chain and sets the proceeds aside for tuition.

Estelle finally notices that Keisha’s usual jokes and numerical observations have disappeared. When Keisha says she is fine, Estelle asks the forbidden second question: “How much did you lose?” Keisha confesses everything.

Estelle reveals that $5,000 is already scheduled for tuition, meaning Keisha only needed to raise $2,800. While showing Keisha the account paperwork, Estelle inadvertently exposes a contract from Gary’s retirement company. Keisha reads it overnight and discovers surrender penalties, a seven-percent commission, and a manufactured deadline. She also realizes Gary encouraged the garage sale as part of his campaign to “simplify” Estelle’s estate.

Keisha estimates that Estelle may have as much as $80,000. The truth is far larger. She persuades Estelle that hidden money is no longer protected simply because the family does not discuss it. Before Gary returns with a pen, Estelle must tell Denise everything.

## Act 5 — The Table

At Sunday dinner, the three women finally say their numbers aloud.

Keisha confesses the bill, the borrowed money, the trading account, and the crash. Estelle responds by revealing that she has accumulated $900,000 over fifty years. She opened Keisha’s college account when Keisha was two, and the same support will be available to every grandchild who enrolls. Her secrecy was meant to make Keisha independent, but instead it nearly caused Keisha to gamble away her education.

Denise then reveals that she knew about the bill all summer and sold Estelle’s chain to pay it. Estelle tells them the chain’s full history: she bought it for herself in 1984, pawned it during the financial crisis of 1987, redeemed it two years later, and never wore it again because it reminded her of the one time fear made her sell.

Three women have now paid separately for the same fear. The tuition money can be restored; the chain cannot. The dealer has already melted it.

The next morning, Gary arrives expecting Estelle to sign. Instead, he finds all three women at the table with his contract covered in notes. Estelle questions his penalties and commission, and he leaves without a signature.

Estelle’s planned $5,000 transfer posts before the deadline. Keisha covers the remaining $2,800 with her fryer wages, her share of the stand, what remains of her investments, and a campus job. On move-in morning, before leaving Sioux Falls, she finally photographs the heron she has chased for three summers. The picture is slightly blurry, earned without expensive equipment, and completely hers.
