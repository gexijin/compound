# FULL PRICE — Ch 6 · The Line

## 1

The company's fourth Saturday, the heron doesn't show. The retiree does. He's back from the dam with a week of pelican photos, and he shows everyone, including me, including the honey woman, who catches my eye over his shoulder with the face of a woman who has seen the pelicans four times. The photos are very good. I hate them.

The market itself is our best yet. The jogger's friends have friends now. The bratwurst guy is up to two cups before nine. A woman buys the last of the records and asks if we'll have more next week, and Sydney says "we're restocking," which is technically true of a garage.

The church lady finds me at the cooler.

"How many weeks till you leave for school, sweetheart?"

"Six."

"Six," she repeats, and pats my hand like the number belongs to both of us now.

At close, Emily runs the tally: customers, thirty-one. Cups, twenty-eight.

"Front of house," Sydney announces, sandal up on the cooler, "we did $61 today, which rounds to $65."

Emily turns the notebook around. $61.25.

Then Sydney doesn't do the next part, which is the victory lap. Instead she stands there watching the morning crowd drift back up toward the parking lot with their kettle corn and their empty coffee mugs, and she says, "We're selling to the wrong people."

We all look at her.

"These are morning people. Morning people already spent their money on coffee before they got here. You know where I was Thursday night? The Levitt. Free show, five thousand people on that lawn, ninety degrees at eight o'clock. The beer line was forty minutes. There was nothing cold between the parking and the grass — nothing. I stood in that crowd and I would have paid five dollars for anything with ice in it. Remember the sunburned guy who paid double without blinking? There are five thousand of him. Every Thursday. Every Friday. Four blocks from here."

Nobody says anything for a second, because she's right, and we're all doing the same math.

Emily does it out loud. "If one person in fifty buys a cup, that's a hundred cups. We can't make a hundred cups in two coolers. We'd need three batches, maybe four, and twice the ice." She's already writing. "I'll have supply numbers by Monday."

Then she flips back a page in the notebook, to a chart she has clearly been keeping privately, and says, in the exact voice she uses for the drive time to Brookings: "Also. I extended our growth line. If the company keeps growing at the current rate, we are all millionaires by Christmas."

Sydney screams. Sofi applauds. I laugh so hard I have to sit on the cooler.

Emily waits for us to finish. "I ran it twice," she says.

That's the thing about Emily's math. It's never wrong. It just doesn't know anything except what we've shown it, and all we've shown it is four Saturdays where every number was bigger than the last one. The line doesn't know that lines end. It's a joke, and everyone treats it like a joke, and I laugh with everyone.

Then I check my phone, where my own line is doing the same thing, and I notice I don't laugh at that one.

There's one problem with Sydney's five thousand customers, and I'm the one who says it: our authorization is for the market. The Levitt is not the market.

"So read the thing again," Sydney says. "That's your whole job. You're the reader."

## 2

Monday, on my break, smelling like a value meal, I read the paragraph again. I practically have it memorized by now, but I read it anyway, because the paragraph has earned that. *Operating under the sponsorship of an already-licensed market or event.* Or event. The Levitt is an event. It's the biggest event in the city twice a week, and it has an office, and the office has a phone number.

The woman who answers listens to my whole pitch — cold agua fresca, permitted at the falls market, we bring our own table — and says the food trucks pack up before the headliner and people complain about it every single week. She says the word "sponsorship" back to me like she's reading it off my own printout. She'll send a letter.

Tuesday we restock at the garage. A little after four, a gray sedan pulls up at the curb — clean, the kind of car that gets washed on a schedule — and my grandmother gets out of the passenger side. The driver leans toward the open window, a man in a golf shirt, and says something that makes her laugh. Then he gives the whole yard a friendly wave, calls out "Ladies," and pulls away.

"That's the gentleman," Sydney says, delighted.

"That's Tuesday," my grandmother says, and goes to get the cash box.

Nobody asks a second question.

The count is longer this week. "$77.25," she announces. "Better." She crosses to the inventory, picks up the lamp, clicks it on, clicks it off.

"This is a $4 lamp."

"It was $3 last week."

"The company grew." She sets it down.

Then Sofi stands up at the card table and takes a folded piece of paper out of her back pocket.

"Before we restock," she says, "I have an invoice."

She unfolds it and reads it to us in her sales-clerk voice. Invoice number one. Use of recipe, weeks one through five: waived. Going forward: two dollars per batch, payable weekly. One vote on company decisions. The recipe remains the property of Sofía Ortiz.

Her mother is there — Sofi asked her to come, which we all assumed was about a ride. Lourdes looks at the invoice, then at her daughter.

"Sofía. In this family you do not bill the people you love."

"I'm not billing the family," Sofi says. "I'm billing the company. That's the whole point of a company."

"She's right," Sydney says. "The company has money now."

"Two dollars a batch pencils," Emily says. "The margin holds."

My grandmother picks up the invoice, reads it top to bottom, and sets it down. "Cheap," she says, which from her is a standing ovation.

Lourdes is still holding the paper. Sofi takes out her pen — Sofi always has a pen — and holds it out. "There's a line for a guardian. I'm sixteen. I checked."

And here is the part I keep thinking about. Lourdes takes the pen, and she does not sign. She reads every line first. Both sides, even though the back is blank. She reads the royalty rate twice, and the sentence about the recipe staying Sofi's property twice, and then she signs and dates it in full, and hands the pen back to her daughter. She objected to the whole idea, and then she read it like a contract worth reading, and I'm pretty sure both of those were the truth.

We all sign under her. My grandmother signs fast and certain, the thirty percent making it official. The company was founded on four handshakes and no paper anywhere on the property, and now our first piece of paper is an invoice from a sixteen-year-old, and honestly that feels right.

I taught Sofi long division at her own kitchen table when she was seven. Now she has me under contract. I tell her I've never been prouder to get billed, and I mean it.

Later, my grandmother has me empty a junk drawer she wants for inventory — rubber bands, dead batteries, a church fan, keys to nothing. There's a pen in there too, a fat glossy one with an orange sunrise printed on the barrel, the kind businesses give away by the box. It writes on the first try, which puts it ahead of every other pen in the drawer. I drop it in the coffee can with the pens and keep sorting.

At 5:40 my grandmother comes out of the house dressed, the earrings that click, and drives off. Sydney opens her mouth. Sofi puts a hand on her arm, and for once, that's the end of it.

Wednesday the Levitt's letter is in my email, and after my shift I take it to the licensing counter, where the same clerk looks up at me.

"You again," she says.

"Me again."

She reads the letter, finds the form in the drawer that doesn't get opened much, and stamps the second authorization. Another ten dollars. The paragraph works exactly the same the second time. I get this one laminated too, and now the company has two laminated documents, which as far as I can tell makes us a chain.

## 3

Thursday, Sofi's kitchen smells like jamaica all afternoon. Three batches — two coolers' worth and every jug the Ortiz kitchen owns. "Six dollars," Sofi says to the pot, pleasantly, and nobody argues, because it's in writing.

We set up at six-thirty on the walkway between the parking and the lawn, the spot where every single person has to pass us twice — once thirsty, once thirstier. I've been picturing that spot since the first Saturday we got turned away. It turns out it wasn't at the market. It was four blocks north the whole time.

By eight the lawn is full. I have never sold anything to a crowd before. The market is browsers; this is a line. An actual line, of strangers, holding money, forming without being asked. On my invented scale, the crowd is a 10 on thirst and a 2 on patience. A man buys four cups and carries them off pressed together in a square. Two girls come back before the encore and buy two more "for the walk to the car." We sell out of the first cooler before the band finishes its first set, refill both from the jugs at intermission, and when the last of the third batch goes, Sydney turns the empty cooler upside down like she's presenting evidence.

Max shows up for teardown because the coolers are heavy, and for once the phone stays in his pocket, because both of his hands are full.

Emily's tally, by flashlight: cups, ninety-two. Take: $184. In one night. Our best Saturday took six hours to make a third of that.

We get home at 11:40 and my mother comes downstairs in her robe. I tell her she doesn't have to. "Money came in," she says, and gets the bands, and we count $184 at midnight at the kitchen table, because the ceremony doesn't check the clock.

Friday there's a bigger act, so we brew four batches. Same spot, longer line. Sold out by nine. Tally: cups, one hundred twenty-three. Take: $246.

"Which rounds to $250," Sydney says.

"$246," Emily says.

Before we load the coolers, Emily counts fourteen dollars into Sofi's hand — seven batches, week one of the contract — and logs it under expenses. Sofi has been quoting prices as long as I've known her. The good-neighbor rate, the family plan, the four-dollar favor. This is the first time in her life I have ever seen her collect one. She folds the bills into her pocket and doesn't say anything about it, but she smiles the whole teardown.

"I updated the projection," Emily says, while we load. "With this week's data, it isn't Christmas anymore. It's Halloween."

Sydney whoops. Sofi takes a bow on behalf of the recipe. I notice that this time I don't laugh, and I notice that on Saturday it was a joke, and that it's amazing what five green days will do to a joke.

Because my line is doing it too. The account was green Monday, green Tuesday, green all week — I check it at red lights now, which is a habit I've decided not to examine. The stand's line and the stock's line, both going up, and Emily's chart says lines like this end at a million dollars, and nobody in the car says otherwise.

At the kitchen table my mother counts the $246, slides it over, and I count it back out loud. Nine minutes, the longest count since the garage sale, and neither of us minds. She bands the twenties, writes $246 on the flap of the grocery envelope, under the other Saturdays, and the column is getting longer, and every number in it is bigger than the one before. Emily's line, in my mother's printing.

"Good crowd?" she asks.

"Five thousand people," I say. "Sydney found them."

She nods, presses the drawer shut, says goodnight.

Upstairs, I check the other number. Green again. $1,652.46 — up $152.46 since the night I pressed the button, for no work at all, while I was busy carrying coolers.

$246, banded.

$1,652.46, unbanded.

Only one of them got counted at a table.
