# FULL PRICE — Ch 14 · The Table

## 1

Sunday at six my grandmother arrives with her purse and a folder, and my mother takes one look at the two of us and sets the serving spoon down.

"All right," she says. "Who died."

"Nobody died," I say. "Can we sit? I need to tell you about my summer. All of it, start to finish, and I need you to let me get to the end before you say anything."

My mother pulls out her chair and sits with her hands folded on the table, in her listening posture, the one she uses when a cashier is about to explain a drawer. The food stays in the middle, untouched, the whole time. Nobody ever does serve it.

I start in June. The email from the school, the week after finals: $7,800, due August 28. I tell her I read it in the car outside the restaurant and decided, before I'd even turned the key, that she was never going to see it — that I'd looked at the drawer in my head, GROCERIES, LIGHTS, CAR, SCHOOL, and decided for both of us that the number was mine to carry. I don't dress it up. I called it protecting her all summer, and I say that too, and then I say what it actually was, which is that I decided my own mother couldn't be told the truth, and I built a whole summer on top of that decision.

Then I give her the summer in order. The second job that didn't exist because the hours didn't exist. The stand. The account with the cartoon rocket — $1,500 of my $1,503.72, moved on a Friday in July, alone in a parking lot, 126 shares at $11.84. The signals group with the green screenshots. The Friday night it was worth $4,292.16 and I stood at JazzFest with my thumb over the sell button and let the crowd take the moment. The Monday I asked the company for a $300 advance, out loud, correctly, and put desperation money into a falling stock after three separate people warned me in three separate ways. The Thursday morning it opened down 80 percent because of a risk I had personally read about on page 61 and decided didn't apply to me.

"There's more," I say, because the worst one is hers. "In July I was short before payday and the stand needed ice, and instead of telling anyone, I took three twenties out of the cash box and didn't say it and didn't write it down. Money moved and nobody counted it. I broke your rule, the milk-crate rule, the only one you ever taught me twice. I paid it back four days later, out loud, in front of everyone, and it's in Emily's book, correction and all. But I did it, and I did it because saying *I'm short* out loud would have started a conversation that ended at this table, and I wasn't willing to end up here."

My mother hasn't moved in ten minutes. When she finally says something, it's a count question, because of course it is.

"What's left?"

"$807.62 in the account. My share of the bill is twenty-eight hundred, and between the fryer checks, my share of the stand, and what's left when I sell the wreckage, I can cover my share myself. That part I get to keep."

"Who else knew?" my mother asks. Second question. She's breaking the family rule with it, and she knows it, and she asks it anyway, looking straight at me.

"Max. Since July. He kept it — even from Sydney." I make myself keep going. "And Grandma. Since Wednesday. She asked me the one question that works, and I told her everything before I told you, and I'm not proud of the order."

"You told the Ellis boy," my grandmother says, to her iced tea, "— all ticker, no tape, that one — before your own mother."

It's meant for me, and it lands on me. But I watch it land somewhere else too. My mother turns and looks at her mother for a long, level moment, the same look she gave me over the hotdish two Sundays ago, and something in the air over the table shifts.

"That's rich, Mama," my mother says quietly. "Coming from you."

She means it generally. She has no idea what she's just done.

## 2

My grandmother sits still for a moment. Then she reaches down, puts Gary's folder on the table where the food should be, and squares it with both hands, and looks at her own knuckles for a second, and does the thing I asked her to do on Friday and honestly wasn't sure I'd live to see.

"Denise. There are things you don't know, and they're mine, and I'm going to say them once, so listen the way the girl asked you to listen."

And she says it. No stories first, no fifteen-cent hamburgers, no walk-up. She leads with the number the way you'd pull off a bandage.

"As of Friday's paper, I'm worth a little over nine hundred thousand dollars."

The kitchen is completely silent. The refrigerator, the clock. My mother doesn't say a word. Her face does something I've never seen it do, which is nothing at all, for three full seconds, while a fifty-year-old building falls over inside her.

"Nine hundred thousand," my grandmother says again, evenly, in the tone she prices lamps with. "Built from line wages, starting in 1965, one folded twenty at a time. The fund in '76. The IRA every year from '82. A thousand dollars of Deere stock in '85 that's eighteen thousand now. The index fund from '89 on, never sold, not once, through every crash they could print. The house, paid off in '98. Nobody helped me and nobody knew — not your father while he lived, not you, not your brother or sister, nobody, for sixty years. That was on purpose. Every day of it was on purpose. Hiding money is the only thing I have ever practiced longer than reading box scores."

She opens the folder and turns it to face my mother.

"And this is a man named Gary, who has been sitting at my kitchen table since February, and who is coming back tomorrow at two with a contract, because the one thing hiding can't survive anymore is being a widow with tenure in a building where salesmen buy the coffee. Keisha read his contract Thursday night. She'll walk you through what's in it; it's her language now. I'm telling you he exists, and that I nearly let him in, because being listened to once a week turned out to be the one price I didn't check."

My mother looks at the folder, then at me — I nod, because that part's mine to confirm — then back at her mother, and asks the exact question I asked Friday, in almost the same words.

"The garage sale. Was that him?"

"It was time anyway," my grandmother says, which is a yes.

I watch my mother take that one. A year of nagging. Her one victory over her mother, the thing she told the neighbor about with her customer-service voice on, *a year of nagging finally worked* — and it was a salesman's line item, and her mother let her wear the win all summer because the win was useful cover. She doesn't say anything about it. She files it, and I can see the file it goes into, and it isn't empty.

"There's one more piece," my grandmother says, "and it's the girl's, so I'll say it plain. Keisha — the five thousand last August wasn't a favor I scraped up. It came out of an account I opened in your name in January of 2009, when you were two years old. Four hundred dollars every January since. The plan was five thousand a year, all four years, and this year's is scheduled — it posts this week, before the due date. It was never in doubt. Not for one day of this summer."

She looks at my mother.

"And so you don't spend tonight doing the wrong arithmetic, Denise: it's not favoritism. The same deal stands for every grandchild in this family who enrolls. Marcus's two, Charlene's three, any of them, the day they register. There are accounts. Keisha's just the first — first in this family, ever, and the account was waiting for her before she could read."

That last part is the only sentence all night she says with the pride showing, and she says it once and puts it away.

"Why," my mother says. Not a full question. It's all she's got.

"Because hard work is the only thing that ever made anything of me," my grandmother says, "and I decided the girl would be made the same way. If she knew the money was certain, the certainty would soften her. So she'd work like it didn't exist, and it would be there anyway, and she'd never owe anybody and never be soft. That was the design." She folds her hands. "I'm not going to argue for it. I'm just telling you it was a design and not neglect."

Nobody argues with it either. Nobody has to. The refutation is sitting at the table in a fryer-scented hoodie with $807.62 to its name: the certainty she hid from me is the exact thing I spent eighteen hundred real dollars trying to manufacture in a parking lot. The lesson built to keep me from being soft is the only reason the tuition was ever in danger at all. Every person at this table can do this math. We all just sit there doing it.

And underneath it, quietly, one more repricing that nobody says out loud: every Christmas of my life, one pair of store-brand crew socks, individually wrapped. Every January of my life, four hundred dollars into an account with my name on it. Same woman. Seventeen years. I sit there holding both of those facts, and the socks will never be funny again.

## 3

My mother stands up.

She doesn't excuse herself. She walks out of the kitchen, and we hear the drawer in the hallway desk, and she comes back with her phone and two envelopes, and she sets all three on the table in a row, in front of her mother's folder, and stays standing.

She turns the phone around. It's the parent portal. Logged in. Her own account, her own password, the balance page: $7,800, due August 28.

"I've known since the first week of June," my mother says. "The school emails parents too, baby. I read it in the break room the day it posted. I asked you one time if you were set for school, and you said *handled,* and I heard exactly what that word was doing, because it's my word. So I didn't ask again. I decided you were never going to feel that bill land on you. A mother is supposed to provide."

She opens the first envelope. It's cash, banded, thick.

"Eleven Sundays. Time and a half, every one the store would give me, since June. Minus what the car took, because the car doesn't care what you're saving for." She lays it flat. "I thought last year was one time too, Mama. I've been building the whole seven-eight alone since June, because I thought there was nobody to split it with. Both of you were at my table all summer and I thought there was nobody."

Then the second envelope, and this one she holds a second longer before she puts it down, and her voice, which has been store-steady through all of it, moves half a step.

"It wasn't going to be enough. The Sundays weren't going to get there. I did the math three weeks ago and every door I checked was shut — no card, no credit, no house — and I'm not sorry about a single one of those doors, I built every one of them myself, because I don't owe anybody a—"

The sentence stops.

Right there, in the middle, the way a machine stops. She's said it my whole life — *I don't owe anybody a dime* — over the drawer, over the checkbook, at the store, it's her amen. And she stands there at her own table with the second envelope in front of her and can't finish it, because sixty seconds ago she found out what owing-nobody cost her mother across fifty years, and what it's been costing her daughter since June, and what it's about to cost her right now, at the end of this sentence.

Nobody finishes it for her.

"So Tuesday," my mother says, instead, "I took the chain to a dealer downtown, and I asked every right question, and I got the offer in writing, and then I sold it in the same visit, because I knew if I walked out wearing it I'd never come back. Nineteen-eighty. It's all there — I haven't touched a dollar. It was for the bill." She puts the receipt on the table on top of the money, and she finally sits down. "I sold Mama's chain Tuesday. That's my summer."

I'm doing arithmetic before I can stop myself, because that's who this family made: her envelopes, plus Grandma's five thousand, plus my twenty-eight hundred. This bill is covered almost three times over. Three women, one number, three complete rescues, sealed off from each other since June — and with my grandmother's five thousand on the calendar since January, my mother's math never needed the chain at all.

It never needed the chain.

My grandmother hasn't moved since the word *chain.* She's looking at the receipt, and her hand has come up to her own collarbone, to nothing.

"Denise," she says. "There's a thing about that chain I never told anybody. Not once, not to anyone, and you're both going to hear it now, because you sold it not knowing, and that's my doing, not yours."

She tells it with her hands flat on the table.

"I bought it in 1984 at an estate sale on the west side. Fifty dollars, cash, from a family that didn't know what they were holding. It was the first thing I ever bought in my life purely for myself. Not the house, not you kids, not the funds. Mine." She takes a breath. "And in the winter of '87 — the strike winter, the crash winter, the one time in my life I ever panicked — I pawned it. Same week I sold the fund at the bottom. The chain and my nerve went to the same counter. I got it back in '89, first spare money I had, walked in with the ticket and walked out with it in my pocket.

"And I never wore it again."

She lets that sit for a second.

"Not once, in thirty-seven years. It was the year I flinched, and every time I put it on, that's what it weighed. So it lived in the drawer. And in June, when it nearly walked off the table in front of me for two dollars, I put it on the one neck in this family where I could see it, and I didn't tell you what you were wearing, because telling you would have meant telling all of it." Her thumb moves once across the receipt. "I never wore it again, and then I gave it to you without the story, and Tuesday you stood at a counter in a bad week and did with it exactly what I did with it in '87. Thirty-eight years apart. Same chain. You didn't repeat my mistake, Denise. You inherited it. There's a difference, and the difference is me."

Nobody says anything for a while. On the table, next to the dealer's receipt for $1,980, the folder is still open to the Deere certificate — the thousand dollars from 1985 that's eighteen thousand now. The two papers just lie there next to each other. Nobody points at them.

It's my mother who finally speaks, and when she does, she doesn't go big. She goes exact, which is how I know it's her deepest register.

"What day does the five thousand post?"

"Wednesday," my grandmother says. "It'll show at the bursar Thursday, Friday at the outside. Two days ahead of the deadline."

My mother nods slowly, taking the answer in, and I realize I am watching her do the thing her entire life was arranged to avoid: ask a money question, out loud, in front of witnesses, and wait, needing the answer, while somebody gives it to her.

"Okay," she says. And then, still quiet, still level, to her mother: "You let me learn everything the hard way, because the hard way was how you learned it. That's the part I'm going to need time with. Not the money. That." She looks down at her own hands. "Thirty-five years I've paid rent, Mama. To this day. You burned your mortgage in '98 and I found out just now, at the same table where I found out everything else. I could have — " she stops herself, does the math anyway, says only the total: "That's a house. Somewhere in all those years, that's a whole house."

She doesn't pursue it. She said it to have it said, and it stays said, one line, priced, done. My grandmother doesn't apologize, because there isn't an apology the size of the thing, and my mother didn't ask for one. They just look at each other across fifty years, and both of them stay at the table, and in this family, staying at the table is the entire language.

Then my grandmother reaches into her purse.

She takes out the manila envelope — soft at the corners, the window gone cloudy, JOHN MORRELL & CO. across the top — the one she confiscated with both hands in June, the one that's ridden in her apron and her purse all summer. She opens the flap, slides out the pay stub, and unfolds the twenty behind it, crease by crease, until it lies flat in the middle of the table, in the middle of all of it — the folder, the envelopes, the receipt.

May of 1965. The first dollar she ever hid.

She smooths it once with her thumb and leaves it there, and doesn't say one word about it, and for once in my life, nobody asks her to.

We sit at that table until almost eleven. At some point the food finally gets served, cold, and eaten anyway, and it's the best meal of the summer by a distance that isn't close.

Tomorrow at two, Gary comes back for his signature.

Every number this family has, said out loud, at one table, on the same night. It only took sixty-one years.

The twenty stays where she put it.
