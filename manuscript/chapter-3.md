# FULL PRICE — Ch 3 · The Leftovers

## 1

At 6:40, Sydney calls for attention.

"Front of house," she announces, one sandal up on the empty lamp table like a podium, "we did $210 today. I'd like to thank the customers, the weather, and everybody who helped out."

Emily turns her notebook around and holds it up. She doesn't say anything. She never has to.

"$208," Sydney amends, without breaking eye contact or pace, "which rounds to $210."

The lawn is down to bones. Six tables folded, two to go, the racks picked to hangers. My mother catches my eye across the yard and tips her head at the storage cabinet — the big oak one, $45 all day, no takers. I peel a sticker. The storage cabinet leaves at 7:00 for $15, in a pickup without my mother's knowledge.

Meanwhile the green crock pot is having the busiest day of anyone here.

Out at 8, with the first wave. Back into the garage at 11, in my grandmother's arms. Out again at 2 — my mother's doing; she hadn't mentioned it to grandma. And now, at 6:45, I watch it cross the driveway a fourth time, riding under a folded moving blanket, my grandmother timing her run for when Sydney's talking, which is not a hard window to hit. Both sides deny holding it.

She fought for every price today like the money insulted her, and now she's carrying the merchandise back to safety a box at a time. She never wanted this sale. So why is there this much of it? I pushed the question to the back of my mind, and make change for a man buying two belts.

"The year of nagging paid off," my mother tells the belt man on his way out. Final tally for the day: twelve sales.

Two church ladies are working the coat rack at the speed of women on a mission. One says her cousin in Brandon gave $900 to a voice on the phone that said it was her grandson. "Tsk," says the other one, and holds up a coat. "Is this wool?"

At 7:15 a man walks up the driveway wheeling the teal bike, with the expression of a man who'd been scammed.

"The chain is broken," he says.

"We disclosed that," Sydney says.

He thinks about it. He was there. She did say those exact words, at the moment of sale. He wheels the bike back down the driveway and rides it home in seconds.

Sofi arrives at 7:30, three doors down, carrying one sweating cup of something dark red with the lime floating in it, and drinks it in front of us. It is ninety-one degrees. We have been outside for ten hours.

"Sofi."

"This is jamaica," she says. "Hibiscus. My recipe."

"Sofi."

"Family rate is $1 a cup," she says, in the voice of a sales clerk reading off a price tag. "First pitcher is promotional, so, free. Terms: you return the cup, and nobody says the word *Kool-Aid*." She waves off the dollar Emily is already holding out and goes home for the pitcher.

The pitcher is half gone when the last browser of the day comes over — a big sunburned man who has just carried a headboard to his truck alone, determined. He looks at the pitcher likes he's just finished a race, beads of sweat forming on his forehead.

"What is that."

"Agua fresca," Sofi says. "Hibiscus."

"I'll give you $2. I've been watching people drink it for ten minutes."

Sofi pours. He drinks the whole cup standing there, and a second one, and puts down $4, and then — moved by something — holds out one more dollar, separate, meaningfully.

"That's a tip," Sofi says, surprised. "We've never gotten a tip before."

 Everyone involved is satisfied.

Sofi sits back down and reports back to us: "Strangers pay double the family rate."

Nobody says anything for a second. Then Emily says Falls Park market runs every Saturday morning through October and has a kettle-corn guy, a bratwurst guy, and not one decent cold drink on the whole walk down to the water. The falls means a bigger market and a bigger market means more potential customers who are thirsty for a cold drink. My thoughts are racing at a hundred miles per hour, thinking about how much profit we could make. 

That's when Max shows up.

"My brother," Sydney says, to the yard, as warning. "He's rich this week."

Max is twenty-two, has one AirPod in, and buys nothing. What he has is a screenshot: a green line going up, a number at the top with a plus sign on it, **+$1,140**. He holds it out to me first, and when I don't take the phone he pivots to the church ladies at the coat rack, who are trapped by politeness, and begins to explain.

The explanation requires both arms. He draws the chart in the air — here's the ground, here's the rocket, here's where the rocket goes, which is up, which is the whole point. The whole sector is about to reprice, he says, because SpaceX is going public soon — *soon*, like it's a date instead of a word — and he is positioned.

"You're in SpaceX?" Emily asks.

"You can't be in SpaceX. It's private. That's the *opportunity*." He taps the air where the chart was. "You get in around it."

The taller church lady leans toward the shorter one, not quietly. "Isn't that the boy who mowed for the Petersens?"

"Rain gutters," the other one says. "It was gutters."

"I did both," Max says, to the air, and the conversation dies right there on the lawn, of natural causes.

I find him annoying, and I notice I've been listening.

The app says what it said this morning. I put my phone face-down on the drawer.

At 8:00 Emily counts the day at the card table, out loud, twice, and bands it: **$846.** She sets the band in the cash box and the cash box on the table, and Max's phone is lying right there next to it, screen up, still green, +$1,140 that nobody mowed a hill for.

Nobody comments. We fold the last table.

 Around 8:30, carrying chairs, I find the last of the ten lamps — the $5 survivor — standing on the cash table. I put it back. When I get home that night it is in our kitchen, on the counter, plugged in, on, and no one in my family acknowledges it.

*— three days —*

## 2

Tuesday I finish the shift at 4:00 and drive to my grandmother's smelling like a value meal, because today was inventory day, and I was the one in charge of organizing it.

This is the plan, agreed Sunday by group chat: the stand sells agua fresca at Falls Park, and it also sells whatever the sale couldn't — the records, the margarine tubs, the clothespins. The founding assets include two coolers, the folding table, the card table, a cash box. And the lamp, which is here, on a cooler, facing me. I didn't load it. Nobody loaded it. Emily has it in the ledger as an asset.

What we don't have is cash to open with. Emily writes down the cost of the ingredients: $84 for ice, cups, sugar, hibiscus, lids, and a float.

My grandmother watches us sort for ten minutes from the garage doorway. Then she comes down into the inventory, hands behind her back, and this is a walk I know — the estate-sale walk, the one she's been practicing every weekend for years. She lifts a cooler lid and finds the crack in the hinge. She spins a record, checking that it still works. She picks up the lamp, clicks it on, clicks it off.

"This is a $2 lamp," she says.

"You priced it at $5 Saturday."

"Saturday was a different situation." She sets it down and looks at the four of us. "I'll give you $100 for thirty percent of the profit," she says.

"Twenty-five," says Sydney, who has watched television.

"Thirty. And I count the box when I visit."

"You'll visit?"

"I'll visit."

We say yes because we were always going to say yes, and she knew it before she came out of the house. Her hand goes into the apron pocket — past a corner of manila, and comes out with five twenties, counted onto the card table out loud, slow. Then she shakes four hands in order of age, her grip landing like a stapler, and the deal is made. $100, thirty percent, no contract beyond the handshake needed.

## Essie, 1976

The salesman's pen was heavier than hers.

He had come to the house twice before and sold nothing at all — just sat at her kitchen table with his coffee going cold and let her talk, all the way to the end of the answer, which no man with papers had ever done. The third Thursday, the papers came inside with him.
A mutual fund. The form said the man kept $8.50 of every hundred dollar of profit, and she asked why, and he said, "That's the load. It's how I get paid." It was the first straight answer a man with papers had ever given her — the cost sitting right there in type, where a person could read it.

She had practiced the signature the night before, on the back of a grocery sack, eleven times. Not because she couldn't write her name. Because she had never once signed it under a printed line with a man watching, and she was not going to learn how in front of him.

Estelle Carter, she wrote, the twelfth time, same as the tenth and the eleventh, and slid the paper back across her own table before he could see her hand shake, and it didn't.

## 3

At 5:40 my grandmother comes out of the house dressed.

Not church dressed. *Dressed* — the good blue blouse, the earrings that click, a purse that matches on purpose. It is Tuesday. The four of us stop sorting cups and stare at her like the crock pot came out a fifth time.

"Miss Estelle," Sydney says, with enormous care. "Is there a *gentleman*."

"The center has a supper," my grandmother says, to her handbag.

"On Tuesdays. A standing supper," Sydney says suspiciously.

My grandmother does not deny the gentleman, which from her is a press release. What she does instead is stop at the garage fridge, where the pop lives and the Canaries schedule hangs, and hold her scorebook up flat against the door like she's serving papers.

I'm in the fridge behind her anyway, raiding the freezer to test the cracked cooler. The schedule is pinned up by a magnet — SUNRISE RETIREMENT SOLUTIONS, a little orange sunrise, the kind of thing that arrives in a mailbox and ends up holding a coupon for eternity. Her ice trays are the old metal kind with the lever. I crack both into the cooler and leave the trays refilled crooked, which she will fix.

I walk her out to the car because my hands are free and hers have the purse. I smell like fast food. She smells like powder and Sunday. She doesn't ask what I need money for so badly that I'm founding companies. I don't ask who at the senior center is worth ironing for. She gets in, keys already in hand, and rolls the window down one inch.

"Count the box before you leave," she says.

"We will."

"Out loud."

"Out loud," I repeat in agreement.

She backs out carefully and is gone to her Tuesday.

We count the box before we leave. All four of us at the card table under the garage light. My mother's rules run this company now; nobody voted, they just apply. Emily counts the five twenties out loud. Then I count them back. Then she bands them — one band, five bills, the band lapping itself twice — and the vault is Emily's pencil box, the tin one, TREASURY on masking tape in her ruler-straight hand. She opens the laptop and says the file name out loud, like a christening: *agua_capital_do_not_lose.*

"You track everybody's money to the dollar," I tell her, "and I have never once seen you open your own app."

"It's fine in there," Emily says, and closes the tin.

Nobody asks a second question. The cooler leaks, exactly as forecasted. We load the hatchback on the whole operation — a folding table, the records, the pitcher recipe, a lamp, and a pencil box holding every dollar the company has.

$100, banded.
