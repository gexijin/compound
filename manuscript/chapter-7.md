# FULL PRICE — Ch 7 · The Walk

## 1

Thursday the Levitt books a band people actually drive in for. Sydney knows the name. Sofi knows the name. I know the parking situation.

We brew five batches — the coolers, the jugs, and a stockpot with foil over it that Sofi's mother lends us on the condition that it comes home tonight. It isn't enough. The line starts before the opener and never gets shorter than ten people, and for three hours I do nothing but pour, make change, and say "next." Somewhere in the middle of it the crowd sings a whole chorus without the band, five thousand people at once, and we sell right through it.

We're dry before the encore. Sydney flips the last cooler upside down on the table like a closed sign, and people keep walking up anyway, and she starts taking orders for tomorrow like we're a restaurant with a reservation book.

Teardown ends a little before midnight. Sofi's cousin picks her up at the curb, stockpot in her lap, foil catching the streetlight. Sydney takes Emily home. Sydney starts to announce the front-of-house number on her way to the car, and Emily says, "I'll have it tomorrow," and closes the notebook, because it's midnight, and even the count is asleep.

Then it's me and Max and the loaded Vibe, the same as every teardown. The cash box is locked in the back under his hoodie. He has the keys in his hand.

He doesn't open the car.

"You ever seen the falls at night?" he says.

"I see them every Saturday."

"At dawn. That's morning. That's a different river."

It's four blocks. We walk.

## 2

I know this park by one light only. At dawn it belongs to the vendors and the joggers and one particular bird, and everybody is there to do something. At midnight it's empty and loud. The falls don't care what time it is. The floodlights are on the water, and the spray drifts sideways through the light, and there is nobody here — no market, no lawn chairs, no church ladies, nobody who knows either of our families. Everyone who has watched us all summer is asleep. I notice that, and I notice that I notice it.

Max checks his phone once on the walk down, out of habit, and whatever he sees makes him happy, and he opens his mouth, and I know exactly what's coming because we've run this argument since June.

"The coin—"

"The coin doesn't publish," I say, and it comes out without any push behind it, like the tenth time through a song. "There's nothing to—"

I don't finish it. He doesn't finish his either. The falls are loud and it's midnight and neither of us actually wants the argument, we just know the words. He puts the phone away without being asked, which might be a first.

We end up at the railing above the third ledge. The water goes over it white in the floodlight, and I point at the flat shallow spot below it.

"That's his spot," I say.

"Whose spot?"

So I tell him. I have never told anybody this all the way through, because out loud it makes no sense, and I tell him anyway. There's a great blue heron that fishes that exact spot at dawn, and I have been trying to photograph him for three summers with a phone that takes a second too long to focus. Three summers of clear, well-lit pictures of water where a heron just was. There's a retired man with a fishing vest and three thousand dollars of lens on a tripod who got the shot on a Thursday, feather detail, an eye ring, and showed me the back of his camera and said "You'll get there" — to my phone, not to me. He shows everyone. The honey woman has seen his pelicans four times.

"So where's the bird now?" Max asks, looking at the empty water.

"Asleep somewhere. Herons don't work nights."

"Smarter than us."

"I want the picture on my phone," I say. "That's the part that makes no sense. I could save up for a lens. That's not it. I want it with what I've got, from the railing, on a morning I was there anyway. Then I want to walk over and show him the back of my phone."

"And then what?"

"Nothing. Then nothing. It isn't for anything."

Max is quiet for a second. He doesn't rate it. He doesn't tell me the heron is pre-revenue. He listens to the whole thing, all the way to the end of the answer, and then he says:

"That's the first thing you've ever told me that didn't have a number in it."

I don't have anything to say to that, so I don't say anything, and we stand there and watch the water come over the ledge, over and over, the same and not the same. His hand is on the railing next to mine. Then his little finger is against mine, barely, the weight of nothing.

I don't move my hand.

Nobody says anything about it. Nobody kisses anybody. There's no crowd, no line, no witnesses, nothing to announce and no one to announce it to, and we stand at the railing until the sprinklers come on somewhere up the hill behind us and make us both jump, and then we laugh, and then we walk back to the car.

He drives me home with the windows down. The kitchen light is on. My mother is up anyway — Thursdays run late now, she's adjusted, the ceremony doesn't check the clock — and we count the box at the table, out loud, twice, and she bands it and writes the number on the envelope flap in her store printing.

By the time I'm upstairs I couldn't tell you what the number was.

That has never happened before in my life.

## Denise, Week 6

Sunday, the store got Denise at seven.

By nine she had solved the day's first problem: two baggers out, one with a doctor's note and one with a lie, and the schedule rebuilt in the margin in her printing before the rush, nobody double-shifted, nobody owed. The produce manager and the dairy manager were not speaking again, over a display neither of them would be able to describe in a week, and she routed the stock run around the two of them the way you route around a closed lane.

At two, register three came up short. It was always register three. The new cashier was close to tears, and Denise sent her on break, pulled the tape, and ran her thumb down it line by line until she found it — a twenty keyed as a ten, change paid out to match, eleven dollars and change gone into somebody's pocket by honest arithmetic. She initialed the tape, made the note, told the girl it was a keying error and not a character flaw, and had the drawer balanced before the shift change. Thirty-five years will teach you a register tape the way other people know a song.

At four, the schedule book came around for the end of August. Somebody needed the last Saturday covered.

"No," Denise said.

She didn't explain. On the store calendar that Saturday had been blocked off in her printing since June — OFF, boxed twice — and everybody knew better than to ask twice, and nobody knew why. She took the Sunday after the schedule book instead, the close on top of her day shift, time and a half, because it was there.

The Sunday money was in the kitchen drawer, behind GROCERIES and LIGHTS and CAR and SCHOOL, in the envelope with nothing written on it. The envelope had gotten thick enough that this week she had put a rubber band around it, and when she pressed it flat with her thumb she let herself think, for exactly one second, that it was starting to be the kind of money that could matter. Not enough. Starting.

She clocked out at 9:40, walked the lot the way she always did — last one out, checking the carts — and got in the car.

The key turned and nothing happened. The dash lit up. The engine didn't. Click, and the second time, click, and the third time she didn't try, because three is when trying becomes hoping.

The lot was empty except for her sedan and the cart corral and the lights on their timers. She sat for a moment with her hands in her lap. Then she got out, came around the front, and opened the hood, because looking was free.
