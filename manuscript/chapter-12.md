# FULL PRICE — Ch 12 · The Papers

## Denise, Week 10

The dealer's shop was downtown, between a title company and a sandwich place, and Denise had picked it the way she picked everything: by asking around until three separate people gave the same name.

She went in on her Tuesday off, meaning only to learn. She said so at the counter. "I'm not selling today. I want to know what it's worth."

The dealer was a heavyset man with a jeweler's loupe on a cord, and he treated the question as completely normal. He weighed the chain, tested it twice, and laid it on a velvet pad while he worked the numbers on a calculator she could see, which she noted and approved of. She asked what gold was trading at today, and he told her. She asked how his number was figured from that number, and he told her that too — the percentage, straight out, no offense taken. She asked what happened to chains like this one after he bought them.

"Heavy rope like this goes to melt," he said. "Usually within the week. Nobody retails a chain this heavy anymore."

She asked for the offer in writing. He wrote it on a card with the date: $1,980.

And there in the shop, holding the card, Denise did the arithmetic she had already done four times at her kitchen table, one more time, with this new number in it. The envelope, plus the last two Sundays, plus $1,980. For the first time since June, the total came out even.

She had planned to take the card home and think. She knew, standing there, exactly what would happen if she did: she would put the chain back on, and it would be at her collarbone tomorrow, and the week after, and on the due date. She knew herself too well for that.

"I'll sell today after all," she said.

He counted the money out loud in front of her, which she also approved of. She hesitated exactly once — when he lifted the chain off the velvet and it hung in the air for a second, catching the light the way it had at her mother's garage sale — and then the money was in the bank envelope, and the receipt was in her purse, in the zip pocket, with the card.

The proceeds were for the bill. Not for the light bill, not for groceries, not for anything that came up. She would put the envelope with the other envelope and not touch either one, and on the due date her daughter's school would be paid, and that would be that.

Outside on the sidewalk, in the sun, her hand went up once to her collarbone, out of a habit two months old. Then it came down, and she walked to the car.

## 1

Wednesday after my shift I drive to my grandmother's, for no reason I say out loud. There are leftovers to sort. There are always leftovers to sort; that's what makes it a place you can go without a reason.

We work for a while in the garage, her at the workbench, me at the boxes. I price a stack of records I already priced in June. The lamp is sitting on the shelf between us, and I walk past it twice.

"You've stopped grading things," my grandmother says.

I look up. She's not looking at me; she's wrapping a candy dish in newspaper.

"The lamp's been a $5 lamp for two weeks," she says. "You walked past it twice and didn't say a thing. The Ellis boy used to be everywhere and now he's nowhere. And you keep turning that phone over and not opening it. You've done it four times since you got here."

"I'm fine," I say. "Long week."

She finishes wrapping the dish. She sets it in the box. And then she doesn't leave the room, doesn't pick up the next thing, doesn't let the silence do its usual work of moving us along. She pulls the second stool over to the workbench with her foot, sits down across from me, and asks the question that nobody in this family has ever asked anybody.

"How much did you lose?"

I have a ready answer for every question people actually ask. *Are you okay* — fine. *What's wrong* — nothing. *Is it a boy* — nope. Nobody has ever skipped all of those and asked me for the number. The number is the one thing I know cold.

"Eighteen hundred," I say, and once the first number is out, the rest come with it. The bill: $7,800, due next week, which Mom doesn't know I know. The savings: $1,503.72 on the day of the garage sale, checked between customers all day even though checking changed nothing. The account with the cartoon rocket, $1,500 in, 126 shares at $11.84. The sixty dollars I took out of the stand's box without telling anyone, and paid back with an audience. The three hundred I borrowed against my own August, done properly, at the table, and lost anyway. The Friday it was $4,292.16 and my thumb was over the button. The Thursday it was $807.62 by lunch.

I say every number I have spent the summer not saying at my mother's table, in my grandmother's garage, to my grandmother's face. She doesn't move the whole time. Her hands stay folded on the workbench, and she listens all the way to the end of the answer.

"I haven't told her," I say at the end. "Mom doesn't know any of it."

I have now told two people the truth about my summer. Neither of them is my mother.

My grandmother is quiet for a moment. She doesn't tell me it will be okay. She doesn't tell me what I should have done, which I appreciate, because I have a complete list already and hers would match it. What she says, finally, looking at the workbench, is:

"I lost a summer's worth once."

## Essie, 1987

The strike had started in the spring, and by October the checks had been gone so long that the union hall had stopped saying *soon.*

Estelle fed three children out of a chest freezer she had spent two years filling, and she knew to the pound how much was left in it and how many weeks it had to last. Charlene was nineteen and had a bus ticket to Atlanta she hadn't mentioned yet; Estelle had found the receipt doing laundry and put it back where she found it. At her collarbone, under her coat on the picket line, she wore the gold rope chain she'd bought three years back at an estate sale on the west side — fifty dollars, cash, from a family selling off a dead man's things without knowing what they were holding. The first thing she had ever bought purely for herself.

On the third Monday in October, the radio said the stock market had fallen further in one day than it had ever fallen in the history of the country. The men on the radio used the word *crash* and then argued about whether to use it, and used it again.

She did not sleep that night, or the next.

On Thursday she walked to the phone at the end of the hall and called the fund office, the account she had signed for at her own kitchen table eleven years before, the one that had grown quietly through every year since without her ever touching it. The man who answered was young and tired; it had been a long week to work at a fund office. She told him to sell it. All of it. He asked if she was certain, given that prices were — he paused — *depressed.* She was holding a grocery list in her other hand with three items crossed off it, and a strike into its seventh month, and a daughter with a secret bus ticket.

"Sell it," she said.

The confirmation came in the mail eight days later, one page, her mistake recorded in type: the number of shares, the price on the worst possible week, the check enclosed.

The Deere certificate she also tried. That one was paper — an actual engraved certificate in the drawer, bought in '85, at the bottom of the farm crisis. The man on that phone said to sign it, mail it in, and allow three to four weeks for processing. Three to four weeks. A panic doesn't have three to four weeks. She hung up, and the certificate stayed in the drawer, too slow to lose.

When the check cleared, she paid down the winter, and she took the fund confirmation and filed it in the drawer, flat, next to the certificate — the mistake she'd made filed next to the one she'd been too slow to make.

Then she went back to the picket line.

## 2

"I lost everything I'd built for myself in nineteen eighty-seven," my grandmother says. "Sold in a panic, with a strike on. It's the one time I flinched. I have the paper to prove it, though I'm not sure why I kept it." She looks at me. "You messed up at nineteen. I messed up at forty. You're overachieving," she jokes.

That's the whole speech. That's all the comfort on offer, and it's strangely enough.

Then she says: "Now. The school money. You've been running your math with a hole in it, so sit there."

She goes into the house. I sit there. She comes back with a short stack of papers — not from the boxes, not from a hiding place I could ever have found; from wherever she keeps the things she's actually using — and sets the stack on the workbench and takes the top sheet off it and puts it in front of me.

It's a statement for an education account. My name is on it. *Keisha Carter, beneficiary.* The date it was opened is January 2009, when I was two years old.

"Last August wasn't a favor," my grandmother says. "It was the first payment of four. This year's five thousand is already scheduled. It goes to the bursar before your due date, same as it did last year, same as it will the next two years. It was never a miracle, and I was never going to let you find out, and both of those were decisions I made on purpose, and one of them was wrong."

I double and triple check my arithmetic, even though I know what the numbers come out to. $7,800 minus $5,000 is $2,800.

$2,800 was my share. The whole time. My savings alone — the $1,503.72 I torched — plus half a summer of fryer checks would have covered it with room. The problem I spent the summer solving was never as big as I believed it was, and the way I chose to solve it destroyed the exact money that would have solved it.

The relief and the sickness arrive at the same time, and I sit there holding both.

While I sit with that, I'm looking at the rest of the stack, because it's in front of me and demanding my attention. There's a dividend statement from some holding — a real number, but not a scary one; a few fryer shifts, four times a year, the kind of money that's nice to get in the mail. There's an old stock certificate, actual engraved paper with a farm company's crest — the thing from the drawer in the story she just told me, kept all these years, maybe just for decoration now. And there's a glossy folder, orange sunrise on the cover, SUNRISE RETIREMENT SOLUTIONS, with a business card clipped to it — *Gary Tollefson, Retirement Specialist* — and a little printed calendar card where a date is circled twice in blue ballpoint: Monday, August 24.

I do some more math I'm not asked to do, because that's who I am. A dividend here, a certificate there, whatever the sale-that-never-needed-to-happen was clearing space for, plus four years of five thousand promised like it's nothing. Sixty thousand dollars, I think. Maybe eighty. My grandmother, the sock lady, is quietly sitting on more money than anyone in this family has ever had, and nobody knows.

She watches my eyes travel, and when they reach the folder she squares the stack and takes it back, all of it except my statement, the way she took the pay envelope out of the crowd in June. Both hands.

"It's your account, you can ask me anything," she says. "The rest is mine to handle."

"The folder—"

"Gary. From the center. He's been helping me simplify." She says it evenly, no weight on it at all. "He comes Thursdays at two."

"I want to sit in."

My grandmother looks at me for a long moment. She doesn't say yes. She doesn't say no. What she says is, "The garage needs sorting either way," and takes the papers inside.

$2,800. That was my share the whole time.

## 3

Thursday I'm at the garage by one, sorting things that don't need me, and at two o'clock exactly the gray sedan pulls up and the man in the golf shirt gets out with nothing in his hands but a folder.

"Miss Estelle," he says, and then, to me, just as warm, "and you must be Keisha. The engineer. SDSU, up in Brookings — your grandmother mentioned. Bridges, was it?"

It was. He knows my major. He's never met me, and he knows my major, and I feel surprisingly exposed and vulernable from that simple fact.

They sit at the kitchen table and I get lemonade nobody drinks, and I stay, and nobody makes me leave.

Here is what happens for the next forty minutes: my grandmother talks, and Gary listens.

That's it. That's the whole event. He asks her about the business pages at the library — fifty years, print only, Thursdays — and when she answers, he asks the second question. What the paper cost then. What she thinks of the paper now. Whether the columnists were better when they were meaner. She tells him about 1965, the pay line, the envelope, the fifteen-cent hamburger; she tells him the skillet story and the two-letters-to-the-Canaries story, and he laughs in the right places, not too much, and asks the third question, and I sit there with my lemonade and count.

The second question. The third. In this family we don't ask the second question; I could write the rule on the fridge. This man asks the fourth. And my grandmother — who answers me in single sentences, who talks prices all day and positions never — talks more at this table in forty minutes than I've heard in a year, and she looks lighter doing it. Younger. She is having a wonderful time. That's the part that scares me.

She never names a single thing she owns. He never asks. That's the part I watch closest, once I start watching: he never pushes toward the money, not once, not even gently. He doesn't need to. He keeps her talking, and he waits.

And while he nods, the room assembles itself around him. The Canaries schedule on the garage fridge, held up since June by a magnet with an orange sunrise on it. The fat glossy pen I filed in the coffee can without reading, orange sunrise on the barrel. The clean gray sedan that dropped her home from the center, the pleasant word through the window, *Ladies.* Verna Iverson on the church sidewalk, happier than anyone has ever looked talking about money: *he comes to me, he sits at my kitchen table, he asks about Daniel by name, he handled the fee business.* Gary. This Gary. That Gary. One Gary, working an entire zip code of women whose families never ask the second question, sitting at my grandmother's kitchen table across from the proudest and loneliest person I know, listening like it's his job.

Because it's his job.

He leaves at a quarter to three, on time, no rush about anything, and he leaves the folder — "all the boring stuff's in there whenever you want it, no hurry at all" — and shakes my hand on the way out and wishes me a good sophomore year, and it isn't until his sedan is around the corner that I realize he never once mentioned Monday. He didn't have to. The circle on the calendar card mentions it for him, twice, in his own blue ink.

"Can I take the folder tonight," I say. Not really a question.

My grandmother looks at me the way she looked at me over the workbench yesterday. Whatever got decided between us this week, it holds.

"Bring it back tomorrow," she says.

I read it at the kitchen table at home while my mother sleeps, the way I read the ordinance, the way I read the annual report: cover to cover, definitions first, pen in hand. It's forty-one pages. The signature page is blank — nothing signed, nothing initialed, nowhere, I check that first. Page one is a welcome letter with a sunrise on it. Page two starts the definitions, and I can already tell that the definitions are where this contract keeps its real rules.

I read until two in the morning, and I am not done, but I am far enough in to know one thing on my own authority.

Whatever this is, it wasn't written to be read. Neither was the ordinance.

Reading is my advantage.
