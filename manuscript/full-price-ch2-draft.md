# FULL PRICE — Chapter 2 (draft)

## 2

The 1 o'clock crowd shops different. The morning people came to hunt; the afternoon people came because the sign was well-placed — Emily's angles, working — and they buy like it's a favor to somebody. Sydney adjusts accordingly. I watch her move a set of TV trays by informing a man that "hosting is back," a sentence with no verifiable content, and the man walks his trays to the car fast, head down, the way you leave a casino, because my grandmother is watching him from the Pyrex table with the expression of a woman memorizing a license plate. He's not safe till the door shuts. Sydney waves at his bumper. Neither combatant looks at the other. The war remains unfought and both sides remain undefeated.

At 1:20 my grandmother announces she'll be a minute, to no one, the way captains announce things, and goes inside. This matters because of what I did Thursday night.

Thursday night I priced the costume jewelry. One shoebox, everything $2 — clip-on earrings, a brooch shaped like a pheasant, beads in colors that got discontinued for a reason, and one big gold-tone rope chain, heavy as a padlock. I remember the chain because I priced it *with confidence*. Real gold doesn't live in a shoebox. Real gold doesn't weigh a pound. My grandmother worked a trim line for 48 years and drives a Buick older than me; the chain is costume, the box is $2, next item. Logic so clean you could teach it.

So of course at 1:25, with the captain below decks, a woman in a sunflower blouse fishes the chain out of the shoebox, says "this is *fun*," and hands me $2. I make change from her five. I thank her. She walks off down the block on foot, swinging the bag, and I serve two more customers, feeling productive.

My grandmother comes back out at 1:31. I know it's 1:31 because what happens next has a timestamp in my head forever.

She doesn't browse her own tables. She *reads* them — one pass, left to right, like a teller counting a drawer — and stops on the shoebox. From 15 feet away. A shoebox of tangled beads, and she stops on it like it beeped.

"Who bought out of this box."

"A lady? The big necklace — it was marked, everything's $2—"

"Which way."

I point. And my grandmother — 78 years old, in church flats, in June heat thick enough to chew — *goes*.

I want to describe this properly, because nobody will ever believe me. She doesn't jog. She drops into a walk I have never seen a human being use, something between race-walking and prophecy, elbows working, absolutely level, gaining on that sunflower blouse like the woman owes her rent. Sydney and I take off after her and we are two healthy teenagers and we do not close the gap for three blocks. As a chase I rate it a 2 on speed and a 10 on inevitability. The blouse never had a chance. Nobody on that street had a chance.

She runs the woman down in front of the Hansens' hedge, four blocks out. By the time we catch up, the transaction is already over: my grandmother is pressing $2 into the woman's hand and lifting the chain out of the bag with the other, saying, "Marked wrong. Not for sale" — second time today for that sentence; at this rate it's the sale's slogan — and the woman is nodding the way you nod at weather. As we walk away she calls after us, to me, genuinely concerned: "Is she all right?"

"She's winning," I say.

The walk back is four blocks of Sydney begging, and half a block from home my grandmother hands her the chain the way you'd hand someone your coat. Sydney weighs it in her palm, retail-floor eye, half a second.

"Whoa. Okay. This is *real*." She bounces it once, twice, like a scale settling. "This is 3, maybe 4 thousand dollars of chain."

I sold it for $2. Me. My thumb wrote that sticker. I stand there recalculating my entire Thursday while Sydney holds 4 thousand dollars at arm's length like it might bounce, and my grandmother takes it back without breaking stride, walks it up the driveway, and puts it in my mother's hands.

"Somewhere I can see it," she says. And then, quieter, already turning away: "Should've given you this when you made supervisor."

My mother stands there with a price gun in one hand and a fortune in the other. "Mama. This is — this is *real*?"

"Sydney says so," says my grandmother, and goes back to the Pyrex, where a man has been waiting to be discouraged.

My mother puts it on. She has to set the price gun down to work the clasp, and Sydney has to help her get it seated, and then it just — sits there, on my mother, gleaming like a lottery ticket, while she goes back to straightening coat hangers as if her neck isn't wearing a used car. Every few minutes I watch her hand go up and touch it, quick, the way you check a pocket.

At 2 the drawer gets heavy and she calls the drop. This is ours — mine and hers. We clear a corner of the card table, and she splits the stack and we count. I don't know how to explain what this is like except that it's the one thing we do at the same speed. Twenties to her, she counts, I count her count; fives and ones to me, banded, called, confirmed; her mouth moving on the numbers, mine half a beat behind like harmony. Six years on register 3 and a childhood on a milk crate, and neither of us has ever miscounted against the other, not once. The chain swings forward off her collarbone when she leans over the twenties. She counts right through it.

"$381," she says.

"$381," I say. Best conversation we've had all week. I'm not even joking; that's not a joke.

Then it's 2:30, and the rush hits — post-lunch, post-nap, everybody's cousin — and I'm three customers deep when my phone starts going off in my apron like a smoke alarm.

The bridge team chat. Fourteen messages and climbing. Fall schedule dropped. Somebody's posted the regionals banner photo again, captioned **DEFENDING CHAMPS** — we came in first at regionals and 23rd at nationals, and the chat has voted to remember one of those numbers. Practice nights are being polled. Roles are being penciled in, and there I am, penciled: rules compliance, connection design, *obviously*, with a little crown emoji. Nobody's asking if I'll be there. Why would they ask. The season is a fact, the team is a fact, I'm a fact.

I should say something about the fall. Here is the thing about the fall.

The bill is $7,800. It posted to the bursar portal in May, due August 21, and under the number, in plain type, the part I have memorized: *unpaid balances are dropped from registration on the due date; housing assignments are released.* Dropped and released — like it's a kindness, like the university is setting you gently back down. I have $1,500. That's the number I've been checking all day, in case it learned to compound on its own: $1,500, saved off spring shifts, sitting in checking like a good dog. Last summer my grandmother paid $5,000 — appeared out of nowhere, an envelope of a year's church dresses and I don't know what else, and nobody explained it, and you don't ask a woman like that to explain a miracle twice. That money had the shape of *once*. The gap between what I have and what August wants is $6,300, and every plan I have for this summer — the fryer, and whatever else I can build by then — is a plan for closing it myself, because that's the whole point, that's the entire point, the first one to college doesn't come home with a bill.

My mother is ten feet away, re-taping the corner of Emily's sign where the wind got it. Ten feet. One sentence. *Mom, the fall costs $7,800.* Seven words; I could say them in two seconds; she would stop what she's doing and she would fix it, because she fixes things, that's what supervisors do — she would fix it out of a kitchen drawer where the envelopes say GROCERIES and LIGHTS and CAR in her handwriting, and every one of them is already spoken for down to the corners, and she'd do it without blinking and eat the blink later, in private, for years.

She smooths the tape flat with her thumb, twice, the way she does everything.

I lock the screen.

I don't answer the chat, either — they don't need me till August, and by August this is handled. That's the plan. I'm the plan.

"That's $4.50," I tell the man holding the casserole dish, and fan his change out loud so we both can count it.

## Denise, June

$7,800.

The number had come to her on a Tuesday, the first week of June, ten o'clock at night at her own kitchen table — an email from the university, *a statement is available for your student*, and a link that wanted a login she didn't have. It took her 40 minutes to build the account, the laptop fan whirring like it disapproved, and then the portal opened and there it was, itemized, due August 21.

She had thought the $5,000 was a one-time thing. Her mother had never said otherwise; her mother had never said anything at all, just paid it, the way weather happens. So: $7,800, and a daughter who hadn't mentioned it, which meant her daughter had a plan or her daughter had a problem, and there was one way to find out that didn't call anybody a liar.

She'd made the call the next evening, kept it light, landed it the way you set a dish down. *You set for school, baby?*

*Handled*, Keisha said.

And Denise let it stand, because that was the rule older than both of them: you got one question. The second question was an accusation.

Since then she had taken every Friday shift nobody wanted — four so far, the closing ones, the ones that eat the weekend — and said yes so easily the schedule manager stopped apologizing by July. The overage went where overages went, unlabeled, growing. And on the store calendar in the back office, in the red marker nobody was allowed to schedule against, she had blocked off August 22: *K MOVE-IN.* She hadn't told Keisha that. It wasn't a secret; it just wasn't said.

Now she pressed the tape down flat on the corner of the sign, twice, and glanced across the driveway at her daughter making change with her head down, phone already away — a good worker, that girl, always had been, didn't owe anybody a dime, same as her mother.

The chain sat new and heavy at her collarbone. Her hand went up to it, once.

The thought that came with the weight, she put where she kept thoughts.
