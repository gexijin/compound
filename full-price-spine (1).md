# Full Price — Story Spine

**The book.** An adult book-club novel, funny and fast, that quietly educates and encourages young women to handle money. Keisha Carter, 19, first in her family to enroll in college, spends one Sioux Falls summer trying to close a $7,800 tuition gap entirely alone — while her mother and grandmother, unknown to her and to each other, are quietly solving the same problem. Three rescues, one number, a season of silence that bought nothing. The family disease is not lying; it is solitary rescue — loving people by carrying things alone — and the climax is three women saying their numbers out loud at one kitchen table.

**Voice.** First-person present tense, Keisha, no hindsight — funny, specific, evasive; panic converted into systems. Comps: *Margo's Got Money Troubles* for voice, *Come and Get It* for money as texture, *Black Cake* for the matriarch's history in installments — on a thriller chassis: short chapters, hard outs, a reader who always holds one card the heroine doesn't. Two strands in close-third past tense: the **Essie strand** (Estelle's memories, each triggered by a present-day object Keisha just touched) and the **Denise strand** (three short pages revealing the second parallel rescue).

## Cast

**Keisha Carter, 19 — narrator.** Black; near-north Sioux Falls; civil engineering at SDSU — bridges, a fascination entirely and comically hers. Turns panic into systems; reads boring documents better than anyone, which saves the stand and then the family. Carries the $7,800 alone as a vow, not a lie: she will not make her mother re-deal the kitchen-drawer envelopes. After the crash she blames Max to avoid reading her own thumb. Arc: from carrying the number alone to saying it out loud.

**Estelle Carter, 78 — grandmother, hidden investor, collector.** Black; eighth grade, then the Morrell packing plant; widowed young; refused by banks; taught herself investing from library books and never felt entitled to say a word of it. Worth $900,000 the slow way, secret even from her daughter. A collector: fifty years of thrift finds narrated by what she paid, never what they're worth; never sells what she holds; never held a garage sale until page one. Never let the grandkids call her Grandma. Arc: from hiding as safety to naming the fortune out loud. Understood, not absolved; does not apologize well.

**Denise Carter, ~52 — mother.** Black; grocery shift supervisor; excellent with every dollar she can hold, defeated by anything with a login — a first-generation college parent's condition, never a punchline. Read the $7,800 on the parent portal in June and said nothing, because her daughter so clearly wanted to carry it; runs her own quiet rescue on Friday shifts and, when those fall short, her father's watch. Her lifelong adaptation is not-asking; her arc pays off in one out-loud question.

**Sofía "Sofi" Ortiz, 16 — the watched one.** Mexican-American; the neighbor girl, family in everything but blood. Her recipe starts the stand; she invoices it and wins a real royalty and a vote. Wants her learner's permit. The final image is hers: the next watcher.

**Lourdes Ortiz, ~40s — counter-wisdom.** Sofi's mother; cash, caution, receipts. Fights the invoice over love's terms, then reads every line before signing as guardian. At JazzFest she is the one who grabbed the cash box in the crush.

**Sydney Ellis, 21 — chaos engine.** White; spends out loud because pleasure feels like proof. Discovers the Levitt concert crowds — the stand's real value. Warns Keisha off the bet mid-winning-streak. Sells her concert ticket in public to clear her card: the dress rehearsal for costs said out loud.

**Max Ellis, 24 — fast-money foil, light love interest.** White; Sydney's brother; warehouse job he hates; finance-bro charm. Becomes Keisha's pressure valve — the safe confession that makes her silence sustainable. Teaches her to open her own account and mirror his trades; crashes on the page; crawls back to the warehouse; doesn't really learn — a new conviction ticker by the coda. His apology is bad; she forgives him anyway. Never touches the family plot.

**Emily Larsen, 18 — math brain.** White; a kind, money-silent house. Runs the ledger, builds the three-column comparison of everyone's ways of earning, and quietly opens the bank account she'd been afraid of, weeks before anyone notices.

**Gary — the advisor.** Retirement-products salesman circling Estelle: patient, pleasant, remembers her late husband's name — the first person in fifty years to let her talk about money. His contract has teeth and a countdown, and the garage-sale idea was his ("simplify your estate"). Defeated by being read, not confronted.

**The concessionaire.** Friendly food-truck owner who offers $600 for the stand nine days after an anonymous complaint nobody can pin on him. Shrugs: "that's business." Stays liked; hints at the good corner for next June.

## The spine — five acts

### Act 1 — The Garage

Denise finally wins the decade-long clutter argument: Estelle's first-ever garage sale is a week out, and the girls are conscripted to sort the garage while Estelle narrates the provenance of every find. Keisha finds the 1965 pay envelope — stub and a folded twenty — and pockets the twenty a half-second before Estelle takes the envelope back: "that's not for sale." → **Essie: 1965.** That night, the memory: seventeen, count and divide, one twenty folded back in, her first hidden keep — then the present: she counts, it's light, she says nothing. The reader knows she knows from the first interstitial. Mid-prep, Sofi carries over a pitcher of her family's agua fresca and the stand is born in one collective gulp. Max turns up at the sale, hauling nothing, flashing one green screenshot. The bursar bill lands — $7,800, due late August — and Keisha's vow forms: she carries it alone. → **Denise strand 1:** the parent-portal email, read in June, answered with silence and the Friday shifts nobody wants. Estelle stakes the stand — $30 for 30% — and the stolen twenty covers the stall fee. **Act out:** the deal signed, three rescues running blind.

### Act 2 — The Stand

First Saturday: turned away at setup — nobody knew there was a vendor permit, and processing takes three weeks. The ice melts in the lot; total defeat. Keisha spends the week inside the municipal ordinance, finds the path, and gets them legal before the next weekend — her first institution saying yes because she asked precisely. Walking home from the win, Denise opens her mouth about the portal email; "everything's handled, Mom" arrives one beat first. The legal debut still flops — ten customers, church ladies counting her weeks to move-in — then Sydney finds the Levitt crowds and the stand takes off. Keisha catches Estelle buying back a piece from her own sale at a markup: the woman cannot let go of what she holds. In Max's keep-showing-up pattern, Keisha tells him the number — not trust, safety — and the pressure valve opens. The permit win is what flips her on his offer: the market looks like one more system with rules. Two phones at a teardown table, flirtation as curriculum: her own account, her thumb, mirroring his trades. **Act out:** the account opens; the boom begins.

### Act 3 — The Boom

The concessionaire's $600 offer; the complaint-to-offer timing noticed but never proven; a paper-slip vote runs 3–1 against selling, and nobody knows whose slip said *sell* — Keisha does. Sofi invoices the recipe; Lourdes argues love's terms in front of everyone, then reads every line as guardian; the amendment gives Sofi royalty and vote. → **Essie: 1976.** Triggered by three girls amending a contract: the fund slip, the patient salesman, the practiced signature. The housing deposit springs the liquidity trap — her savings are in the market, climbing, untouchable — so wages cover the deposit and a $60 borrow from the stand float covers the ice supplier. On a paperwork errand Keisha files, unread, an envelope from the same institution that credited her portal last year. → **Essie: 2009.** The January ritual: $400 into an account bearing a two-year-old's name. The reader now holds the answer Keisha just handled. JazzFest: record night, Denise's one visit and one full-price cup, the position peaked high enough that the gap suddenly looks reachable — Keisha is reaching for sell when the cash box vanishes in the crush. Suspicion slides toward the wrong kid; Keisha forces an honest audit by confessing the $60 first, publicly. Then Lourdes appears with the box: the theft was never real, the confession is irreversible, and the sell window is gone. She rides. The quiet centerpiece follows: three women at one supper, hotdish and weather talk, the reader holding all three cards while Keisha checks a green number under the table. → **Denise strand 2:** the shift math falls short; the watch goes to the appraiser. **Act out:** Sydney's warning lands mid-winning-streak.

### Act 4 — The Bust

Keisha presses anyway: her stand-share advance added to the position, eyes open, twice warned. One red morning: down 80%, savings and advance gone — her account, her thumb, her loss. → **Essie: 1987.** Strike pay, Black Monday, her one panic sale near the bottom, and the boring certificate that survived because it was slow. Max crawls back to the warehouse and asks Emily, publicly, how to open a boring account. Keisha ices him completely, loading the whole loss onto a story in which her thumb never appears — and the valve closes; a hider with no valve is a hider whose silence becomes unbearable. She applies for a fall campus job — her first outward ask. → **Denise strand 3:** the watch sells on Tuesday; the pawn ticket goes into her purse. Out of private moves, Keisha goes to Estelle's to help with a paperwork box, and turns detective: a dividend statement that doesn't belong in a poor woman's garage, a decades-old stock certificate, letterhead matching last year's portal credit — and a glossy retirement-services folder with a date circled in someone else's hand, eleven days out. One engineered look at Gary: Estelle at her own table, animated, listened to for the first time in fifty years. Keisha borrows the contract overnight and reads it the way she read the vending ordinance: surrender charges, the commission, the countdown. In the folder, "simplify your estate" — the garage sale that opened the book was Gary's fingerprint, and Estelle let Denise believe the nagging worked. Before Keisha can act, Estelle arrives at the shame visit — being almost-had twice in one life cannot have a witness — and trades the whole story for silence: the plant, the banks, the erasure, the fund slip, $900,000 the slow way, and a 529 in Keisha's name since age two, transferred every August, paid last year too. The find stories were the teaching all along. She calls herself Essie once. Then, quietly: "And you owe a cash box twenty dollars." Mid-accusation — *you built a system of secret help so you'd never have to have one honest conversation* — Keisha hears herself: the hidden number, the prosecuted boy, the same wound at three scales. She gives her whole summer back. **Act out:** Estelle asks the one price Keisha won't pay — keep it all from Denise.

### Act 5 — The Table

The race is against shame, with a circled date. Keisha refuses secrecy and refuses to go behind Estelle's back, and wins with Estelle's own arithmetic: a fortune nobody knows about is a fortune nobody can defend — hiding stopped being safe the day Gary found you. → **Essie: 2021.** The 529 shifted to safety because college has become real: she has been watching all along. The kitchen-table chain reaction, one scene, three collapses: Keisha first — the vow, the float, the account, the crash — with Estelle's needle landing mid-confession: *you told the Ellis boy before your own mother.* Which forces Estelle: the fortune, the 529, the guarantee, named across fifty years of kitchen, Gary's folder on the table. Which breaks Denise, who leaves the room and returns with her phone — the parent portal, logged in since June — and a pawn ticket: *I've known all summer. I sold Daddy's watch Tuesday.* Then Denise does the thing her whole adaptation was built to avoid: asks one small, concrete question about the 529, out loud, and waits to be answered. Gary's appointment is a sentence, not a showdown: three women, a contract with margin notes, two surrender clauses read back, one question about his commission — he built the pitch for a woman alone, and he leaves politely. The transfer posts days before the due date, the way it was always going to, and the remainder is closed by exactly the summer she had: wages, stand share, the job that said yes. **Coda:** the costs get papered — a laptop loan from Estelle at real terms, signed, "thanks, Grandma"; the lost advance repaid from campus-job shifts; the watch traced by the family's great finder of objects and gone anyway — some prices stay paid. Max's bad apology, forgiven anyway; a split check; her laugh at his new ticker. Sofi's custodial account opens; the good corner is hinted for next June. Then I-29 north, overpasses rated, next year's gap visible on a family ledger instead of in one girl's head — and Sofi at the fence, driver's manual against her side, watching Keisha the way Keisha once watched Estelle. The pattern still transmits. It just talks now.
