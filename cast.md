# Cast

**Conventions (this revision).** The spine is canonical for everything that happens on the page; this file is **texture only** — who each person is, how they behave and talk, what they hide and why. It holds registers and sample lines, money wounds, right/wrong pairs, hard guardrails, dyad logic, and pre-book backstory the spine relies on but never states. Present-day events, scene mechanics, and reveal scheduling live in the spine and appear here only as **(spine)** pointers. Each dyad is written once, in the entry of the character who owns it dramatically. All arithmetic lives in the money fact-sheet.

**Open reconciliation flag:** Max's 2023 round trip is written below as a reader-held card, but the spine currently has no beat that deals it to the reader. Either place one (a natural slot is near the Wk 4 account opening or the Act 4 public bottom) or demote it to author-side backstory.

## Keisha Carter, 19 — narrator

Black; born and raised near-north Sioux Falls; only child of Denise Carter and Terrence "Terry" Boyd — a Carter, not a Boyd, a fact nobody has ever explained out loud. Sophomore-to-be in civil engineering at SDSU; **first in her family to enroll in college.** Her fascination is ordinary infrastructure under stress — she rates I-29 overpasses on expansion joints and ugly repairs, has opinions about culverts, and rejects on principle the claim that bridges are interesting because they *bring people together*. Dream job: county engineer — public works, drainage, the person who signs off on the ugly repairs. Her dream is *maintenance*, in a family that only knows *rescue*.

- **Body:** 5'9"; braids done herself at the kitchen sink every three weeks — eight hours, $0; fryer-burn scars on her right forearm; white sneakers kept obsessively clean.
- **Comic register — deadpan quantifier:** rates things on invented axes (*that apology was a 4 on load-bearing, an 8 on paint*); says "noted" when hurt; never "I can't afford it," always *that's outside the load path*. The quantifying is armor and the book knows it — the tic thins as she gets honest; fires at most once per chapter in Act 1.
- **Job and money physics:** McDonald's fryer, weekday lunch shifts, held at **30 hours** — the wage physics she grew up inside, ceiling in writing. Hours-for-dollars is the only lever she has ever seen pulled; the failed second-job hunt (spine, Wks 2–3) is what makes the brokerage legible as *the second job she was looking for, except it pays in maybe*. **Savings: $1,500** — the balance she keeps rechecking, the sale-day tell. (Gap anatomy and crash arithmetic: money fact-sheet.)
- **Coping:** panic converted into systems — *archive is technically a filing system*; checks the banking app between customers as if the number might behave differently under supervision.
- **The vow:** her silence is a vow, not a lie — the kitchen-drawer envelopes are spoken for, and she will not make her mother re-deal them.
- **Campus:** the ASCE Steel Bridge team is the first place she became known outside her family. Freshman keeper of the competition rules — the tabbed binder is her prop, on her desk all summer — she caught a prohibited connection before fabrication and earned the timed assembly; regional champions (the chat says so without irony), her sophomore return treated as settled fact. This year: rules compliance and part of the connection design. Feeds the mute-and-design-call thread (spine, Acts 3–5).
- **Superpower (planted early, fired late):** she reads boring documents — steel-bridge rules, a vending ordinance, an annuity contract. The first two teach her, a little too successfully, that systems yield when she reads their definitions more carefully than everyone else.
- **Before Max:** one almost-relationship freshman year, dissolved over winter break because she wouldn't text back during fryer shifts — no villain, never mentioned at home. She has never had a real breakup because she has never let anything get far enough to require one. Max is the first person who ever got in — through the money door, which is the joke and the problem. The freeze is her first real breakup, conducted in the family's native silence. (Walk, freeze, apology: spine.)
- **Non-thematic want:** the heron war (spine runner) — three summers, her phone against a smug retiree's $3,000 lens, public and escalating, costs nothing and teaches nothing; hands Estelle her best needle.
- **Mistake ledger (character calibration):** her errors are always locally rational — *poor grandma, heavy chain, obviously fake* is the ledger's first entry (spine, page one), and the crash completes the her-thumb-her-loss accounting. Blaming Max afterward is externalizing — one more form of hiding. **The refusal:** she mirrors the ticker and declines the coin — epistemology, not prudence: a ticker has filings she can out-read; the coin has no text. One correct call inside the losing campaign; the ledger stays honest, not total.
- **Money wound:** needing help feels like failure.
- **Right about:** work has value, and asking can cost dignity. **Wrong about:** that work is the only lever, and that grinding substitutes for speaking.
- **Dyads (owned here):** *Denise* — the count duet, learned at seven standing on a milk crate at her mother's register: they can count money together in perfect sync and cannot discuss it at all; every money scene between them is a counting scene. *Estelle* — the only grandchild who listens to the provenance stories all the way through; that is why she is the one in the garage, the one trusted with the paperwork box, the one who reads Gary's contract. *Emily* — best friends since branch-library homework partnerships; the person Keisha texts first. *Sofi* — taught her long division at the Ortiz table; now Sofi invoices her — the reversal is the point. (Sydney: see Sydney. Max: see Max.)
- **Arc:** from carrying the number alone to saying it out loud; from one money physics to three; from prosecuting other people's failures to owning her own thumb. Her victory is not that school gets paid for; it's that she stops disappearing from a life already in motion.

## Estelle Carter, 78 — grandmother, hidden investor, collector

Black; family came up from Arkansas in the Great Migration; finished eighth grade, then the John Morrell plant at seventeen — forty-eight years on the trim line, retiring in 2013. Married **Nathaniel "Nate" Carter** at twenty; widowed at twenty-eight in 1975 when the plant killed him — and a helpful man in a good suit "handled" the settlement, which is how it got small. Erased twice in one year: by the accident, and by every institution that would not deal with a woman alone. Raised three children on line wages; taught herself everything from library books and the business pages, and never felt entitled to say any of it out loud — she could always *do* it, never *say* it.

**Her secret timeline:**

- **1965** — seventeen, one twenty folded back out of her pay: the first money she ever hid.
- **1975** — Nate dies; the shrunk settlement is the zeroth rung of the predator ladder.
- **1976** — first fund account: a salesman's load fund, signature practiced on scrap paper.
- **1982** — reads about IRAs in the paper and maxes hers, twenty years running.
- **1984** — the gold rope chain, cash, the first thing she ever bought purely for herself. Worn exactly twice.
- **1985** — $1,000 into Deere & Co. at the bottom of the farm crisis; one paper certificate, never sold.
- **1987** — the worst winter: the strike, then Black Monday. Her one panic — sells near the bottom *and* pawns the chain, the flinch recorded in paper and metal. Charlene, nineteen, leaves for Atlanta before the strike ends.
- **1989** — back in via a no-load index fund and never sells again; redeems the chain and never wears it again, because it means the year she flinched.
- **1998** — pays off the house; two more decades of wages with nowhere to go but the funds.
- **2009** — opens a 529 in two-year-old Keisha's name, $400 every January, one for each grandchild after; the standing rule, never spoken: the same four-year deal for any grandchild who enrolls. Keisha is simply the first to trigger it.
- **2013** — retires. The timeline ends here; everything after June 2026 is spine business. (Drafting note for the chain's handover: the gift is protective, not confessional — the near-loss prompts it, the 1987 story stays untold, and a decade-old impulse finally lands. Her canon line for the moment is *somewhere I can see it*, with the mutter *should've given you this when you made supervisor*.)

Total: **$900,000, built the slow way** — plus a **$5,000 transfer she makes herself every late summer** against Keisha's bill; she never said it would recur, and both Keisha and Denise filed year one as a one-time gift. **Her story about the silence — the curriculum:** hard work made her; certainty would have made the girl soft. A rationalization, not the motive — the compulsion predates the philosophy by sixty years — and the book treats it that way: she offers it out loud exactly once, and it collapses under arithmetic, not argument (spine).

- **The children:** **Charlene, 57, Atlanta** — left at nineteen and made leaving her personality; three grown kids, no college (which is what makes Keisha *first*); one call a month; knows nothing and would be loudest at learning it. **Marcus, 55, Omaha** — the Sunday-at-4:00 call, eleven minutes, thirty years; two school-age kids with silent 529s; knows nothing. **Denise, 52** — the one who stayed; the child Estelle raised hardest and taught least. Six grandchildren in all.
- **Body:** the plant is written on her — thick knuckles, knife scars, hands always cold, a grip that surprises. Sits like sitting is a decision — and can sprint blocks when an object is at stake, which page one proves.
- **Daily geometry:** fixed orbits — Tuesday senior center, Thursday the downtown library (business pages in print, fifty years), Saturday estate sales, Sunday church with Denise. The fixed orbits are the vulnerability: the senior center is where Gary found her in February, warmly referred by Verna. Since spring she dresses up for Tuesdays and does not deny the "gentleman friend" teasing — truer than the joke knows: someone is finally listening, and she likes it too much to correct the record. His swag sits in plain sight because her cover guards it: nobody audits advice given to a woman with nothing to advise.
- **Voice register:** business-page vocabulary in plant-floor syntax — *that fund's carrying a load like a bad mule; an expense ratio will eat you quiet, like damp.* **The rule beneath it — fluent about prices, silent about positions:** past money is dead money, safe to narrate; forbidden are present tense and first person — what she holds, what anything is worth *now*. It's why the chain gets no story: the one object whose history is a position. The purchasing-power talk is also her cover — remembered prices read as nostalgia, not wealth — and the one door left open: Gary walks in through 1965 and waits for the present tense.
- **Comic register — the provenance set:** every object gets its price, its provenance, and one withering line about the fool who sold it; entry price public, valuation secret. At her own sale the tic inverts: she prices to repel. The find stories are her displaced autobiography and, it turns out, the teaching all along. **The one exception is the chain** — the conspicuous silence belongs to the reader alone (guardrail in the design guide).
- **The collector:** fifty years of finds curated like a museum; she cannot let go of what she holds — not the finds, not the certificate, not the truth. The flip side of holding everything is knowing where everything is: she registers a moved object within a day — the object-radar the plot leans on twice (spine, page one and Act 4).
- **The chain's lesson (carried, never stated):** gold is not how you grow money; it is how you *hold* money when institutions won't hold it for you — the bank that can't refuse you. Paired with the 1965 twenty: cash holds, assets grow, taught by juxtaposition only.
- **The socks (family lore, plant-and-detonate):** famously modest Christmas gifts — store-brand crew socks, every grandchild, every year. The lore files the chain as a fluke and keeps the visible-evidence budget at zero; the detonation is the dinner's silent math. **Guardrail (hard):** no visible generosity, ever — conspicuous giving would break the cover, the psychology, and the dinner's calibrated re-pricing at once. Systematic and invisible, or nothing.
- **The pride:** Keisha's enrollment is the proudest fact of her life, never said in the second person — it leaks sideways, in her native registers. Stated plainly exactly once, at the table, inside the fairness rule. **Guardrail:** never on the record early.
- **Non-thematic want:** the Canaries — decades of perfect hand-ruled scorebooks, at war with a scoreboard that is frequently wrong and never sorry. Numbers as pure pleasure, no lesson. (Garden texture: the annual failed Arkansas beefsteak campaign.)
- **Dyads (owned here):** *Denise* — the garage-sale nagging is the whole relationship in miniature: Denise pushes, Estelle permits, neither says why. *The stand* — 30% silent partner who is not silent: drops by to "just visit" and recounts the cash box; the recounts stop being a joke midway (spine).
- **Secret ledger (what she hides, from whom):** the total — from everyone; the 529 system and the four-year plan — from everyone, Keisha and Denise included; the chain's 1987 story — never told, on the page or off it; the garage sale's true instigator; the fairness rule for the other five grandchildren; and Gary himself — the "gentleman friend" teasing stands uncorrected, guarded by the family's own assumption that she has nothing worth advising. (Reveal order and venues: spine, Acts 4–5.)
- **Money wound:** once erased, she built a life around never needing anyone again.
- **Right about:** visible money attracts predators — proved three times (the settlement man, the 1976 salesman, Gary). **Wrong about:** the armor outlived its war — by 2026 hiding is what makes her huntable, and the loneliness the armor produced is itself the breach: Gary's product is listening, and she is the standard profile precisely because no one at her own table asks a second question. The phone era hunts hidden and visible alike, and the community's rational answer — a trustworthy real person — is precisely the channel that finds her.
- **Guardrails:** the collection is curated, never squalor — and **never the piggy bank**; the gap closes through the 529 and labor, full stop. Her vulnerability is not intelligence; it's fifty years of being unlistened-to. What shames her is not being fooled — it's being *findable*.
- **Arc:** from hiding as safety to saying her position out loud and allowing the family to defend it with her. Understood, not absolved; she does not apologize well, and the book does not make her.

## Denise Carter, 52 — mother, the middle strand

Black; youngest of Estelle's three; a toddler when Nate died — no memory of her father, only rationed stories. Bagger at the same grocery store in 1991, out-worked everyone to shift supervisor: thirty-five years, one store, honest work that never compounds. Married Terry Boyd in 2003; never took his name and gave Keisha hers — never explained out loud. **The 2016 collision (a feature):** divorce, promotion, and the envelope system all land the same year — she rebuilt herself around being excellent at work and airtight at home, exactly the woman who takes every Friday shift. (**The parallel lever:** more hours is also the only solution she can see — identical to her daughter's hunt one strand over; two silences, never compared.) Runs the household on labeled cash envelopes — GROCERIES, LIGHTS, CAR, SCHOOL — and taught Keisha the cash-drawer liturgy (count it twice, band the twenties). **Knowledge state on the chain:** it reaches her a decade late, without its history (spine); she witnessed the chase and the appraisal, so she knows it is real and roughly what it's worth — which is why any later transaction of hers is a fully priced transgression, never a discovery at a counter.

**Her money physics — perfect defense, no offense:** every dollar labeled, guarded, spent on purpose, none of it compounding. A brilliant goalie on a team with no forwards:

- **One paycheck since 2016;** child support ended in 2025 — this summer is her thinnest margin ever.
- **She rents** — a check every month of her adult life, in the family where her mother burned the mortgage in 1998. The reader does the math on whose silence that is.
- **The 401(k) she never joined:** the store has one, with a match — a portal, a form, a decision nobody ever explained; her systems gap, twenty years before the bursar's portal. The book leaves this cost unremedied.
- **The thin file:** no credit card ever means no credit history; the one car loan came at a brutal rate, paid off early, of course. Avoiding credit is not free.

- **The pride:** *no credit card debt, not one dime, not once* — and she is **right**: thirty years of watching advances and minimums eat her coworkers. The book validates the discipline and quietly prices it. **Standing rule (the Sheldon card):** the plot prices her rule once, hard (spine, strand 3); no character ever states the counterfactual — only the reader. Twin of Terry's would-he-have-helped.
- **Money wound:** being asked reveals you failed to anticipate — need discovered is competence disproven. Her account ends every month at zero and she calls that **"even."** Breaking even as identity: pride and prison in one word.
- **Right about:** debt eats people and discipline is real protection. **Wrong about:** that safety is the same as standing still — perfect defense doesn't score, and the system charges you for the credit you never used.
- **The gap:** not sense but systems literacy — bursars, portals, aid letters, anything with a login; the structural condition of a first-generation college parent, never a punchline. Nobody taught her, because Estelle never taught anyone.
- **Adaptation:** her lifelong habit is not-asking; performed certainty is her camouflage. She believes a competent mother anticipates need without making her child ask.
- **Body and props:** a fast walker on her feet since 1991 — good shoes resoled, never replaced; reads a room's mood the way she reads a register tape. Props: the envelopes; the chain — new since June, worn daily, a weight she's still getting used to; the store keys carried so long the lanyard is store history.
- **Register — supervisor-speak as control:** she runs the household like a shift — *I need a decision on this hotdish by four; that's not my area, but I'll cover it.* She means every word; performed certainty, not routine. Tics: **"we're even"** and **"I don't owe anybody a dime"** — her recurring self-benediction. (Its detonation: spine.)
- **The store:** structural comedy, serious center — the produce guy's feuds, the register-3 curse, the Friday quitter rehired every Monday; Denise resolves each without shaping a punchline. Her competence is the fixed point; the reader never laughs at her systems gap.
- **Non-thematic want:** the church potluck — the decades-long unacknowledged hotdish rivalry with Sister Deloris Hayes (spine); the book does not score a final winner.
- **Private want:** she has protected move-in day on the store calendar. More than making a payment, she wants to be included in her daughter's return to Brookings, and cannot quite say so.
- **The runner:** she keeps meaning to visit the stand and never does — the supervisor covering everyone else's Friday. The one visit is spine business (one cup, full price).
- **Dyads (owned here):** *Terry* — spoken of rarely and never badly; "your father" in logistics-voice only; the divorce ended in silence and is maintained in silence. *Charlene* — approves loudly from Atlanta and does nothing; Charlene lectured about "making Mama downsize," Denise spent a year doing it. *Marcus* — the group text's weatherman: present, pleasant, no information exchanged. *Emily* — the girl whose count she trusts; recognition across a generation, register tape to spreadsheet. (Estelle, Keisha, Lourdes: see their entries.)
- **Secret ledger:** she hides the June portal login, the Friday-shift rescue, and the chain sale. Hidden from her: the fortune, the chain's 1987 history, the 529 system, and the true shape of Keisha's summer. The 401(k) match is the one secret nobody was keeping — the system just never introduced itself. (Reveal order: spine.)
- **Arc:** from anticipating in silence to asking and staying in the answer — asking can be survived, and speaking does not require instant forgiveness.

## Max Ellis, 22 — fast-money foil, light love interest

White; Sydney's younger brother by one year; the Ellis kids grew up four blocks over, family friends since grade school — Washington High class of 2022, a few years ahead of Keisha and Emily: neighborhood, not school. One semester at Southeast Tech, ghosted by October. Warehouse picker since the 2022 holiday surge — 6 a.m. shift, a scanner that tells him where to walk, a metric board that ranks every picker by percentile. Top-ten-percent and hates that he's good at it: the shift genuinely runs whether he's on it or not, and the shift *knows*. Lives in the Ellis basement at $200 a month, which is how a warehouse wage produced the stake he'll burn in August (figures: money fact-sheet).

**The Ellis household — money as event (shared block; Sydney's entry points here):** Rick Ellis put in floors for thirty years, feast and famine, and only the feasts became stories. The founding legend is **the boat year** — one fat season (~2012), a used Lund, three summers on Lake Madison, endless narration — and the boat's quiet sale in a lean year, never mentioned. Pam Ellis kept the actual books and was never once the story. Both kids learned the same lesson and answered it differently: money arrives in *moments*, and the years in between are silence. Sydney recreates the moment by spending it; Max hunts the moment that will end the in-between. **Guardrail:** Pam's ledger stays texture — never a plot device or late reveal.

**His secret origin (the three weeks):** summer 2023, warehouse savings into a meme stock, tripled in three weeks, told everyone; rode it back down past even by September — net minus $400, told no one, ever. He is not chasing money; he is chasing the three weeks — the only time the number on the board meant he mattered. The 2026 conviction ticker is the three weeks trying to happen again, and this time it starts by working. **The position is split:** the ticker plus bitcoin (arithmetic: fact-sheet); when the ticker craters the coin merely wobbles — *half right*, and half right is all the religion needs; no character ever remarks on the coin. (Reader-card — see the reconciliation flag up top.)

- **Body:** 6'1", warehouse shoulders, a knee that complains at 22; steel-toes by the basement door; one AirPod perpetually in; phone at 9% from ticker-checking; the Ellis grin, the family's actual currency.
- **Prop — the camera roll:** a survivorship-bias museum — every green day screenshotted, no red day photographed in his life. **The supply chain:** the ticker comes from a "signals" group whose admin posts wins, never losses; Max is a retail franchise of his own curation logic. He can spot the break room's scams on sight (*that's a farm account, dude*) — fluency as proximity, not safety — and after any public loss the "funds recovery" DMs arrive: a loss is a lead.
- **Comic register — bro-quant:** cup sizes are *entry, growth, and moon*; the stand is *pre-revenue* even when it's revenue; flirting is *initiating a position*; his retirement number changes every time he says it. **The tell:** the register drops when he means something — he never talks like this at the warehouse; the apology becomes possible only when he drops it for one sentence.
- **Money physics — all offense, no defense:** the true inverse of Estelle — *she does money brilliantly and cannot speak it; he speaks money constantly and does it terribly.* The one person who can hear "$7,800" as information instead of shame — the whole pressure-valve mechanism — and he keeps the secret, even from Sydney: he clocks that the Carters run different rules and honors them without understanding them. His one act of discipline, and it's load-bearing.
- **The mechanics — her agency, his influence:** her account, her thumb, her loss; no handover, no debt, ever. Not weightless, though: he packages luck as knowledge because he likes being trusted. Two flinches, both overridden (spine): *I'm not, like, a fiduciary* at the opening; *desperation money never wins* at the press — genuinely meant, never once followed, leaving him an *I told you* he never uses.
- **The stand:** self-image contribution, market wisdom; actual contribution, the hatchback and his back. Paid in agua fresca and mockery; never asks for a cut — the cap table stays girls-and-family only. The gap is the comedy.
- **Non-thematic want:** the Vibe — his grandfather's 2004 Pontiac, 190,000 miles, the stand's fleet; annual rust war, junkyard haggling (his one real edge — Estelle's edge too, and nobody notices). Dreams aloud about the Porsche while waxing a $2,100 car the book leaves useful, loved, and unpriced.
- **Money wound:** slow money means being nobody — steady money is invisible money, and invisible is dead.
- **Right about:** hours-for-dollars alone can't close every gap; ownership and compounding are real levers the Carter women can't see; saying numbers out loud is normal. **Wrong about:** speed — that conviction is analysis, that boredom is death, that because the market rewarded him once, it *saw* him.
- **Secret ledger:** hides the 2023 round trip from everyone, forever; hides Keisha's $7,800 from his own family, Sydney included. Hidden from him: the entire Carter plot — plus the two facts the book never tells him: the Vibe is his best-managed holding, and Dale is the retirement plan working.
- **Dyads (owned here):** *Sydney* — mirror siblings; roast each other's version, defend each other to anyone outside. *Keisha* — the first person who ever argued with him instead of eating the bro-talk; he finds the invented axes electric. The flirtation is denominated entirely in valuation jokes — the coin argument is standing material — and he honors every Carter silence without understanding it, including the one around the two of them. (The walk, the freeze-as-unnamed-breakup, the apology: spine.) *Emily* — post-crash he calls her *my CFO*; the joke is entirely his, while she quietly audits the $85 — the male mirror of the book's lesson: shame survives being public. (The public bottom: spine.) *Dale* — offstage: ~58, thirty years in distribution, paid-off truck, talks about his 401(k) match the way other men talk about fishing; Max files him under *cautionary tale* and is wrong in a way the book never corrects. No reveal scene, ever.
- **Presence budget (drafting rule):** eight touches total, all placed in the spine; between the public bottom and the apology he disappears — **his absence from the page is Act 4's loneliness engine.** The apology is specific but incomplete, possible only with the bro-voice dropped; he never claims her loss, never asks absolution, never spends the *I told you*.
- **Boundaries (hard):** entirely outside the family plot, forever — his Carter knowledge tops out at the $7,800, sale-day gossip, what the market saw at JazzFest, and the crash he can infer. He never touches her money in any direction. Never villainous, never pathetic — not a rung on the predator ladder but its counterweight, the man in the money story who doesn't price women at scrap; not redeemed, just priced. No written exchange with Estelle after the sale; she prices him exactly once, inside her existing needle — *all ticker, no tape, that one* — and only where her market fluency is already minutes from full disclosure (placement: spine). The romance rises above check-splitting altitude exactly once — the walk — and never again: no label, no declaration.
- **Arc (financial flatline, relational notch):** survives failure visibly and remains financially himself; the book ends before another cycle certifies reform or relapse. The narrow change: once, without bro-language, he distinguishes his influence from her agency and names what he did with her trust.

## Sydney Ellis, 23 — chaos engine

White; Max's older sister; Washington High class of 2021, then a nail-tech program at Stewart School left half-finished — undramatically: the schedule collided with the job that paid *now*, and she doesn't mourn it. Sales floor at an Empire Mall apparel chain, where the wardrobe expectation is spending that is secretly a job requirement. One roommate, real rent, a credit-card balance — broke in a way nobody names, least of all her. She was the designated older kid of the block — fifteen watching eleven reads as babysitting only to the eleven-year-old — which is how she became Keisha's first Levitt show, her parallel-parking teacher, and her standing permission to have fun.

- **The Ellis money culture:** see Max's shared block — same boat-year lesson, opposite answer: she recreates the moment by spending it. Her warning to Keisha (placement: spine, Act 3 out) is, unspoken, the first time she ever bet against her brother's entire religion — and doubly loaded: she half-knows, from the teasing she ran herself, what else Keisha has riding on him, and warns her anyway.
- **Money wound:** an unnarrated life doesn't count. In a house where only the feasts became stories, she buys the moments that make the week tellable — and her superpower is the mechanism: she reads every mood in the room first, feels the night dying before anyone else, and buys the round that saves it. The balance is built of both kinds of purchase — good seats and picked-up checks; a statement that reads like a scrapbook, where other people's evenings are itemized. (Figure: fact-sheet.)
- **Comic register — justification arithmetic (never named, by anyone):** cost-per-wear, cost-per-memory, *it was basically free because I skipped lunch twice* — the Ellis dialect in its second grammar: Max's version exists to sound smart, hers to feel licensed. **Guardrail:** her math is occasionally *right* — cost-per-wear on her work boots genuinely pencils — so the tic reads as a reasoning style, never a deficiency the book smirks at.
- **Body / cast function:** the Ellis grin; nails as rotating gallery; dressed a notch above every occasion. She appraises — retail-floor eye, weighs the recovered chain and says a number, one sentence, no second lesson (page one).
- **The nails:** the half-certificate left one real skill — elaborate sets, done free, for the whole block: generosity in her native currency without a receipt, the one giving that never touches the balance. **Guardrail:** nobody ever suggests she charge; she cheered Sofi's move and knows it isn't hers.
- **Non-thematic want:** the block's stray tom loves everyone except Sydney — two summers into a public, increasingly researched campaign; the book does not declare a winner.
- **Right about:** joy is real value and money spent on people isn't wasted — her taste is real, and the value discovery (the Levitt crowds) belongs to the person everyone calls irresponsible. **Wrong about:** that unsaid costs stay unpaid — the balance compounds whether or not it's narrated.
- **Dyads (owned here):** *Keisha* — the standing contract, built on the block-kid years: Keisha is her designated adult, she is Keisha's permission to have fun — why the warning lands as a role reversal Keisha can't file. *Emily* — opposite numbers, front-of-house and back-of-house: one money culture performs, one never speaks, both broke in ways nobody names; each privately thinks the other is the brave one. *Estelle* — the book's two object-lovers, holder and circulator; Estelle rates her purchases in appraisal register (*you overpaid, but the cut's good*) — object talk, never market talk; the one young person she grades on judgment rather than thrift.
- **Boundary:** outside the family money plot, forever — she knows about the trading but never learns the $7,800, so her warning is made blind: she recognizes the behavior, not the stakes, because gambling for a feeling is her own purchase order. That blindness is what makes it the brave version.
- **Arc:** from spending to prove value to choosing joy with eyes open — the appetite stays; only the accounting changes. Her priced choice (spine, Act 5) takes the loss on her own category, not on gifts — which is what makes it Keisha's dress rehearsal for costs said out loud. The book is not anti-spending; Sydney is why.

## Emily Larsen, 18 — math brain

White; Norwegian-Lutheran, fourth-generation Sioux Falls; only child; class of 2026, one grade behind Keisha, best friends since branch-library homework partnerships. Bound for Augustana — the default nobody ever discussed, expensive in the exact solvent-seeming way. Her father moves other people's balances all day in card operations at a downtown bank; her mother runs the hotdish economy, concern always arriving as food, never as a sentence. The Larsen home is kind, warm, and has never once discussed money out loud.

- **The calibrating event (her secret — lives only here; per her guardrail it never reaches the page):** the culture is the soil, but one night, maybe ten, she overheard her parents say a single exact figure through a door, once, and the house went cold for a season without anyone acknowledging why. The lesson took: a named number is a detonation; precision is what the silence protects everyone *from*. She fled into math because numbers everywhere else are safe.

Her forecasts are earnest, never bits; the drive to Brookings is *48.7 minutes, conditions permitting*, no performance in the saying. **Guardrail:** her math is never wrong — only incomplete when the world refuses to hold still. **Lane markers:** Keisha quantifies as armor (comic); Sofi prices as sunlight (comic); Emily measures as safety (serious).

Lanes kept: Estelle's money metaphors come from her own life; Emily's come from yours.)

Speaks late and lands the sentence that reorganizes the conversation. The face-down phone is the one tell that reads as habit and is actually a wound.

## Sofía "Sofi" Ortiz, 16 — the watched one

Mexican-American; three doors down since before she was born, family in everything but blood; junior-to-be at Washington. Mother: Lourdes. - **The calibrating event:** the frozen account (canonical statement in Lourdes's entry) — with Sofi, nine, doing the talking, because the bank listened to the child in English before the woman with the documents. Two lessons took: institutions must be read word by word, and the work she did in those rooms was real work nobody counted. Lourdes got her receipts; Sofi got her meter.

The one wound in the book caught early: the invoice is the cure in progress, and it costs her real nerve — billing the people you love, at sixteen, in a house where you don't — which keeps her the book's hope instead of its prodigy.

## Lourdes Ortiz, ~40s — Sofi's mother, counter-wisdom

Mexican-American; three doors down; married to a contented Smithfield man, mother of Sofi and Diego. Cash, caution, liquidity, family obligation — a money culture with receipts, not folklore. One account for what requires an account; cash for everything else; and a favor economy she runs the way Estelle runs a portfolio, because it *is* her portfolio: help placed with the right families, compounding for decades, redeemable in kind.

- **The calibrating event (canonical statement; Sofi's entry points here):** the frozen account — a mismatched middle name, three weeks, four trips, one fee, zero apologies — with her nine-year-old doing the talking. Her distrust has a paper trail.
- **The Estelle rhyme (owned, never staged):** the book's two armored women took the same wound — un-listened-to by institutions — and built opposite fortresses: Estelle hid *inside* the system, Lourdes stays *outside* it. They share no scene and never compare fortresses; the reader does.
- **Money wound:** once charged a fee for being herself, she keeps her money where it can't be misspelled.
- **Right about:** liquidity is real safety, institutions bill the unlucky, and an obligation network is wealth — hers has never frozen, never charged a fee, never asked for a middle name. **Wrong about:** a fortress outside the system is invisible to it — cash melts quietly and favors don't compound into a transcript. The book never corrects her on the page; her one concession is made on her own terms, pen in hand, every line read first.
- **Register — the favor ledger:** every favor ever traded, both directions, to the day, repaid in kind with exact interest — never money. The Denise treaty (trade favors, never money; audit each other's daughters with mutual respect) is her diplomacy in miniature.
- **The two canon gestures (scenes: spine):** the invoice — she argues love's terms (*in this family you do not bill the people you love*), then, handed the guardian pen, reads every line before signing: both halves true at once, and the second is where Sofi comes from. JazzFest — pure competence, with the consequence coming from nobody passing the word: the family disease demonstrated, for one night, in the counter-wisdom house too.
- **Guardrails:** never backward, never folklore, never a lesson — her system works on the page, visibly, all summer. Warm to the girls, exact with everyone.

## Gary Tollefson, ~60 — the advisor

*Sunrise Retirement Solutions.* Licensed, compliant, legal — that is the lesson. Not a scammer: a commission salesman for a bad-but-lawful product, working a channel. The scam epidemic is his camouflage and lead generation: a community freshly afraid of phone voices concludes *trust a real person who comes here* — and he is the real person who comes there.

He never learns the total, the 529s, or that his contract died at the hands of a nineteen-year-old who reads definitions. That ignorance keeps him Tuesday-sized.
