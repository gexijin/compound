### Keisha Carter, 19 — narrator

Black; born and raised near-north Sioux Falls; only child of Denise Carter and Terrence "Terry" Boyd — a Carter, not a Boyd, a fact nobody in the family has ever explained out loud. Sophomore-to-be in civil engineering at SDSU in Brookings — ordinary infrastructure under stress, a fascination entirely hers and comic in its specificity. She rates the I-29 overpasses on expansion joints, drainage, sightlines, and the quality of ugly repairs; has opinions about culverts; and rejects on principle the claim that bridges are interesting because they *bring people together*. **First person in her family to enroll in college.** Her dream job is not the famous bridge; it's county engineer — public works, drainage, the person who signs off on the ugly repairs. Her dream is *maintenance*, in a family that only knows *rescue*.

- **Body:** 5'9"; braids she does herself at the kitchen sink every three weeks — eight hours, $0; small fryer-oil burn scars on her right forearm; white sneakers kept obsessively clean.
- **Comic register — deadpan quantifier:** two tics, used with restraint. She rates things on invented axes (*that apology was a 4 on load-bearing, an 8 on paint*), and she says "noted" when she's hurt. She never says "I can't afford it"; she says *that's outside the load path*. The quantifying is armor, and the book knows it — the tic thins out as she gets honest.
- **Job and money physics:** a fast-food fryer job — weekday lunch shifts, never market days — the wage physics she grew up inside. Hours-for-dollars is the only lever she has ever seen pulled; equity feels like pretend money. (The anatomy of the tuition gap — aid, loans, why the number grew — lives in the money fact-sheet, not here.)
- **Coping style:** she turns panic into systems; she doesn't say she's terrified, she says *archive is technically a filing system*.
- **The vow:** her silence is a vow, not a lie — the kitchen-drawer envelopes are spoken for, and she will not make her mother re-deal them.
- **The father:** Terry Boyd, ~54, HVAC tech in Harrisburg — solvent, ordinary, remarried, with **Jordan, 7**, a half-brother Keisha has met exactly once. Divorce in 2015, Keisha nine: old enough to remember the household, young enough that her entire money education happened in the all-women version. The marriage didn't end over money or an affair; it ended the way this family does everything — he stopped bringing things up, she stopped asking, and one day the silence was the whole house. Child support arrived with reasonable reliability and stopped at eighteen. **Boundaries:** one scene (placed in the spine); he never learns about the bill; he does not appear in the resolution; the book never answers whether he'd have helped if asked. One more door nobody knocked on.
- **Offstage web:** cousins in Omaha (her uncle's kids — Estelle's other 529s), seen at holidays. A paternal grandmother in Terry's orbit: alive, distant, a birthday card with $10 in it every year — that is all she is and all she will ever be. The near-north Baptist congregation where Denise and Estelle are fixtures — Keisha lapsed at college but attends when home, because *not* going would be a statement and she is avoiding statements; the flop's church ladies and Gary's senior-center channel both grow out of it.
- **Campus life:** the SDSU ASCE Student Steel Bridge team is the first place she became known outside her family. In her freshman spring they won their regional competition, then finished anonymously enough at nationals to produce a durable body of team excuses. The group chat calls them regional champions without irony and treats her sophomore return as settled fact.
- **Her part in the bridge:** she was the freshman keeper of the competition rules — the physical binder is her prop, tabbed and annotated, on her desk all summer — and caught a prohibited connection before fabrication made it expensive, and earned a place in the timed assembly. The older students knew more engineering and designed most of the bridge; she mattered because she read closely, spoke in time, and practiced until her hands knew the build. This year they expect her to own rules compliance and part of the connection design.
- **Superpower (planted early, fired late):** she reads boring documents — steel-bridge rules in Brookings, a vending ordinance in June, an annuity contract in August. The first two teach her, a little too successfully, that systems yield when she reads their definitions more carefully than everyone else.
- **The dyads:** *Denise* — the cash-drawer liturgy is their shared language: they can count money together in perfect sync and cannot discuss it at all; every money scene between them is a counting scene. *Estelle* — Keisha is the only grandchild who listens to the provenance stories all the way through, which is why she is the one in the garage, the one trusted with the paperwork box, and eventually the one who reads Gary's contract. *Emily* — best friend since Washington High; homework partners at the branch library, escaping a house that was money-silent under constant noise (Keisha's: TV on, Denise on the phone with the store) and a house that was silent all the way down (Emily's). The person Keisha texts first about everything. *Sydney* — two years older; babysat her, took her to her first Levitt show, taught her to parallel park. Standing contract: Keisha is Sydney's designated adult, Sydney is Keisha's permission to have fun — which is why Sydney's mid-winning-streak warning lands as a role reversal Keisha can't file. *Sofi* — the little sister Keisha tutored at the Ortiz kitchen table; she taught Sofi long division, and now Sofi invoices her. The reversal is the point.
- **Before Max:** one almost-relationship freshman year — a boy from her dorm floor, dissolved over winter break because she wouldn't text back during fryer shifts. No villain, no heartbreak, never mentioned at home. She has never had a real breakup because she has never let anything get far enough to require one. Max is the first person who ever got in — through the money door, which is the joke and the problem.
- **Non-thematic want (comic runner):** the heron war. She has spent three summers trying to photograph the great blue heron below the falls, and this year a smug retiree birder with a $3,000 lens keeps getting there first and showing everyone. She shoots with her phone, which is both the handicap and the joke. The campaign is public, escalating, and witnessed by the whole market. It costs nothing, teaches nothing, and hands Estelle her best needle: *you can wait three hours for a bird but not thirty seconds to tell your mother something.* Last beat reserved: whether she gets the shot is decided in the coda, after the money is settled.
- **Money wound:** needing help feels like failure.
- **Right about:** work has value, and asking can cost dignity. **Wrong about:** that work is the only lever, and that grinding substitutes for speaking.
- **After the crash:** she blames Max — externalizing as one more form of hiding — until she hears herself mid-accusation at Estelle's.
- **Her timeline:** 2006, born. 2013, age seven, learns the cash-drawer liturgy standing on a milk crate at her mother's register. 2015, the divorce; the labeled envelopes appear in the kitchen drawer that same fall. 2024, first-generation enrollment at SDSU. May 2025, the regional win; June 2025, the garage sale.
- **Arc:** from carrying the number alone to saying it out loud; from one money physics to three; from prosecuting other people's failures to owning her own thumb. As the gap closes around her she mutes the bridge-team chat and lets people who expect her mistake silence for ordinary summer absence. Her victory is not that school gets paid for; it's that she stops disappearing from a life already in motion.


### Estelle Carter, 78 — grandmother, hidden investor, collector

Black; family came up from Arkansas in the Great Migration for packing work; finished eighth grade, then the John Morrell plant at seventeen — forty-eight years on the trim line, knife work, through every change of the plant's ownership, retiring in 2013. Married **Nathaniel "Nate" Carter** at twenty; eight good years; widowed at twenty-eight in 1975 when the plant killed him — and a helpful man in a good suit "handled" the settlement for the young widow, which is how it got small. Erased twice in one year: by the accident, and by every institution that would not deal with a woman alone — banks legally until 1974, in practice longer. Raised three children on line wages. Taught herself everything from library books and the business pages, and never felt entitled to say any of it out loud: a woman who stopped school at eighth grade does not lecture; she could always *do* it, never *say* it.

**Her secret timeline:**

- **1965** — seventeen, the plant, one twenty-dollar bill folded back out of her pay: the first money she ever hid.
- **1975** — Nate dies on the floor; the shrunk settlement is the zeroth rung of the predator ladder, before the salesmen ever knock.
- **1976** — first fund account in her own name: a salesman's load fund, the slip filled out in handwriting practiced on scrap paper.
- **1982** — reads about IRAs in the paper and maxes hers, twenty years running.
- **1984** — the gold rope chain, paid in cash: the first thing she ever bought purely for herself. Worn exactly twice.
- **1985** — $1,000 into Deere & Co. at the bottom of the farm crisis; one paper certificate, never sold.
- **1987** — the worst winter: the Morrell strike, then Black Monday. Her one panic — sells near the bottom *and* pawns the chain in the same season, her flinch recorded in paper and in metal. Charlene, nineteen, leaves for Atlanta before the strike ends.
- **1989** — back in via a no-load index fund, and she never sells anything again; redeems the chain the same year — and never wears it again, because it means the year she flinched.
- **1998** — pays off the house: no rent, no debt, and two more decades of wages with nowhere to go but the funds.
- **2009** — opens a 529 in two-year-old Keisha's name, $400 every January, and opens one for each grandchild born after; the standing rule (never spoken): the same four-year deal for any grandchild who enrolls. Keisha is simply the first to trigger it.
- **2013** — retires.
- **~2015** — Denise makes supervisor; Estelle gives her the chain, plainly, as her own gift — her wordless way of retiring the flinch, the closest she ever comes to telling the story. The 1987 story itself stays untold.
- **2021** — shifts the college money to safety just ahead of the crash.

Total: **$900,000, built the slow way** — plus a **$5,000 transfer she makes herself every late summer** against Keisha's bill. Keisha and Denise know Estelle paid $5,000 freshman year, but Estelle never said it would recur; both filed it as a one-time gift, while Estelle privately planned the same payment for all four years.

- **The children:** **Charlene, 57, Atlanta** — left at nineteen and made leaving her whole personality; three grown kids (work, the Army, the trades — no college, which is what makes Keisha *first*); one call a month, mutual respect at a fixed distance; knows less than nothing about the money and would be loudest at learning it. **Marcus, 55, Omaha** — the Sunday-at-4:00 call, eleven minutes, every week for thirty years; two school-age kids whose 529s run as silently as everyone's; knows nothing. **Denise, 52** — the one who stayed; a toddler when Nate died, no memory of her father; the child Estelle raised hardest and taught least. Six grandchildren in all.
- **Body:** the plant is written on her — thick knuckles, old knife scars, hands always cold from forty years on a refrigerated floor, a grip that still surprises people. Moves deliberately; sits like sitting is a decision.
- **Daily geometry:** fixed orbits — Tuesday senior center, Thursday the downtown library where she has read the business pages in print for fifty years and the librarians hold her chair, Saturday estate sales (the collection's supply chain), Sunday church with Denise. The fixed orbits are the vulnerability — the senior center is where Gary found her.
- **Voice register:** business-page vocabulary in plant-floor syntax — *that fund's carrying a load like a bad mule; an expense ratio will eat you quiet, like damp.* She can talk about money the way other women talk about weather — but only to herself, until Gary (the trap) and the coda (the cure).
- **Comic register — the provenance set:** every object in the garage gets its price, its provenance, and one withering line about the fool who sold it; entry price public, valuation secret, the whole character in a storytelling tic. Haggles at other people's sales for sport. Owns the book's best needle (demonstrated in Keisha's heron runner). The find stories are her displaced autobiography and, it turns out, the teaching all along.
- **The collector:** fifty years of thrift-store finds curated in the garage like a museum. She has never once held a sale — until page one. She cannot let go of what she holds: not the finds, not the certificate, not the truth.
- **The chain (her object):** 1984 bought → 1987 pawned → 1989 redeemed, never worn again → ~2015 given to Denise, its history untold. What happens to it in 2025 is spine business. **The lesson it carries:** gold is not how you grow money; it is how you *hold* money when institutions won't hold it for you — the bank that can't refuse you.
- **The refused name:** she never let anyone call her Grandma — grandmas were old women, and she was busy. She is Stell or Miss Estelle; "Essie" is reserved, said by her exactly once, inside her own story. Keisha's unadorned "thanks, Grandma" at the loan signing is the payoff.
- **Non-thematic want (comic runner):** the Canaries. She keeps score by hand at Sioux Falls Canaries home games — a perfect hand-ruled scorebook, decades of them — and is at war with the new scoreboard, which is frequently wrong and never sorry. Numbers as pure pleasure, no lesson. (Garden texture, not a runner: the annual failed campaign to grow her mother's Arkansas beefsteak tomato in a South Dakota yard.)
- **Dyads:** *Denise* — the nagging about the garage sale is their whole relationship in miniature: Denise pushes, Estelle permits, neither says why. *Keisha* — the only grandchild who listens to the provenance stories all the way through; that is why she is the one in the garage. *The stand* — 30% silent partner who is not silent: drops by to "just visit" and recounts the cash box, which after JazzFest stops being a joke.
- **Guardrails:** the collection is curated, never squalor — and it is **never the piggy bank**; the gap closes through the 529 and labor, full stop. Her vulnerability to Gary is not intelligence — it's fifty years of being unlistened-to.
- **Secret ledger (what she hides, from whom, revealed when):** the total — never spoken to anyone, said once, at the table; the 529 system and the four-year plan — revealed at the shame visit; the chain's 1987 story — the dinner's final blow; the garage sale as Gary's planted idea — found by Keisha in the paperwork; the fairness rule for the other five grandchildren — said at the table, the sentence that turns the reveal from favoritism into a system.
- **Money wound:** once erased, she built a life around never needing anyone again.
- **Right about:** visible money attracts predators — the book proves her right three times (the settlement man, the 1976 salesman, Gary). **Wrong about:** the armor outlived its war; by 2025 hiding is what makes her huntable, and survival is not the same as living.
- **Arc:** from hiding as safety to teaching money out loud, twice in one week. Understood, not absolved; she does not apologize well, and the book does not make her.
