## Cast

### Keisha Carter, 19 — narrator

Black; born and raised near-north Sioux Falls; only child of Denise Carter and Terrence "Terry" Boyd — a Carter, not a Boyd, a fact nobody in the family has ever explained out loud. Sophomore-to-be in civil engineering at SDSU in Brookings — ordinary infrastructure under stress, a fascination entirely hers and comic in its specificity. She rates the I-29 overpasses on expansion joints, drainage, sightlines, and the quality of ugly repairs; has opinions about culverts; and rejects on principle the claim that bridges are interesting because they *bring people together*. **First person in her family to enroll in college.** Her dream job is not the famous bridge; it's county engineer — public works, drainage, the person who signs off on the ugly repairs. Her dream is *maintenance*, in a family that only knows *rescue*.

- **Body:** 5'9"; braids she does herself at the kitchen sink every three weeks — eight hours, $0; small fryer-oil burn scars on her right forearm; white sneakers kept obsessively clean.
- **Comic register — deadpan quantifier:** two tics, used with restraint. She rates things on invented axes (*that apology was a 4 on load-bearing, an 8 on paint*), and she says "noted" when she's hurt. She never says "I can't afford it"; she says *that's outside the load path*. The quantifying is armor, and the book knows it — the tic thins out as she gets honest.
- **Job and money physics:** a fast-food fryer job — weekday lunch shifts, never market days — the wage physics she grew up inside. Hours-for-dollars is the only lever she has ever seen pulled; equity feels like pretend money. (The anatomy of the tuition gap — aid, loans, why the number grew — lives in the money fact-sheet, not here.)
- **Coping style:** she turns panic into systems; she doesn't say she's terrified, she says *archive is technically a filing system*.
- **The vow:** her silence is a vow, not a lie — the kitchen-drawer envelopes are spoken for, and she will not make her mother re-deal them.
- **The father:** Terry Boyd, ~54, HVAC tech in Harrisburg — solvent, ordinary, remarried, with **Jordan, 7**, a half-brother Keisha has met exactly once. Divorce in 2015, Keisha nine: old enough to remember the household, young enough that her entire money education happened in the all-women version. The marriage didn't end over money or an affair; it ended the way this family does everything — he stopped bringing things up, she stopped asking, and one day the silence was the whole house. Child support arrived with reasonable reliability and stopped at eighteen. **Boundaries:** one scene (placed in the spine); he never learns about the bill; he does not appear in the resolution; the book never answers whether he'd have helped if asked. One more door nobody knocked on.
- **Offstage web:** cousins in Omaha (Marcus's kids — the other running 529s), seen at holidays; adult cousins in Atlanta (Charlene's three), known mostly from the group text. A paternal grandmother in Terry's orbit: alive, distant, a birthday card with $10 in it every year — that is all she is and all she will ever be. The near-north Baptist congregation where Denise and Estelle are fixtures — Keisha lapsed at college but attends when home, because *not* going would be a statement and she is avoiding statements. Church is where the family is watched; the flop's church ladies and Gary's senior-center channel both grow out of it.
- **Campus life:** the SDSU ASCE Student Steel Bridge team is the first place she became known outside her family. In her freshman spring they won their regional competition, then finished anonymously enough at nationals to produce a durable body of team excuses. The group chat calls them regional champions without irony and treats her sophomore return as settled fact.
- **Her part in the bridge:** she was the freshman keeper of the competition rules — the physical binder is her prop, tabbed and annotated, on her desk all summer — and caught a prohibited connection before fabrication made it expensive, and earned a place in the timed assembly. The older students knew more engineering and designed most of the bridge; she mattered because she read closely, spoke in time, and practiced until her hands knew the build. This year they expect her to own rules compliance and part of the connection design.
- **Superpower (planted early, fired late):** she reads boring documents — steel-bridge rules in Brookings, a vending ordinance in June, an annuity contract in August. The first two teach her, a little too successfully, that systems yield when she reads their definitions more carefully than everyone else.
- **The dyads:** *Denise* — the cash-drawer liturgy is their shared language: they can count money together in perfect sync and cannot discuss it at all; every money scene between them is a counting scene. *Estelle* — Keisha is the only grandchild who listens to the provenance stories all the way through, which is why she is the one in the garage, the one trusted with the paperwork box, and eventually the one who reads Gary's contract. *Emily* — best friend since Washington High; homework partners at the branch library, escaping a house that was money-silent under constant noise (Keisha's: TV on, Denise on the phone with the store) and a house that was silent all the way down (Emily's). The person Keisha texts first about everything. *Sydney* — two years older; babysat her, took her to her first Levitt show, taught her to parallel park. Standing contract: Keisha is Sydney's designated adult, Sydney is Keisha's permission to have fun — which is why Sydney's mid-winning-streak warning lands as a role reversal Keisha can't file. *Sofi* — the little sister Keisha tutored at the Ortiz kitchen table; she taught Sofi long division, and now Sofi invoices her. The reversal is the point.
- **Before Max:** one almost-relationship freshman year — a boy from her dorm floor, dissolved over winter break because she wouldn't text back during fryer shifts. No villain, no heartbreak, never mentioned at home. She has never had a real breakup because she has never let anything get far enough to require one. Max is the first person who ever got in — through the money door, which is the joke and the problem.
- **Non-thematic want (comic runner):** the heron war. She has spent three summers trying to photograph the great blue heron below the falls, and this year a smug retiree birder with a $3,000 lens keeps getting there first and showing everyone. She shoots with her phone, which is both the handicap and the joke. The campaign is public, escalating, and witnessed by the whole market. It costs nothing, teaches nothing, and hands Estelle her best needle: *you can wait three hours for a bird but not thirty seconds to tell your mother something.* Last beat reserved: whether she gets the shot is decided in the coda, after the money is settled.
- **Money wound:** needing help feels like failure.
- **Right about:** work has value, and asking can cost dignity. **Wrong about:** that work is the only lever, and that grinding substitutes for speaking.
- **After the crash:** she blames Max — externalizing as one more form of hiding — until she hears herself mid-accusation at Estelle's.
- **Her timeline:** 2006, born. 2013, age seven, learns the cash-drawer liturgy standing on a milk crate at her mother's register. 2015, the divorce; the labeled envelopes appear in the kitchen drawer that same fall. 2024, first-generation enrollment at SDSU. May 2025, the regional win; June 2025, the garage sale.
- **Arc:** from carrying the number alone to saying it out loud; from one money physics to three; from prosecuting other people's failures to owning her own thumb. As the gap closes around her she mutes the bridge-team chat and lets people who expect her mistake silence for ordinary summer absence. Her victory is not that school gets paid for; it's that she stops disappearing from a life already in motion.

### Estelle Carter, 78 — grandmother, hidden investor, collector

Black; family came up from Arkansas in the Great Migration for packing work; finished eighth grade, then the John Morrell plant at seventeen — forty-eight years on the trim line, knife work, through every change of the plant's ownership, retiring in 2013. Married **Nathaniel "Nate" Carter** at twenty; eight good years; widowed at twenty-eight in 1975 when the plant killed him — and a helpful man in a good suit "handled" the settlement for the young widow, which is how it got small. Erased twice in one year: by the accident, and by every institution that would not deal with a woman alone — banks legally until 1974, in practice longer. Raised three children on line wages. Taught herself everything from library books and the business pages, and never felt entitled to say any of it out loud: a woman who stopped school at eighth grade does not lecture; she could always *do* it, never *say* it.

**Her secret timeline:**

- **1965** — seventeen, the plant, one twenty-dollar bill folded back out of her pay: the first money she ever hid.
- **1975** — Nate dies on the floor; the shrunk settlement is the zeroth rung of the predator ladder, before the salesmen ever knock.
- **1976** — first fund account in her own name: a salesman's load fund, the slip filled out in handwriting practiced on scrap paper.
- **1982** — reads about IRAs in the paper and maxes hers, twenty years running.
- **1984** — the gold rope chain, paid in cash: the first thing she ever bought purely for herself. Worn exactly twice.
- **1985** — $1,000 into Deere & Co. at the bottom of the farm crisis; one paper certificate, never sold.
- **1987** — the worst winter: the Morrell strike, then Black Monday. Her one panic — sells near the bottom *and* pawns the chain in the same season, her flinch recorded in paper and in metal. Charlene, nineteen, leaves for Atlanta before the strike ends.
- **1989** — back in via a no-load index fund, and she never sells anything again; redeems the chain the same year — and never wears it again, because it means the year she flinched.
- **1998** — pays off the house: no rent, no debt, and two more decades of wages with nowhere to go but the funds.
- **2009** — opens a 529 in two-year-old Keisha's name, $400 every January, and opens one for each grandchild born after; the standing rule (never spoken): the same four-year deal for any grandchild who enrolls. Keisha is simply the first to trigger it.
- **2013** — retires.
- **~2015** — Denise makes supervisor; Estelle gives her the chain, plainly, as her own gift — her wordless way of retiring the flinch, the closest she ever comes to telling the story. The 1987 story itself stays untold.
- **2021** — shifts the college money to safety just ahead of the crash.

Total: **$900,000, built the slow way** — plus a **$5,000 transfer she makes herself every late summer** against Keisha's bill. Keisha and Denise know Estelle paid $5,000 freshman year, but Estelle never said it would recur; both filed it as a one-time gift, while Estelle privately planned the same payment for all four years.

- **The children:** **Charlene, 57, Atlanta** — left at nineteen and made leaving her whole personality; three grown kids (work, the Army, the trades — no college, which is what makes Keisha *first*); one call a month, mutual respect at a fixed distance; knows less than nothing about the money and would be loudest at learning it. **Marcus, 55, Omaha** — the Sunday-at-4:00 call, eleven minutes, every week for thirty years; two school-age kids whose 529s run as silently as everyone's; knows nothing. **Denise, 52** — the one who stayed; a toddler when Nate died, no memory of her father; the child Estelle raised hardest and taught least. Six grandchildren in all.
- **Body:** the plant is written on her — thick knuckles, old knife scars, hands always cold from forty years on a refrigerated floor, a grip that still surprises people. Moves deliberately; sits like sitting is a decision.
- **Daily geometry:** fixed orbits — Tuesday senior center, Thursday the downtown library where she has read the business pages in print for fifty years and the librarians hold her chair, Saturday estate sales (the collection's supply chain), Sunday church with Denise. The fixed orbits are the vulnerability — the senior center is where Gary found her.
- **Voice register:** business-page vocabulary in plant-floor syntax — *that fund's carrying a load like a bad mule; an expense ratio will eat you quiet, like damp.* She can talk about money the way other women talk about weather — but only to herself, until Gary (the trap) and the coda (the cure).
- **Comic register — the provenance set:** every object in the garage gets its price, its provenance, and one withering line about the fool who sold it; entry price public, valuation secret, the whole character in a storytelling tic. Haggles at other people's sales for sport. Owns the book's best needle (demonstrated in Keisha's heron runner). The find stories are her displaced autobiography and, it turns out, the teaching all along.
- **The collector:** fifty years of thrift-store finds curated in the garage like a museum. She has never once held a sale — until page one. She cannot let go of what she holds: not the finds, not the certificate, not the truth.
- **The chain (her object):** 1984 bought → 1987 pawned → 1989 redeemed, never worn again → ~2015 given to Denise, its history untold. What happens to it in 2025 is spine business. **The lesson it carries:** gold is not how you grow money; it is how you *hold* money when institutions won't hold it for you — the bank that can't refuse you.
- **The refused name:** she never let anyone call her Grandma — grandmas were old women, and she was busy. She is Stell or Miss Estelle; "Essie" is reserved, said by her exactly once, inside her own story. Keisha's unadorned "thanks, Grandma" at the loan signing is the payoff.
- **Non-thematic want (comic runner):** the Canaries. She keeps score by hand at Sioux Falls Canaries home games — a perfect hand-ruled scorebook, decades of them — and is at war with the new scoreboard, which is frequently wrong and never sorry. Numbers as pure pleasure, no lesson. (Garden texture, not a runner: the annual failed campaign to grow her mother's Arkansas beefsteak tomato in a South Dakota yard.)
- **Dyads:** *Denise* — the nagging about the garage sale is their whole relationship in miniature: Denise pushes, Estelle permits, neither says why. *Keisha* — the only grandchild who listens to the provenance stories all the way through; that is why she is the one in the garage. *The stand* — 30% silent partner who is not silent: drops by to "just visit" and recounts the cash box, which after JazzFest stops being a joke.
- **Guardrails:** the collection is curated, never squalor — and it is **never the piggy bank**; the gap closes through the 529 and labor, full stop. Her vulnerability to Gary is not intelligence — it's fifty years of being unlistened-to.
- **Secret ledger (what she hides, from whom, revealed when):** the total — never spoken to anyone, said once, at the table; the 529 system and the four-year plan — revealed at the shame visit; the chain's 1987 story — the dinner's final blow; the garage sale as Gary's planted idea — found by Keisha in the paperwork; the fairness rule for the other five grandchildren — said at the table, the sentence that turns the reveal from favoritism into a system.
- **Money wound:** once erased, she built a life around never needing anyone again.
- **Right about:** visible money attracts predators — the book proves her right three times (the settlement man, the 1976 salesman, Gary). **Wrong about:** the armor outlived its war; by 2025 hiding is what makes her huntable, and survival is not the same as living.
- **Arc:** from hiding as safety to teaching money out loud, twice in one week. Understood, not absolved; she does not apologize well, and the book does not make her.

### Denise Carter, 52 — mother, the middle strand

Black; youngest of Estelle's three; a toddler when Nate died — she has no memory of her father, only the stories other people ration. Washington High to bagger at the same grocery store in 1991, and she out-worked everyone up to shift supervisor: thirty-four years, one store, honest work that never compounds. Married Terrence "Terry" Boyd in 2003; never took his name and gave Keisha hers — a fact nobody in the family has ever explained out loud. **The 2015 collision (a feature):** the divorce, the promotion, the chain, and the envelope system all land in the same year — she rebuilt herself around being excellent at work and airtight at home, which is exactly the woman who takes every Friday shift. Estelle marked the promotion with the gold chain, its history untold. Runs the household on labeled cash envelopes — GROCERIES, LIGHTS, CAR, SCHOOL — and is flatly excellent at every dollar she can hold; she taught Keisha the cash-drawer liturgy (count it twice, band the twenties, the way she closes the store every night).

**Her money physics — perfect defense, no offense:** every dollar labeled, guarded, spent on purpose, and none of it ever compounding. A brilliant goalie on a team with no forwards. The mechanisms of paycheck-to-paycheck, none of them flaws:

- **One paycheck since 2015;** child support ended in 2024 when Keisha turned eighteen — this summer is her first year running the household on her paycheck alone, her thinnest margin ever.
- **She rents** — a check every month of her adult life, in the family where her mother burned the mortgage in 1998. The down payment never happened; the reader can do the math on whose silence that is.
- **The 401(k) she never joined:** the store has one, with a match. Enrollment was a portal, a form, a decision nobody ever sat down and explained — her systems gap, twenty years before the bursar's portal. Free money left on the table since the nineties, and she does not know that is what it was. (What the family ledger does about it is coda business.)
- **The thin file:** no credit card ever means no credit history. The car loan, years back, came at a brutal rate — paid off early, of course; deposits run larger; the system reads her discipline as risk. Avoiding credit is not free.

- **The pride:** *no credit card debt, not one dime, not once* — and she is **right**: she has watched paycheck advances and minimum payments eat her coworkers for thirty years, front-row seat. The book validates the discipline and quietly prices it. **Standing rule (the Sheldon card):** the plot is allowed to price her rule once, hard — and no character ever states the counterfactual; only the reader does that math. Twin of Terry's would-he-have-helped.
- **Money wound:** being asked reveals you failed to anticipate — need discovered is competence disproven. Her account ends every month at zero and she calls that **"even."** Breaking even as identity: pride and prison in one word.
- **Right about:** debt eats people, and discipline is real protection — Sydney's balance and thirty years of coworkers prove it. **Wrong about:** that safety is the same as standing still — perfect defense doesn't score, guarded cash still shrinks, and the system charges you for the credit you never used.
- **The gap:** what she lacks is not sense but systems literacy — bursars, portals, aid letters, anything with a login — the structural condition of a first-generation college parent, and never a punchline. Nobody taught her, because Estelle never taught anyone.
- **Adaptation:** her lifelong habit is not-asking; performed certainty is her camouflage. She believes a competent mother anticipates need without making her child ask.
- **Body and props:** a fast walker who has been on her feet since 1991 — good shoes resoled, never replaced; reads a room's mood the way she reads a register tape. Props: the envelopes, the chain on important workdays, the store keys she has carried so long the lanyard is store history.
- **Voice register — supervisor-speak, everywhere:** she runs the household like a shift — *I need a decision on this hotdish by four; that's not my area, but I'll cover it.* Tics: **"we're even"** and **"I don't owe anybody a dime"** — her recurring self-benediction, detonated at the dinner when she learns what being owed nothing cost her mother to arrange and her daughter to imitate.
- **Comic register — deadpan store sitcom:** the store is a workplace comedy she narrates with total flatness — the produce guy's feuds, the register-3 curse, the employee who quits every Friday and is rehired every Monday. Her comedy is competence under absurdity; the reader laughs *with* her authority, never at her gap.
- **Non-thematic want (comic runner):** the church potluck. A decades-long, never-acknowledged hotdish rivalry with Sister Deloris Hayes — first pan emptied wins; everyone knows it, no one says it — competition in the family's native language. No money, no lesson; last beat reserved for the coda.
- **Private want:** she has protected Keisha's late-August move-in day on the store calendar. More than making a payment, she wants to be included in her daughter's return to Brookings, and cannot quite say so.
- **Dyads:** *Estelle* — the nagging about the garage sale is their whole relationship: Denise pushes, Estelle permits, neither says why; she wears her mother's chain without its story. *Keisha* — the liturgy is their shared language: they can count money together in perfect sync and cannot discuss it at all. *Terry* — spoken of rarely and never badly; "your father" in logistics-voice only; the divorce ended in silence and is maintained in silence. *Charlene* — approves loudly from Atlanta and does nothing; it was Charlene who lectured about "making Mama downsize," and Denise who spent a year actually doing it. *Marcus* — the family group text's weatherman: present, pleasant, no information exchanged. *Lourdes* — three doors down, two money-culture matriarchs, envelopes versus liquidity; they trade favors and never money, and audit each other's daughters with mutual respect. *Emily* — the girl whose count she trusts
- **Secret ledger (hides / hidden from her):** she hides the June portal login, the Friday-shift rescue, and the chain sale — all held until the dinner. Hidden from her: Estelle's fortune, the chain's 1987 history, the 529 system, and the true shape of Keisha's summer — the solitary rescue, the account, the crash — until the dinner. The 401(k) match is the one secret nobody was keeping — the system just never introduced itself.
- **The runner:** she keeps meaning to visit the stand and never does — she's the supervisor covering everyone else's Friday. The one visit she finally makes is spine business (one cup, full price).
- **Her timeline:** 1973, born. 1975, Nate dies. 1991, bagger at eighteen. 2003, marries Terry. 2006, Keisha. 2015, the collision — divorce, promotion, the chain, and the labeled envelopes appearing in the kitchen drawer that fall. 2024, child support ends. June 2025, the portal (spine business from here).
- **Arc:** from anticipating in silence to asking and staying in the answer. The small, concrete 529 question at the climax demonstrates that asking can be survived; her anger at Estelle demonstrates that speaking does not require instant forgiveness.




# Max Ellis, 24 — fast-money foil, light love interest

White; Sydney's brother, three years older; the Ellis kids grew up four blocks over, family friends since grade school. Born spring 2001; Washington High class of 2019 (gone before Keisha and Emily arrived — he's neighborhood, not school); one semester at Southeast Tech he ghosted by October, and nobody made him go back. Hired in the 2020 pandemic surge at a third-party distribution warehouse out by the interstate — 6:00 a.m. weekday shift, a scanner that tells him where to walk, fourteen miles a day, and a metric board that ranks every picker by percentile. He is top-ten-percent at the job and hates that he's good at it, because the board is the whole problem in one screen: the shift genuinely runs whether he's on it or not, and the shift *knows*. Lives in the Ellis basement, $200 a month to his mother, which is how a warehouse wage produced five basement years of savings — the stake he'll burn in August. (Exact stake and crash anatomy live in the money fact-sheet, not here.)

**The Ellis household — money as event:** Rick Ellis put in floors for thirty years, self-employed, feast and famine, and only the feasts became stories. The founding legend is **the boat year** — one fat season (~2012), a used Lund, three summers on Lake Madison, endless narration — and the boat's quiet sale in a lean year, which is never mentioned. Pam Ellis kept the actual books the whole time and was never once the story. Both kids learned the same lesson and answered it differently: money arrives in *moments*, the moments are the only part anyone tells, and the years in between are silence. Sydney recreates the moment by spending it; Max hunts the moment that will end the in-between. Same wound, sibling variants. **Guardrail:** Pam's ledger stays texture — she never becomes a plot device or a late reveal.

**His secret origin (the three weeks):** January 2021, age nineteen — stimulus check plus savings into a meme stock, tripled in three weeks, told everyone, screenshotted everything. Rode it back down past even by April; net minus $400; told no one, ever. He is not chasing money. He is chasing the three weeks — the only time in his life the number on the board meant he mattered. The 2025 conviction ticker is the three weeks trying to happen again, and this time it starts by working. (The reader who holds this card watches his "winning streak in a confident voice" knowing he has already round-tripped once.)

- **Body:** 6'1", warehouse shoulders, a right knee that already complains at 24; steel-toes by the basement door; one AirPod perpetually in (markets podcast, or nothing — nobody checks); phone battery always at 9% from ticker-checking; the Ellis grin, the family's actual currency — Sydney runs on the same one.
- **Prop — the camera roll:** a survivorship-bias museum. Every green day screenshotted, annotated, ready to show; no red day photographed in his life. The one green screenshot at the garage sale is curation, not luck. (The minus-$400 spring of 2021 exists in no archive anywhere.)
- **Comic register — bro-quant, applied to everything:** everything is a play, a position, a thesis. Cup sizes at the stand are *entry, growth, and moon*; on hearing of Sofi's royalty: *founder behavior, respect*; the stand is *pre-revenue* even when it's revenue; flirting is *initiating a position*. His retirement number is $1.5 million by thirty-five, except when it's $1.2, except when it's $2 — it changes every time he says it, and when Emily asks to see the math he changes the subject. **The tell:** the register drops when he means something. Bro-voice is a costume he only wears where it might work — he never talks like this at the warehouse. The apology fails precisely because he retreats *into* register when the moment needed him out of it.
- **Money physics — all offense, no defense:** the true inverse of Estelle — *she does money brilliantly and cannot speak it; he speaks money constantly and does it terribly.* (The Ortiz kitchen owns sound practice *and* plain speech; what Max owns is fluency without flinch.) He is the most casual money-talker in Keisha's orbit — the one person who can hear "$7,800" as information instead of shame, because in his family you can say any number out loud. That is the whole mechanism of the pressure valve, on the terms the book already set: he won't worry, won't sacrifice, won't tell her mother. And he keeps the secret — even from Sydney, which in the Ellis house is actual effort — not because secrecy is his instinct but because he clocks that the Carters run different rules and honors them without understanding them. His one act of discipline in the entire book, and it's load-bearing.
- **The mechanics (hands clean by design):** he teaches her to open her own brokerage account and mirror his trades — her money, her thumb, her loss; no handover, no debt, no borrowing, ever. He flinches once at the account-opening — *I'm not, like, a fiduciary* — a joke that is also the truth, and she overrides it. **His one flinch of doctrine is saved for the Act 4 press:** when she adds the approved stand advance to the position, even Max hesitates — *desperation money never wins* — a gambler's proverb, genuinely meant, that he has never once followed himself. It lands as the third warning in a row (after Emily's triptych and Sydney's), it is ignored like the others, and it buys him the one decent thing in his bad apology: he has an *I told you* available, and never uses it.
- **The stand:** his usefulness is the hatchback and his back — ice runs, coolers, folding tables, the 6 a.m. shift leaving market Saturdays and Levitt nights free. The girls pay him in agua fresca and mockery; he never asks for a cut, partly because he doesn't value stand-scale money and partly because equity would make him a partner — and the stand's cap table (the 3–1 vote, Estelle's 30%, Sofi's royalty) stays girls-and-family only. His self-image contribution is market wisdom; his actual contribution is cargo space. The gap is the comedy, and everyone but Max can see it.
- **Money wound:** slow money means being nobody — the board ranks him, the shift runs without him, and no one in his family ever told a story about a wage. Steady money is invisible money, and invisible is dead.
- **Right about:** hours-for-dollars alone can't close every gap; ownership and compounding are real levers the Carter women can't see (he is the corrupted introduction to Keisha's third money physics); and saying numbers out loud is normal — the skill the Carter house lacks is the one he was born with. **Wrong about:** speed — that the lever must be pulled hard and all at once; that conviction is analysis; that boredom is death; that because the market rewarded him once, it *saw* him.
- **Secret ledger (hides / hidden from him):** he hides the 2021 round trip — from everyone, forever; the reader holds it, no character ever does. He hides Keisha's $7,800 from his own family, Sydney included. Hidden from him: the entire Carter plot — the fortune, the 529s, the chain, the dinner, Gary — plus two things the book never tells him: that the Vibe is the best-managed holding he owns, and that Dale is the retirement plan working.
- **The dyads:** *Sydney* — mirror siblings; he roasts her balance, she roasts his tickers, and each defends the other to anyone outside the family. Her mid-streak warning to Keisha is also, unspoken, the first time Sydney ever bet against her brother's entire religion — sibling stakes under the friendship beat. *Keisha* — she is the first person who ever argued with him instead of eating the bro-talk or dismissing it; she rates his claims on invented axes and he finds it electric. The flirtation is denominated entirely in valuation jokes, and it ends where the book prices it: a boy at correct valuation, a laugh across a split check. *Emily* — post-crash, his public bottom is asking her, in front of everyone, how to open a boring index fund; she hands him a one-page setup with an absurd filename, he starts calling her *my CFO*, and the account holds $85 by the coda while she quietly keeps auditing. Comic, warm, and the male mirror of the book's lesson: shame survives being public. *Dale* — offstage, the warehouse: ~58, thirty years in distribution, paid-off truck, talks about his 401(k) match the way other men talk about fishing. Max files him under *cautionary tale* and is wrong in a way the book never corrects out loud; Dale gets no reveal scene, ever.
- **Non-thematic want (comic runner):** the Pontiac — his grandfather's 2004 Vibe, 190,000 miles, the stand's logistics fleet. He wages an annual autumn war on rust, changes the oil early, and haggles junkyard part prices with genuine skill (his one real edge, and nobody notices it's Estelle's edge too — she works estate sales, he works pull-a-part lots). He dreams out loud about the Porsche at thirty-five while waxing a car worth $2,100. **Last beat folded into the coda's split-check scene, not a new beat:** someone has offered to buy the Vibe; he mentions declining, without hesitation, mid-pitch for the new ticker — held for love, not thesis. No character observes the contradiction. Only the reader does that math.
- **The crash and after:** wiped out on the page; crawls back to the warehouse he swore he'd escaped and doesn't die of it; opens the boring account publicly — **and doesn't really learn:** by the coda he has "diversified" into a new conviction ticker, boring account holding $85, camera roll already curating the next museum. Between-cycle texture: a crypto app deleted in the 2022 winter, reinstalled in the 2024 thaw — the dormant years were cycles, not cures. His growth is $85 deep and his function is complete anyway: what Keisha needed to *see* was someone fail in public and remain a person.
- **The apology:** bad — vague, weather-ish (*rough summer, market-wise*), register instead of substance, and no *I told you* even though he's owed one. She forgives him anyway, having learned that waiting for the perfect apology is one more way of not moving.
- **Screen map (planned, like Gary's):** eight touches — the garage sale; the valve; the account opening; boom-season background at the stand; the JazzFest night in the crowd; the press flinch; the red morning and the public bottom; the coda check. Then nothing during the freeze: **his absence from the page is Act 4's loneliness engine** — cutting him off is how Keisha ends up with no one, so the drafting rule is that he vanishes completely between the freeze and the coda.
- **Boundaries (hard):** he stays entirely outside the family plot, forever — never learns about the fortune, the 529s, the chain, the dinner, or Gary. His Carter knowledge tops out at: the $7,800, whatever the whole market witnessed at JazzFest, and the crash he can infer because the trade was his. He never touches her money in any direction. He is never villainous and never pathetic — not a rung on the predator ladder (that ladder is Gary's; Max is its counterweight, the man in the money story who doesn't price women at scrap), not redeemed, just priced. He and Estelle share no written exchange after the garage sale — background co-presence at the stand is fine; dialogue is not. **Estelle prices him exactly once, at the dinner, inside her existing needle:** *you told the Ellis boy — all ticker, no tape, that one — before your own mother.* (Placed there because her market fluency is minutes from full disclosure anyway; anywhere earlier, the line breaks her only-to-herself rule.) The romance never rises above check-splitting altitude: no label, no declaration, warm and undefined.
- **His timeline:** spring 2001, born. ~2012, the boat year — the summer that calibrated him. 2019, graduates Washington High; the ghosted Southeast Tech semester. 2020, the warehouse. Jan–Apr 2021, the three weeks and the untold round trip. 2021–2024, dormant: the crypto app, deleted and reinstalled by cycle. Spring 2025, the new ticker; June 2025, one green screenshot at a garage sale.
- **Arc (a flatline, on purpose):** from needing money to be a story… to needing money to be a story, with a public dip in the middle. The book lets him not change — he survives failure visibly, which is his gift to Keisha, and resumes the hunt, which is his truth. Keisha's arc does the revaluing for both of them: what changes is not Max, but what he costs.


# Sydney Ellis, 21 — chaos engine

White; Max's sister, three years younger; the Ellis kids grew up four blocks over, family friends since grade school. Washington High class of 2022, then a nail-tech program at Stewart School left half-finished — undramatically: the schedule collided with the job that paid *now*, and she doesn't mourn it. Sales floor at an apparel chain in the Empire Mall, where the employee discount is a standing temptation and the wardrobe expectation is spending that is secretly a job requirement. One roommate, real rent, real bills, a credit-card balance — broke in a way nobody names, least of all her. She was the designated older kid of the block — thirteen watching eleven reads as babysitting to the eleven-year-old — which is how she became Keisha's first Levitt show, her parallel-parking teacher, and her standing permission to have fun.

- **The Ellis money culture:** see Max's profile — same boat-year lesson, opposite answer: he hunts the moment that will end the in-between; she recreates it by spending it. Mirror siblings: they roast each other's version and defend each other to anyone outside the family. Her mid-streak warning to Keisha is, unspoken, the first time she ever bet against her brother's entire religion.
- **Money wound:** an unnarrated life doesn't count. In a house where only the feasts became stories, she buys the moments that make the week tellable — and her superpower is the mechanism: she reads every mood in the room first, feels the night dying before anyone else, and buys the round that saves it. The balance is built of both kinds of purchase — the good seats and the picked-up checks, the festival weekends and the exactly-right gifts — a statement that reads like a scrapbook, where other people's evenings are itemized. (The figure lives in the money fact-sheet, not here.)
- **Comic register — justification arithmetic (never named, not once, by anyone):** cost-per-wear, cost-per-memory, *it was basically free because I skipped lunch twice* — pseudo-finance with its own emotional logic, the Ellis dialect in its second grammar: Max's version exists to sound smart, hers to feel licensed. **Guardrail:** her math is occasionally *right* — cost-per-wear on her work boots genuinely pencils — so the tic reads as a reasoning style, never a deficiency the book smirks at.
- **Body:** the Ellis grin, the family currency; nails as rotating gallery; dressed a notch above every occasion because the job requires it and the wound prefers it.
- **The nails:** the half-certificate left her one real skill — elaborate sets, done free, for the whole block. Generosity in her native currency without a receipt: the one form of giving that never touches the balance, sitting in plain sight, unremarked. **Guardrails:** nobody ever suggests she charge, and she never invoices — she cheered Sofi's move and knows it isn't hers.
- **Right about:** joy is real value and money spent on people isn't wasted — her taste is real, her gifts land, and the value discovery (the Levitt crowds, the stand's best business decision) belongs to the person everyone calls irresponsible. **Wrong about:** that unsaid costs stay unpaid — the balance compounds whether or not it's narrated, and proof-of-existence can't be purchased on credit.
- **Non-thematic want (comic runner):** the cat — a stray tom of ambiguous ownership who works the block and loves everyone on it, Max, Keisha, Sofi, the mail carrier — except Sydney, two summers into a public, escalating, increasingly researched campaign. The block's best mood-reader, illegible to one animal; costs nothing, teaches nothing, hands Max his best roasting lane. Last beat reserved for the coda, folded into an existing scene.
- **Dyads:** *Max* — same wound, opposite answers (see above). *Keisha* — the standing contract: Keisha is her designated adult, she is Keisha's permission to have fun, which is why the warning lands as a role reversal Keisha can't file. *Emily* — opposite numbers, front-of-house and back-of-house: one money culture performs, one never speaks, both broke in ways nobody names; each privately thinks the other is the brave one. *Estelle* — the book's two object-lovers, the holder and the circulator; Estelle rates her purchases in appraisal register (*you overpaid, but the cut's good*) — object talk, which is public Estelle, never market talk, which isn't; the one young person she grades on judgment rather than thrift.
- **Boundary:** outside the family money plot, forever — she knows about the trading (it's semi-public among the girls) but never learns the $7,800, so her warning is made blind: she recognizes the behavior, not the stakes, because gambling for a feeling is her own purchase order. That blindness is what makes it the brave version.
- **Arc:** from spending to prove value to choosing joy with eyes open — the appetite stays; only the accounting changes. The priced choice (the Premier Center ticket sold in front of everyone, the card paid down, mourned a little) takes the loss on her own category, not on gifts — which is what makes it Keisha's dress rehearsal for costs said out loud. The book is not anti-spending; Sydney is why.


# Emily Larsen, 18 — math brain

White; Norwegian-Lutheran, fourth-generation Sioux Falls; only child; class of 2025 at Washington High — graduated the same June this book opens, one grade behind Keisha, best friends since homework partnerships at the branch library: one girl escaping a house money-silent under constant noise, the other a house silent all the way down. Bound for Augustana in the fall — the Lutheran college across town, the default nobody ever discussed, expensive in the exact solvent-seeming way. Her father has spent twenty-some years in card operations at one of the downtown banks, moving other people's balances all day for a living; her mother runs the hotdish economy, concern always correctly calibrated, always arriving as food, never as a sentence. The Larsen home is kind, warm, and has never once discussed money out loud.

- **The calibrating event (her secret):** the culture is the soil — money-modesty as virtue, exactness as vulgarity — but one night, maybe ten years old, she overheard her parents say a single exact figure to each other through a door, once, and the house went cold for a season without anyone acknowledging why. The lesson took: a named number is a detonation; precision is what the silence protects everyone *from*. She fled into math because numbers everywhere else are safe.
- **Money wound (canon):** exact truth feels more dangerous than guessing — she trusts numbers except the one that belongs to her. Materially: her own account — wages from weekday shifts as a page at the same branch library, saved against a first Augustana bill whose exact shortfall she has never let herself compute, because an exact number would force a conversation her family's culture cannot hold. (Anatomy in the money fact-sheet.)
- **The prop:** the cracked phone, kept face-down, holding the banking app she refuses to open — until she quietly does, on her own terms, weeks before anyone notices. Why the door was shut is above; when it opens is spine business.
- **The stand:** she keeps the books — supply math, projections, the take — back-of-house to Sydney's front, and the reason the money is always exactly known even when nobody wants to know it. After JazzFest, the two-person count is hers: procedure is her native form of care.
- **Comic register — precision, deadpan, misapplied:** the filenames (every spreadsheet named like something between a doom prophecy and a shrug — *stand_math_do_not_perceive.xlsx*); the flat forecast (any trend extended to its absurd conclusion in a monotone, the room left to decide if she's joking); significant figures on things that don't deserve them (the drive to Brookings is *48.7 minutes, conditions permitting*). **Guardrail:** her math is never wrong — only the occasion is. **Lane marker:** Keisha quantifies what can't be measured (armor); Emily measures what needn't be (pleasure) — and, when asked, what must.
- **Teaching register (how the math gets said):** she volunteers *instruments*, never explanations — the artifact goes on the table wordlessly, and words come only when asked. Answers arrive one sentence at a time, in the asker's own units: to Keisha, a fee is *forty fryer-minutes a year, forever, for nothing*; to Denise, a match is *a raise you have to walk over and pick up*; to Sydney, interest is *the ticket you're still buying in March*. A second sentence must be requested. Never *you should* — she prices; the other person decides. (Sample register only; drafted lines must pencil, per the guardrail. Lanes kept: Estelle's money metaphors come from her own life; Emily's come from yours.)
- **Body:** still, watchful, economical; a mechanical pencil behaving like a sixth finger; library posture in every chair; burns, never tans, and treats sunscreen as a scheduled event. Speaks late in any conversation and lands the sentence that reorganizes it. The face-down phone is the one tell that reads as a habit and is actually a wound.
- **Right about:** measurement is protection — an uncounted number is where the trouble hides, and her instruments are the book's cleanest sight-lines. **Wrong about:** that it runs both ways — that a number unlooked-at stays smaller. She applies to herself the one method she'd never accept in a spreadsheet.
- **Non-thematic want (comic runner):** the Norwegian streak — a 400-something-day language-app streak in the heritage language nobody in her family has spoken for two generations, campaign target: one full sentence exchanged with Great-aunt Solveig. Discovered mid-campaign: Solveig knows only table grace and three words about weather. Precision versus an unquantifiable target; costs nothing, teaches nothing. Last beat reserved for the coda.
- **Dyads:** *Keisha* — the branch-library bond; the person Keisha texts first, and the one friendship where both silences understand each other without approving. *Sydney* — opposite numbers, front-of-house and back-of-house: one money culture performs, one never speaks; each privately thinks the other is the brave one. *Max* — his post-crash "CFO," reluctantly; she audits, he orbits. *Denise* — recognition across a generation, register tape to spreadsheet: the two disciplines in the book that trust a second count.
- **Arc:** from guarding the number to knowing it — the door opens quietly, unannounced, on her own terms. Where Keisha's cure is saying the number out loud at a table, Emily's is allowed to stop at *looking* at it: the same disease, cured at a volume true to the patient.
- **Guardrails:** the Larsen parents stay kind and offstage; *solvent-seeming* remains genuinely ambiguous forever — the inheritance is the silence, not a hidden crisis. The overheard figure is never identified, amount or cause. Her Augustana bill never gets a scene — the stray transfer is its only surface — so her thread never competes with Keisha's. And nobody ever diagnoses Emily on the page: the cracked phone, the filenames, and two lines of backstory are the entire case file.


# Sofía "Sofi" Ortiz, 16 — the watched one

Mexican-American; the Ortiz family has lived three doors down since before she was born, family in everything but blood; a junior-to-be at Washington. Her mother is Lourdes; her father works the Smithfield floor — the same plant, two generations after Estelle's forty-eight years, a fact no character ever remarks on — and is the book's one contented working man seen at his own table: warm, present, minor. Her brother is **Diego, 10**. Sofi has translated, interpreted, and helped for free her whole life — forms, phone calls, front desks — and supplies the agua fresca recipe (jamaica, plus a rotating second flavor that is her call, weekly, hers alone): page one is her pitcher, carried over for the sweating sorting crew, and the stand is born in one collective gulp.

- **The calibrating event (shared with Lourdes):** the frozen account — three weeks, four trips, one fee, zero apologies — with Sofi, nine, doing the talking, because the bank listened to the child in English before the woman with the documents. Two lessons took at once: institutions must be read word by word, and the work she did in those rooms was real work nobody counted. Lourdes got her receipts; Sofi got her meter.
- **Money wound (soft, already healing):** the family-obligation economy — help that's expected is help that's invisible; labor-as-love keeps no ledger. Hers is the one wound in the book caught early: the invoice is the cure in progress, and it costs her real nerve — billing the people you love, at sixteen, in a house where you don't — which is what keeps her the book's hope instead of its prodigy.
- **The invoice (canon, and the whole character in one act):** she recognizes unpaid value faster than the older girls because she has been unpaid value her whole life. The recipe is the first contribution anyone tried to put a number on; she puts the number on it herself. The amendment gives her a per-batch royalty and a vote, and the recipe never transfers with the business — it is hers. (Rate lives in the money fact-sheet.)
- **Comic register — cheerful out-loud pricing:** she names the value of labor as she performs it, sunnily, no resentment attached — *that's a four-dollar favor; the good-neighbor rate is two; translating for the cable guy is eight but Mom gets the family plan.* **The tell:** she quotes constantly and has never once collected — the invoice is the first quote she enforces, which is why it lands serious in a comic register. **Lane markers:** Keisha reads systems' fine print; Emily prices concepts, when asked; Sofi prices labor, unprompted — including her own, which is the part nobody taught her.
- **Secret ledger: none.** In a book of secret ledgers, hers is the only open book. That is not a gap; it is the point.
- **Body and prop:** always has a pen — nobody registers why until the invoice. Otherwise: the pitcher, the condensation, the permit manual studied soft at the spine, a girl who stands like she's next in line for something.
- **Right about:** love and labor can share a ledger — naming a price is not a betrayal, and the book's youngest character is the first to prove it. **Wrong about (small, age-true):** she thinks everything valuable can be priced. The permit denial is just the joke of a girl who prices everything meeting a mother who won't name a price — friction, not lesson.
- **Non-thematic want (comic runner):** the learner's permit — the manual studied like scripture, practice tests perfect, the request denied casually and without reasons (*when you're ready*, from a woman who won't say ready for what). **Guardrail:** the denial is never about money. Last beat already placed in the coda: the errand, next on the list.
- **Diego, 10:** hers to tutor at the same kitchen table where Keisha once taught her long division — the pattern traveling one more step. He runs a trading-card economy of genuine sophistication that no adult audits. **The unstated observation:** at the age Sofi was translating in bank lobbies, Diego is trading cards at a profit — nobody has ever expected his help, so nobody has ever failed to pay for it. No character says this. **Guardrails:** offstage-to-light, never plot.
- **Dyads:** *Keisha* — long division then, invoices now; the reversal is the point, and Sofi keeps it light because Keisha is the one who feels it. *Lourdes* — the invoice is their negotiation of love's terms, settled the moment the guardian pen makes her mother read every line: watched by the person she watched over first. *Diego* — the table, one generation on.
- **Arc (the smallest in the book, on purpose):** from watched to watcher. She doesn't heal — she was never fully broken; she absorbs. A royalty, a vote, a custodial account, the pattern learned by proximity — and the final image is hers: watching the car go the way Keisha once watched Estelle. The family disease passes down still, but the strain she inherits already talks.
- **Guardrails:** never precocious-cute — her competence is earned in specified rooms, not innate. Her function is the fourth generation by proximity, not blood — the book's pattern travels through anyone paying attention.

# Lourdes Ortiz, ~40s — Sofi's mother, counter-wisdom

Mexican-American; three doors down; married to a contented Smithfield man, mother of Sofi and Diego. Cash, caution, liquidity, family obligation — a money culture with receipts, not folklore. One account for what requires an account; cash for everything that doesn't; and a favor economy she runs the way Estelle runs a portfolio, because it *is* her portfolio: help placed with the right families, compounding for decades, redeemable in kind.

- **The calibrating event (shared with Sofi, told once, never as a lesson):** the frozen account — a mismatched middle name, three weeks, four trips, one fee, zero apologies — with her nine-year-old doing the talking, because the bank listened to the child in English before the woman with the documents. Her distrust has a paper trail.
- **The Estelle rhyme (owned, never staged):** the book's two armored women took the same wound — being un-listened-to by institutions — and built opposite fortresses: Estelle hid *inside* the system, paper and funds and certificates; Lourdes stays *outside* it, cash and kin. They share no scene and never compare fortresses. The reader does.
- **Money wound:** once charged a fee for being herself, she keeps her money where it can't be misspelled.
- **Right about:** liquidity is real safety, institutions bill the unlucky, and an obligation network is wealth — hers has never frozen, never charged a fee, never asked for a middle name. **Wrong about:** a fortress outside the system is invisible to it — cash melts quietly and favors don't compound into a transcript. The book never corrects her on the page; her one concession is made on her own terms, pen in hand, every line read first.
- **Voice register — the favor ledger:** she carries every favor ever traded, both directions, to the day, and repays in kind with exact interest — never money. Trade favors, never money; audit each other's daughters with mutual respect: the Denise treaty, in force for years, is her diplomacy in miniature.
- **The two canon gestures:** the invoice — argues love's terms (*in this family you do not bill the people you love*), then, handed the guardian pen, reads every line before signing: both halves true at once, and the second is where Sofi comes from. JazzFest — she saw the surge and grabbed the box, pure competence; the consequence came from nobody passing the word: the family disease, demonstrated for one night in the counter-wisdom house too.
- **Dyads:** *Denise* — two money-culture matriarchs, envelopes versus liquidity, mutual respect at three doors' distance. *Sofi* — watched over first, auditing now; the pen scene is the whole relationship settling its terms. *Estelle* — deliberately none.
- **Guardrails:** never backward, never folklore, never a lesson — her system works on the page, visibly, all summer, and the one time it's priced (the coda's custodial account) it's priced by her own hand. Warm to the girls, exact with everyone.

### Gary — the advisor

*Sunrise Retirement Solutions.* Pleasant, patient, remembers Nate's name, laughs at Estelle's jokes; found her through the senior-center channel and planted the "simplify your estate" idea behind the garage sale Denise thinks she won.

- **What he knows:** not the number — he's a volume fisherman working the senior-center channel, and a homeowning widow with fifty years of tenure is simply the standard profile he nets. That ignorance is what keeps him Tuesday-sized.
- **The contract:** surrender charges, a 7% commission dressed in jargon, a signing "incentive" expiring on a circled date — legitimate advice never comes with a countdown.
- **Screen time:** three scenes total, no raised voice; defeated by being *read*, and leaves politely, because his species always does.
- **The ladder:** fourth rung of the predator ladder — the 1975 settlement man, the 1976 fund salesman, the concessionaire, Gary: four men across six decades pricing women's work and money at scrap value.
- **Scale:** Tuesday-sized, always — real financial predation is boring and patient, and depicting it accurately is the lesson.

### The concessionaire

Food-truck owner, liked by the girls all summer; offers $600 for booth and "brand" — nine days after an anonymous permit complaint nobody can pin on him. Shrugs when asked: "that's business." Buys a cup. Stays a market fixture; hints at the good corner for next June. One shade dark, no darker.
