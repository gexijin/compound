# The Thirty Percent — A Novel

## Book Plan v11

**Title:** *The Thirty Percent*
**Category:** Adult commercial fiction with book-club appeal
**Primary readership:** women 18–35, purchased from the adult shelf
**Target length:** 78,000–85,000 words
**Structure:** approximately 28 short chapters in three acts
**Narration:** first-person Keisha, present tense, comic, no hindsight

**Pitch-language note:** The book should not be pitched as “financial literacy” or “New Adult.” The educational intent is real, but the reader-facing promise is story, voice, family, friendship, shame, romance, and secrets.

---

## One-page pitch summary

Keisha, nineteen, is $2,800 short of returning to college. The number is small enough that one honest conversation might solve it, which is exactly why she cannot say it out loud. Instead, she builds a careful little machine of lies: archived emails, forwarded old paperwork, a housing deposit she cannot afford, and a bag of dorm bedding for a room she may never occupy.

To hide from the problem, Keisha spends the summer helping her grandmother Elena clear out decades of receipts and boxes. Then Elena stakes Keisha and her friends in an agua fresca stand: thirty dollars for thirty percent. What begins as a joke business becomes real enough to expose everyone. Maya wants money to make life bigger now. Emily can calculate anything except her own fear. Sofi, sixteen, wants her family recipe recognized as hers. Marcus, Maya’s brother, wants to hit it big and retire by thirty-five. And Elena, who seems merely frugal and secretive, has been quietly wealthy for years.

By August, the stand has taught the girls profit, ownership, labor, risk, and the danger of fast money. But the deeper lesson is not financial. It is emotional: every woman in Keisha’s family has been hiding something. Elena hid a fortune. Keisha’s mother hid what she was never taught. Keisha hid $2,800. Sofi is watching all of them.

The ending is hopeful but not tidy. Keisha returns to school at a visible cost: a campus job, loan payments, and next year’s gap already named. The family pattern has not vanished, but it has been interrupted.

---

## Core promise

A funny, big-hearted novel about three broke young women, one summer business, and the grandmother whose secret fortune forces every woman in the family to stop hiding.

---

## Logline

Keisha, nineteen, is $2,800 short of returning to college and has built an entire architecture of lies to avoid saying so — to her mother, her friends, and herself. When her grandmother Elena stakes Keisha and her friends in an agua fresca stand for thirty dollars and thirty percent, the summer becomes a crash course in friendship, ownership, desire, and the family talent for hiding. Because Keisha is not the first woman in this family to disappear inside a secret. She is the third.

---

# Reader experience

The reader should feel they are reading:

* a funny college-money crisis story
* a friendship/business summer plot
* an intergenerational family-secret novel
* a light slow-burn romance
* a story about shame, pride, and asking for help

The money education should arrive through scenes, not explanations. The reader should learn almost accidentally.

---

# The money architecture

## The $2,800 gap

The gap remains **$2,800**.

It is shame-sized: small enough that one honest conversation could change everything, large enough to feel impossible alone. The tragedy is not that the number is huge. The tragedy is that Keisha lets a small number grow teeth.

The number should not be widened for melodrama.

## The 529

Elena opened a 529 when Keisha was two. She made small monthly deposits for years, then shifted the money toward safety as college approached.

The 529 disburses a fixed amount each year.

Freshman year, that annual slice quietly helped cover Keisha’s costs. Keisha never understood it clearly; she assumed it was folded into “aid.” Sophomore year, costs rise and the fixed amount no longer covers the gap.

The reveal should feel like emotional archaeology, not rescue. The money was already there, already helping, already incomplete.

Elena only needs one clean explanation:

> “It pays the same every year. The world doesn’t charge the same every year.”

Then the story moves on.

## How the gap closes

The ending closes the gap through Keisha’s actions plus old help already in motion:

| Source                        | Approximate role                                       |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| 529 annual slice              | already disbursed, misunderstood by Keisha             |
| Campus job / circulation desk | comes from Keisha asking again after earlier rejection |
| Stand share                   | earned through summer labor                            |
| Margin                        | very small, roughly $80; no triumphant surplus         |

There is **no tuition rescue from Elena**.

The replicable lesson is: small early help matters, asking again matters, work matters, and naming the number matters.

## Laptop loan

Elena does not pay Keisha’s tuition gap. But she does later make Keisha a small formal loan for a laptop: real terms, token interest, negotiated by Keisha, signed by both.

This preserves the signing ritual without turning Elena into a rescuer.

# Emotional architecture

## Elena suspects, but does not know

Elena does not know the $2,800 number. She recognizes the shape of hiding.

She sees Keisha pricing old receipts instead of talking about school. She sees the phone flinch. She asks once about housing, hears the lie, and does not press.

This is Elena’s flaw: she was once erased by people who acted on her behalf without asking. So she refuses to intervene unless invited. She thinks she is respecting Keisha’s agency. In truth, she is repeating the family pattern in a more elegant form.

## Three key Elena-Keisha beats

**Early:** Elena asks, “You have your housing?” Keisha lies. Elena never asks again.

**Middle:** Keisha handles an envelope from the 529 institution while filing Elena’s papers. She does not read it. The answer passes through her hands.

**Late:** Keisha begins, “Can I ask you something about money?” Elena almost offers. Both retreat. Keisha sees Elena watching her leave.

The theme performs itself: two women trained by secrecy miss each other by inches.

## The confrontation

Keisha’s accusation is not simply, “You knew and let me panic.”

It is sharper:

> “You built an entire system of secret help so you would never have to have one honest conversation.”

The 529 is love engineered to avoid intimacy.

Elena hid the 529 from Keisha’s mother too because explaining it would reveal the larger fortune. One secret required another. That makes the mother’s later admission hurt more: someone could have taught her and chose envelopes instead.

During the confrontation, Keisha hears herself. She has hidden $2,800 for four months. Elena hid a portfolio for forty years. Same wound, different scale.

Elena does not apologize well. She explains:

> “Hiding was the first way I ever felt safe.”

Keisha names the problem:

> “That is an explanation. It is not an apology.”

# Keisha’s active cover-up

Keisha’s avoidance must stay active. She is not waiting around. She is maintaining a lie.

## The lie escalates

1. **Archive:** she archives the school hold notice.
2. **Failed attempt:** she calls the bursar, gets trapped in hold music and forms, and gives up.
3. **Fabrication:** she forwards her mother an old housing email as if current.
4. **Costly lie:** she pays a real housing deposit she cannot afford to keep the lie alive.
5. **Visible prop:** she lets her mother buy dorm bedding for a room she may never occupy.
6. **Float borrowing:** short after the deposit, she borrows $60 from the stand cash box, intending to replace it.
7. **Drafts folder:** the follow-up email to student employment sits in drafts for much of the book.

The final confession is not admitting a number. It is dismantling a structure.

## Keisha’s stand role

Keisha is operations: supply runs, permits, setup, inventory, and the cash box.

This makes her competence real. The stand survives partly because of her. That is why the hidden incompetence — the college gap, the float borrowing, the fear of asking — hurts.

# Core cast

## Keisha, 19 — narrator / protagonist

Keisha is funny because she turns panic into systems. She does not say she is terrified. She says archive is technically a filing system.

**Money wound:** needing help feels like failure.
**Comic behavior:** deadpan denial and over-practical avoidance.
**Contradiction:** she thinks she is low-maintenance, but her silence forces everyone else to live around a problem they cannot see.
**What she is right about:** asking for help can cost dignity.
**Arc:** from hiding the number to saying it out loud.

Her victory is not that school gets paid for. Her victory is that she stops disappearing from her own life.

## Maya, 21 — older friend / chaos engine

Maya is already in the adult world: job shifts, bills, dating, maybe a credit-card balance, and the feeling that everyone else is living louder.

She spends because pleasure feels like proof she exists.

**Money wound:** she wants money to make life feel bigger now.
**Comic behavior:** theatrical confidence with money she does not have.
**Contradiction:** she acts careless but notices everyone’s mood first.
**What she is right about:** joy has value.
**Arc:** from spending to prove value to choosing joy with eyes open.

Maya’s ending is not “I am not ready.” It is a priced choice: she pays down the card, keeps one joyful purchase without apology, and sells the concert ticket in front of everyone. The ticket sale should hurt. The book should mourn it a little.

## Emily, 18 — math brain

Emily is brilliant with numbers and terrified of her own. Her cracked phone holds the banking app she refuses to open.

**Money wound:** exact truth feels more dangerous than guessing.
**Comic behavior:** terrifying precision; spreadsheets with absurd filenames.
**Contradiction:** she trusts numbers except the one that belongs to her.
**What she is right about:** clarity is kindness, even when it hurts.
**Arc:** from studying the door to opening it.

Emily opens her account before Keisha knows. That proves Keisha’s limited POV has missed other people’s courage.

## Sofi, 16 — the watched one

Sofi is not a co-lead. She is the fourth-generation mirror.

She supplies the family recipe, recognizes unpaid value faster than the older girls, and asks questions adults hate answering.

**Money wound:** family love often expects unpaid contribution.
**Comic behavior:** calm literal questions.
**Contradiction:** she seems younger, but understands ownership faster than everyone.
**What she is right about:** love does not erase value.
**Arc:** from family asset to named contributor.

Her major scenes are the recipe invoice, the custodial-account scene, and the final watching image.

## Elena, ~83 — grandmother / hidden investor

Elena is funny, severe, frugal, secretive, and secretly wealthy.

She was financially erased as a young widow. She taught herself never to be invisible again. She became a brilliant saver and investor, but she never learned how to feel safe enough to spend.

She shops thrift, reuses bags, saves jars, negotiates over tiny amounts, refuses to replace broken things, and treats comfort as suspicious. At first it is comic. Later it is sad.

**Money wound:** once erased, she built a life around never needing anyone again.
**Comic behavior:** thrift as performance.
**Contradiction:** she has wealth but no permission to enjoy it.
**What she is right about:** slow money works.
**What she is wrong about:** survival is not the same as living.
**Arc:** from saving to survive toward spending once, deliberately.

Her late spending beat should be small: one beautiful item at full price, one replaced broken thing, or one meal paid for without making the bill a courtroom proceeding.

## Marcus, 24 — Maya’s brother / fast-money foil / light love interest

Marcus dislikes his job and dreams of hitting it big, retiring by thirty-five, and never answering to anyone again.

He is not stupid. He is smart, scared, charming, and seduced by speed.

**Money wound:** ordinary work makes him feel replaceable.
**Comic behavior:** finance-bro language applied to normal life and flirting.
**Contradiction:** he says he hates work, but he really hates feeling trapped.
**What he is right about:** young people are not crazy to want escape.
**Arc:** from performance wealth to quiet ownership.

Marcus is a subplot, not the spine. His crash happens on the page and for his own reasons. He does not reform for Keisha.

## Keisha’s mother — emotional anchor

Keisha’s mother is practical, perceptive, and institutionally under-informed.

She knows groceries, schedules, moods, and how to stretch a week. She does not know how to decode college finance systems.

She should have at least one earlier moment of almost-knowing: a look at the bedding bag, a hesitation over the forwarded email, or a glance at Keisha’s face. She is not blind. She simply does not know what system she is looking at.

## Sofi’s mother — counter-wisdom

Sofi’s mother represents cash, caution, liquidity, family obligation, and practical protection.

She is not backward. She distrusts systems because systems have fees, forms, delays, penalties, and rules that punish people for one missed step.

Her conflict with Sofi over invoicing the recipe should be a real scene, not a stance.

---

# Romance subplot: Keisha and Marcus

This is a light slow burn, around five key beats. It should add commercial pleasure and thematic pressure without taking over.

## Why it works

Keisha and Marcus are mirrors. Both are ashamed of the slow path. Both are tempted by speed. Both want money to erase humiliation.

## Five beats

1. **Flicker:** Marcus shows up at the stand more often than a brother needs to. Keisha notices herself noticing.
2. **Alignment:** during a value fight, he takes Keisha’s side for reasons that are not quite math.
3. **The offer:** at his peak, Marcus offers to “make” Keisha the money. Fast money arrives wearing a boy’s face. Keisha declines.
4. **Maya’s warning:** Maya notices the danger first. She loves both of them and sees the cliff.
5. **The beginning:** after the crash, after the accounts, they split the cost of a date. A beginning, not a couple.

## Guardrails

* The romance never rescues Keisha.
* Keisha never fixes Marcus.
* Marcus’s crash and shift toward the boring account happen for his own reasons.
* The emotional spine remains Keisha, Elena, and Keisha’s mother.

---

# Comedy engines

The humor should be character-driven, not gag-driven.

* **Keisha:** deadpan denial; wry documentation of her own cover-up.
* **Maya:** theatrical confidence; bad but seductive logic.
* **Emily:** terrifying precision.
* **Sofi:** literal questions adults do not want answered.
* **Elena:** thrift-store severity; stinginess as performance.
* **Marcus:** investor vocabulary applied to ordinary life and flirting.
* **Keisha’s mother:** practical bluntness.

The book should be funny on every page because the people are funny, not because the plot becomes slapstick.

# Form and devices

## Keep

* Keisha’s first-person comic narration
* group chat
* the napkin deal
* stand ledger
* amended agreement
* 1965 pay envelope
* 1976 fund slip
* drafts-folder email
* bedding bag in the corner
* Emily’s one-page cheat sheet as an in-world artifact only

## Cut from manuscript

No back-matter appendix. The expanded cheat sheet can become a website/newsletter extra.

An adult reader should not feel they have bought homework.

## Artifact rule

Artifacts appear only when they create emotional or plot impact. No artifact-per-chapter rhythm.

# Chapter plan

## Act I — The Lie and the Deal

### 1. The Lie at the Kitchen Table

Mom asks about fall housing. Keisha says “handled.” Her phone shows the hold notice. She archives it.

Classmates post leases. Keisha flees to Elena’s garage sale. The 1965 pay envelope appears. So does the broken chair Elena refuses to replace.

**Turn:** Keisha chooses usefulness over truth.

### 2. Hold Music

Keisha calls the bursar. Hold music, forms, a transfer, a dropped call. This is the origin of the archive habit.

**Turn:** avoidance becomes a scar from a failed attempt.

### 3. Thirty Dollars for Thirty Percent

Necklace receipt. Elena haggles over junk and refuses to sell treasures. The agua fresca idea begins. Elena offers $30 for 30%. Sofi asks what profit means. Nobody answers well.

Elena asks, “You have your housing?” Keisha lies. Marcus drops a green screenshot: retirement by 35 is ahead of schedule.

**Turn:** everyone signs before understanding the terms.

### 4. Ten Customers

First Saturday flop. Too much ice, too many cups, ten customers, almost no money. Elena makes them count what actually happened.

**Turn:** they feel poor in public.

### 5. The Spring No

Keisha’s earlier RA rejection is revealed. “I already tried” becomes her alibi. She takes charge of the cash box because being trusted with money feels like competence.

**Turn:** Keisha mistakes being responsible for being safe.

### 6. The Forwarded Email

Mom asks about the deposit. Keisha forwards an old housing email as if current. The stand has its first decent Saturday. The reinvest-versus-split fight begins.

**Turn:** the lie now requires fabrication.

### 7. Elena Takes Her Cut

Elena takes thirty percent in cash. The girls resent her because she did not haul ice. Emily has a quiet home scene where no one talks directly about money. Elena refuses to replace the cracked cooler handle.

**Turn:** ownership looks unfair when labor is sweaty.

### 8. Drafts

Keisha drafts the follow-up email to student employment and does not send it. The stand finds rhythm. The group chat gets funny. Optimism becomes briefly dangerous.

**Turn:** Keisha knows the next right action and postpones it.

---

## Act II — The Stand Works, Then Doesn’t

### 9. The Brother Who Keeps Showing Up

Marcus appears at the stand more than necessary. Keisha notices herself noticing. He uses portfolio language for cup sizes.

**Turn:** fast money becomes charming.

### 10. Maya’s Ad

Maya spends her own money on promotion. Sales jump. She wants a bigger cut.

**Turn:** the person everyone calls irresponsible may have created the value.

### 11. The Deposit

Mom pushes housing paperwork. Keisha pays a real deposit she cannot afford to keep the lie alive. The bedding bag enters her room.

**Turn:** the lie now costs money.

### 12. The Value Fight

Emily proves Maya’s ad mattered. Keisha’s operations labor also enters the argument. Marcus takes Keisha’s side for reasons that are not purely math. Sofi realizes no one has priced the recipe.

**Turn:** value becomes personal.

### 13. The Invoice

Sofi invoices the recipe. Her mother is appalled. The girls confront Elena. Elena admits the original deal was not fair. The agreement is amended. Elena signs last.

**Turn:** the teacher has to admit she wrote an erasing deal.

### 14. The Envelope She Files

Keisha helps Elena with paperwork and handles an envelope bearing the 529 institution’s name. She files it unread.

**Turn:** the answer passes through her hands.

### 15. The Vendor

A vendor offers $600 for the booth and “brand.” Keisha secretly wants to sell. Emily’s math says keep it. Sofi asks whether the recipe transfers. Vote: 3–1 against selling.

**Turn:** Keisha almost sells the summer to solve a secret hole.

### 16. The Float

Short after the deposit, Keisha borrows $60 from the stand float, meaning to replace it. She calls it internal bridge financing.

**Turn:** the cover-up crosses a line.

### 17. The Offer

Marcus peaks. He has quit his job and talks like a man who escaped gravity. He half-guesses Keisha is short and offers to make her the money. Keisha declines, barely.

**Turn:** fast money arrives wearing a boy’s face.

### 18. Maya Sees It

Maya privately calls out the danger. Rain kills a Saturday. The stand makes nothing.

**Turn:** Maya sees both the romance and the cliff.

### 19. The Near-Miss

Emily’s best-case math says Keisha is still short. Keisha begins to ask Elena for help. Elena almost offers. Both retreat. Keisha sees Elena watching her leave.

**Turn:** two women miss each other by inches.

### 20. The Thumb

Keisha sees the investing tab. One hyped stock could close the gap. Her thumb hovers over money that includes borrowed float money.

Maya catches her. That night, Keisha audits Marcus’s full-year return and sees red. She sends the follow-up email.

**Turn:** Keisha chooses reality over speed.

### 21. Sixty Dollars

Keisha confesses the float borrowing to the girls before they discover it. Emily reruns the ledger. Maya says it is survivable.

**Turn:** the small confession rehearses the big one.

### 22. The Crash

Marcus’s fall happens on the page. The screenshot cannot be cropped clean. He makes the quiet call about getting his job back. He asks Emily how to open the boring account.

**Turn:** the fast path loses its costume.

---

## Act III — The Truth and the Names

### 23. The Ledger and the Envelope

Season winding down. Stand shares total about $1,000 each. Keisha is still short. She decides to stop hiding, starting with Elena.

**Turn:** the numbers force the first honest conversation.

### 24. Elena’s Story

Elena tells the truth: widowhood, financial erasure, the 1976 fund slip, decades of boring ownership, the 529 opened when Keisha was two.

The 529 paid last year too. The beam was always there and still not enough.

Keisha accuses Elena of building secret help to avoid honest conversation. Mid-tirade, Keisha hears herself.

**Turn:** Elena is understood, not absolved.

### 25. Names on Accounts

Emily has already opened her account. Maya makes her priced choice: card paid down, one joyful purchase kept, ticket sold. Sofi opens a custodial account and asks the question no one can answer comfortably. Keisha signs alone.

**Turn:** every character practices a different kind of courage.

### 26. Full Price

Elena pays full price for one beautiful or necessary thing. Then comes the laptop loan: real terms, token interest, negotiated by Keisha, signed by both.

Keisha and Marcus have a post-crash conversation. They split the cost of a date.

**Turn:** money can be care without being rescue.

### 27. The Kitchen Table Again

Keisha tells her mother everything: hold notice, $2,800, deposit, bedding bag, float, months of silence.

Her mother admits she did not know what to ask. The gap closes on the page: 529 slice, circulation-desk job, stand share, tiny margin.

The bedding comes out of the bag.

**Turn:** the family pattern is named.

### 28. The Watched One

Move-in weekend. The costs remain visible: campus job, laptop loan, spring payments, junior-year gap on the horizon.

The stand’s future is left open. The amended agreement remains in the ledger.

Final image: Keisha catches Sofi watching her the way Keisha once watched Elena.

**Turn:** the pattern is still transmitting — but now someone sees it.

---

# Ending note

The ending should be warm but not tidy.

Keisha returns to school at visible cost. Maya is not a perfect saver; she has made one real trade. Emily is not fearless; she was brave earlier than Keisha knew. Marcus is not a financial saint; he is back at work and beginning again. Elena is not absolved; she is understood. Keisha’s mother is not magically educated; she is awake to what she was never taught. Sofi is watching.

One woman planted money.
One woman admits she was never taught.
One woman says the number out loud.
One woman learns to pay full price, once.
And the youngest one is taking notes.

# Drafting priorities

1. **Voice first.** Keisha must be funny, specific, and emotionally avoidant on page one.
2. **Story before education.** If a scene feels like a lesson, rewrite it as a fight, joke, temptation, or secret.
3. **Protect Elena’s ambiguity.** She is loving, wounded, wrong, and not easily forgiven.
4. **Keep Marcus secondary.** He sharpens Keisha’s temptation; he does not replace the family spine.
5. **Respect Maya’s joy.** The book is not anti-spending. It is anti-hiding, anti-denial, and anti-speed-as-salvation.
6. **Let objects accumulate.** The bedding bag, drafts folder, envelope, float, and receipts should gain meaning naturally, not announce themselves.
7. **Do not over-explain the 529.** The emotional meaning matters more than the mechanics.
8. **End with cost.** The gap closes, but adulthood begins.
