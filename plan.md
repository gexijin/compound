# Compound — A Novel (v6)

A funny, story-driven YA novel teaching money and investing to teen girls (16–18).
Thirteen chapters, three acts, two reveals.

**Logline:** Keisha is lying to everyone about the school she gave up. Hiding in the work of
pricing fifty years of her grandmother's life at a garage sale, she and three friends launch an
agua fresca stand on Elena's $30-for-30% stake — and spend one summer living Elena's
eleven-year arc at compressed speed, while the boxes give up her secret: she was never selling
because she was broke. She was auditioning her heirs.

---

## Craft models (v6)

The pitch-line comp is "Wimpy Kid for teen girls" — it communicates *density of humor* to
buyers. The craft models are more precise, and agents should study the mechanism, not mimic
the surface:

- **Louise Rennison (Georgia Nicolson)** — ambient voice comedy from a teen-girl narrator;
  the humor lives in every paragraph of narration, not in set pieces. The proven register for
  this demographic.
- **Sophie Kinsella (*Confessions of a Shopaholic*)** — a funny first-person narrator lying
  to herself about money, whose comic self-deception the reader sees through. The money-denial
  engine, adult version.
- **Wimpy Kid** — one borrowed element only: visual artifacts breaking up the text.

What does NOT transfer from Wimpy Kid: a static, amoral narrator who never grows. Keisha has
real stakes and must change; the reader laughs at her blind spots *and* roots for her, or
Ch. 13 collapses.

## Voice & form (locked)

- **First-person Keisha, present tense, no foreknowledge.** The narrator is hiding her want
  from the reader too; her dry, too-practical narration is the comedy instrument. The Ch. 13
  betrayal is hers and the reader's in the same instant. **No retrospective narration of any
  kind** — "I didn't know it then, but…" is foreknowledge and is banned.
- **Comedy, two tiers (v6):**
  - **Narration humor is unlimited and mandatory.** Keisha is funny on every page because her
    deadpan over-practicality keeps misfiring — she prices a fondue set like a hostile
    takeover, narrates her own denial with total confidence, judges a napkin contract
    "elegant." The reader laughs at her blind spots; her flaw *is* the comedy, which is also
    the theme.
  - **Situational gags stay budgeted: one per chapter** (booth disasters, the vendor, the
    lemonade kids), so the plot never turns slapstick.
  - **No jokes past the turn:** once a chapter hits its emotional pivot, the narration plays
    it straight until the envelope line. The funny voice going quiet is the emotional signal.
  - **Each character has one repeatable comic mechanism**, escalated across the book, not
    one-off jokes: Maya — catastrophic overconfidence with money she doesn't have (the
    saved-for-later cart is a running bit); Sofi — devastating literal questions adults can't
    answer; Emily — pathological precision ("reads it twice and says nothing," recurring, with
    escalation); Elena — theatrical stinginess played as performance.
  - **Quality bar — the screenshot test:** every chapter needs lines a 16-year-old would
    screenshot to a friend. Humor skews teen-native, never adult-nostalgic; period objects
    (the 1983 haircut receipt) are funny only when a girl interrogates them in-scene.
- **The group chat is on the page** — typeset as texts, timestamps, **3–6 exchanges per
  chapter as the default; up to ~10 in chapters where the chat carries plot** (the breakup,
  Ch. 10's catch). It carries: Maya's voice (and, in Act I, her breakup — jokes with a crack
  in them, on the page, boy never seen), Marcus's screenshot drops (the crops are the comedy),
  Sofi's deadpan, and Keisha lurking the admitted-students chat she never left.
- **POV limit is theme.** Keisha can't see what her friends don't show her (Emily's dad
  conversation, Sofi's kitchen) — the narrator who never asks for help never asks the real
  questions either. Negative space is a feature, enforced by the form.
- **The envelope as running device:** each chapter closes on its line, written in-scene by
  whoever earned it — never recited, sometimes fought over, once amended, once repealed.

## Illustration rule (new in v6)

**Every chapter contains at least one artifact rendered as an image on the page, and the
artifact is always a real object from the story.** One visual grammar throughout: everything
looks taped into Keisha's notebook (receipts, torn chat printouts, the ledger), which unifies
the artifacts and quietly explains who is assembling this record.

Core recurring visuals:
- **The envelope, evolving** — shown at each act break: clean maxims → the Ch. 7 amendment in
  different ink, Elena's signature below the girls' → the Ch. 5 rule struck through and
  rewritten in Ch. 10 → coffee-stained and handed forward in Ch. 13.
- **The two ledger curves** (Ch. 4 onward), hand-drawn in Emily's precise handwriting,
  updated in later chapters — the reader *sees* the gap widen.
- **Marcus's cropped screenshots** as actual images — the crop is the comedy.
- One-off artifacts: the 1965 pay envelope, the 1975 necklace receipt, the passbook, the
  misspelled sign, the dead certificate, the 1976 fund slip, Emily's cheat-sheet facsimile,
  the final three-document image in Ch. 13.

**Reveal constraints on art:** no artifact before Ch. 11 may show or imply Elena's totals; no
artifact before Ch. 13 may hint at the 529. (See guardrail 3.)

## Elena discipline: right about money, wrong on the page

Elena stays the living proof — but she loses one negotiation (Ch. 7: the flawed deal was hers,
and she signs the amendment below their names), closes one door she shouldn't (Ch. 10: Keisha
almost asks; Elena deflects into a lesson — re-readable as guilt after Ch. 13), and gets one
modern thing charmingly wrong (Ch. 7: "flyers with extra steps"). Mentor becomes character;
both fractures and the Ch. 13 confession have on-page roots.

## The cast

- **Keisha, 18 — narrator.** Practical, proud, never asks. Declined the flagship (~$20k/yr
  illustrative) after running the number once — never opened the aid letter. Live problem: a
  ~$3,000 gap for the settled-for school. Turns 18 mid-book: opens her accounts with no adult.
  Flaw on the page from Ch. 2: she notices things and decides not to ask. Her deadpan
  narration is the book's comedy engine.
- **Emily, 16 — the avoider, the math brain.** Won't open her banking app; looking was the
  problem. One wound scene (Ch. 5), one middle beat (Ch. 9: almost opens the app, two lines),
  one payoff (Ch. 12). Comic mechanism: pathological precision.
- **Maya, 17 — hustler + spender, the comic voice.** ~$900 earned, almost nothing kept; the
  breakup lives in the chat as a register, not a plot — with one real beat outside the chat,
  never moralized as spending. One thread: *Maya seeks value outside herself.* Comic
  mechanism: catastrophic overconfidence with money she doesn't have.
- **Sofi, 15 — the unembarrassed question-asker.** Cash-only household; her mom's "keep it
  safe" is the loving counter-voice; the recipe is her family's. Comic mechanism: devastating
  literal questions adults can't answer — one of them (Ch. 2) is the book's thesis,
  unanswered.
- **Elena — the living proof.** Dry, unhurried, never recites a rule. Tin box → passbook →
  the 1976 index fund ("Bogle's folly"), held through everything, **~$1.7M by 2026**. The
  vow: *never invisible again.* The flaw: a woman erased once hides what she has from
  everyone — operating in visible scenes (see Elena discipline). Comic mechanism: theatrical
  stinginess played as performance.
- **Marcus, 24 — Maya's brother, wallpaper with a ticker on it.** ~5 spoken lines. Screenshots
  → spike → peak → off-screen crater → "how do you open the boring one?" Never humiliated.
- **Margins:** Sofi's mom (Chs. 3, 12, 13); Keisha's mom (kitchen-table lie in Ch. 1; one
  beat in Ch. 13 — she never knew either); the smug vendor (Ch. 8); the lemonade kids (one
  scene); regulars.

## Emotionally-straight scenes (new in v6)

Two or three scenes per act play **feelings-first, with the money meaning legible only on
reflection or re-read** — emotionally complete even if the reader never notices the lesson.
The model is Emily's Ch. 12 payoff: a scene about a girl and her father in which the finance
is incidental. Declared candidates:

- **Ch. 1 / Ch. 13, Keisha's mom at the kitchen table** — the lie and its echo; a daughter
  protecting her mother from disappointment. The tuition is set dressing.
- **Ch. 5, Emily's dinner** — a family that doesn't talk, period. No maxim in sight.
- **Ch. 9, Sofi's quinceañera money** — what her family expects of a daughter; the reader
  does the money math themselves.
- **Maya's breakup beat** — one or two lines outside the chat; nobody makes it about her
  spending. Her thread pays off better because the book resists moralizing it until she does.

## ACT I — THE DEAL (a lie, a failure, a mistake)

**Ch. 1 — The $20 in the Garage** *(inflation)*
**Cold open, page 1: the lie, witnessed.** Mom at the kitchen table asks about orientation at
the commuter school; Keisha says it makes more sense while the flagship chat pings in her
pocket — the roommate-she'll-never-have posting a move-in countdown. She mutes it and goes to
Elena's to hide in work. The garage sale starts on page 3. In a coat-pocket box: the 1965 pay
envelope, $20 inside. Emily: "It got robbed. Slowly." **Plants:** among Elena's papers, a kept
clipping of Keisha's acceptance announcement — *Elena knew, and said nothing* (feeds "you
watched me panic all summer") — and beneath it the aid letter, unopened. The shoebox glimpsed.
Situational gag: the fondue-set pricing war, narrated in Keisha's dry deadpan.
*Artifact: the 1965 pay envelope.*
*Envelope: Money sitting still is money shrinking.*

**Ch. 2 — The Fifty-Dollar Necklace** *(store of value + the mistake, staged)*
The buyer, the "not for sale," the 1975 receipt: $50 then, ~$1,050 now, "the first pretty
thing nobody co-signed." Then **the signing scene, on the page, as comedy:** four girls
treating a $30 napkin-deal like a Fortune 500 closing — Maya signs in cursive, Emily reads it
twice and says nothing, and Sofi asks the book's thesis out loud: **"What does 'profit' mean
exactly?"** — and they all laugh instead of answering. Narrator-Keisha clocks that the page is
one paragraph long and decides that's *elegant* — practicality misfiring, her flaw in
miniature, planted for the reader.
*Artifact: the 1975 receipt (and/or the napkin one-pager).*
*Envelope: Real things hold what paper drops.*

**Ch. 3 — The First Saturday** *(cost, revenue, profit — taught on wreckage)*
**The disaster Saturday.** The batch over-steeps and comes out bitter; a customer spits it out;
the eight-year-olds' lemonade stand across the aisle outsells them (one scene, never a
recurring rival). Take: **$19 and a ruined afternoon.** Elena, ledger open, walks them backward
through costs on the wreckage — the failed batch's hibiscus and sugar are real costs too.
Marcus's first green screenshot hits the chat; Maya's breakup arrives *in the chat* — jokes
with a crack in them. Situational gag: the sign misspells "hibiscus," and the chat's renaming
spree.
*Artifact: the misspelled sign.*
*Envelope: Profit is what's left, not what comes in.*

**Ch. 4 — The Two Coolers** *(compounding, lived)*
**Cold open: the $84 Saturday** — one week later, the fix worked, and they count their first
real money ("You *kept* $37"). Which makes the vote hurt: reinvest-or-spend splits 2–2; Elena:
"Run both." Two ledger curves open — poured-back vs. pocketed — insultingly flat, widening all
book. Twin artifacts: the 1965–76 passbook and Maya's saved-for-later cart screenshot ($212 —
a running bit from here on). First envelope wording-fight (Maya too profane, Emily precise and
dead; the compromise survives).
*Artifact: the two ledger curves, first drawing (plus the passbook — no totals context that
implies Elena's present wealth).*
*Envelope: Small and early beats big and late.*

**Ch. 5 — Payout Day** *(what a stock is — taught through envy)*
Elena collects her 30% in cash, in front of girls who hauled ice. "She didn't even *do*
anything." The resentment flips: **ownership** — and anyone can be on her side of the table;
the market is millions of little 30%s for sale. Refuse-the-pitch rep #1: Marcus's meme tip,
declined with love. **The girls add their own rule to the envelope — "The ledger never lies"**
(their first authored maxim; it will be repealed). **The Emily scene:** dinner, her dad's one
question, fifteen lines, never revisited — played emotionally straight, a family that doesn't
talk; no maxim in sight. Situational gag: Maya reenacts Elena's cash-count, badly.
*Artifact: the envelope with the girls' first authored rule.*
*Envelope: Buy the slice, not the ticket.*

## ACT II — THE DOUBT (the rules under fire)

**Ch. 6 — The Two Certificates** *(what makes a business worth holding)*
Elena away; two 1980s certificates — one compounded four decades, one was "unstoppable" and is
a corpse. Her note: "I keep the dead one where I can see it." The quality questions (moats,
never the word). Situational gag: they try to cash the dead certificate; the chat mocks two of
the envelope maxims as fortune cookies — and keeps them anyway.
*Artifact: the dead certificate.*
*Envelope: Buy the machine, not the moment.*

**Ch. 7 — The Ad Fight** *(labor vs. capital — the fracture, and Elena loses a round)*
Elena dismisses Maya's plan as **"flyers with extra steps."** Maya spends her own ~$40; Saturday
explodes; Monday she wants a bigger cut. Emily's attribution test (ad or heat wave?) makes math
pick a side between friends — and proves the ad. Elena pays Maya's cut with theatrical
reluctance (her comic mechanism, on full display) — the girls' native knowledge wins a round,
which is Elena's own shoebox thesis turned on her. The gut punch: pricing contributions prices
Sofi's recipe — given, never invoiced — and she leaves the porch without a word. **No jokes
past this turn.** Resolution: they re-read the one-pager and someone says the quiet part —
**the deal that shortchanged labor and never priced the recipe was written by the woman
teaching them to read what they sign.** Elena doesn't defend it: "I wrote the deal I'd have
wanted at your age. Nobody gave your age to me" — and **signs the amendment below their
names.** Sofi's exit is partly Elena's fault; bad arrangements are usually written by decent
people.
*Artifact: the envelope, amended — different ink, Elena's signature last.*
*Envelope: (amended, on labor.)*

**Ch. 8 — The Shoebox** *(valuation + invest in what you know)*
Cold open: the smug vendor offers to buy the booth. Emily builds **payback years** (price ÷
yearly earnings; $600 = two summers; $15,000 = fifty); armed, the girls try to buy back
Elena's 30% — her price is eye-watering and fair; they decline. Then the shoebox: fifty years
of receipts — **she never bought a stranger** — plus the chains she loved and never bought
(too dear), including a video store. The shoebox *nominates*; the math *decides*. Each girl
scouts one company from her own week for the porch watchlist. Situational gag: the 1983
haircut receipt — funny because Sofi interrogates Elena about it, not as a prop.
*Artifact: a shoebox receipt spread (no totals).*
*Envelope: Price is what you pay, years are what you count.*

**Ch. 9 — The Rainout** *(risk & diversification)*
A rained-out Saturday zeroes the weekend: one product, one corner, one weather system *is* one
basket. Fix: Maya's thrift rack, split per the Ch. 7 amendment. Sofi's quinceañera obligation
comes due: family-money-with-a-date vs. her-money-for-later — played emotionally straight:
what her family expects of a daughter; love, not leak; the reader does the money math
themselves. **Emily's middle beat (two lines):** waiting out the rain, she gets as far as the
banking app's login screen — and locks the phone. Situational gag: emergency rack pricing by
committee.
*Artifact: the 1974 crash clipping, Elena's margin note.*
*Envelope: All the eggs, never one basket.* Hook: Marcus is still green. Are the rules just…
slow?

**Ch. 10 — The Hot Streak** *(horizon — the second fracture, and the closed door)*
Marcus peaks: "$900 in a day. We made $63 and I smell like hibiscus." **The closed door:**
after the crater of the week, Keisha stands on Elena's porch with the question half-formed —
the closest she comes all book to asking — and Elena deflects into a lesson. In first person
the reader feels the door shut; on re-read, it's the flaw operating (and the acceptance
clipping from Ch. 1 makes it damning). That night: the roommate posts her move-in date, and on
**the payment app the booth uses for Saturday sales — which has an invest tab** — a hyped
stock could close the gap in a month, booth money one thumb-press away. *Maya catches her, and
says so in front of everyone* (the chat may run long this chapter; it is carrying plot). What
saves Keisha is the ledger reflex: she runs the audit upward on Marcus — "show me the whole
year, not the good days" — and the whole-year number is red. One tax line: quick wins split
with the IRS. Off-screen, his position craters. **The repeal:** the girls strike their own
Ch. 5 rule — the ledger never caught Keisha's thumb; Maya did. Struck through, rewritten:
*"The ledger only answers what you ask it."* A rule repealed proves the envelope is alive.
**No jokes past the porch.**
*Artifact: the envelope — rule struck through, rewritten.*
*Envelope: Money with a date on it doesn't get to gamble.* Hook: how do you own everything
without being an expert?

## ACT III — THE TRUTH AND THE HANDS

**Ch. 11 — Own Everything, Quietly** *(index funds + THE ELENA REVEAL)*
**This chapter carries only what the reveal needs.** The answer: an index fund — the whole
market in one purchase. The 1976 slip resurfaces; the dry lines: the divorce, the paperwork
all in his name, the vow, the mocked new fund bought two years after a crash — Vanguard's
first, "you've heard of them now" — held through everything: **~$1.7M** (≈ $84k contributed;
all figures from the assumptions file). Every strange habit was curriculum; they were being
auditioned. The check Keisha never asks for is refused before it's asked. The $20 three ways
stays as the emotional coda of an object from page 3: envelope ~$2 of buying power / passbook
~$210 / riding the 1976 buy ≈ $2,900. **Nothing else with a percent sign; no fees material
anywhere** (cut from the book, v6).
*Artifact: the 1976 fund slip.*
*Envelope: Boring wins — own everything, hold on.*

**Ch. 12 — Opening Day** *(the girls' finale — the exit exam)*
The last mile, dramatized — **each girl hits exactly one snag on the page, and the snag is
the lesson:**
- **Sofi:** custodial needs her mom — the tin honored as step one; her mother's scene.
- **Maya:** her hustle income has a tax invoice — Elena: flips over $400 net owe
  self-employment tax; "the IRS respects your hustle, mija; it invoices it."
- **Keisha:** 18, alone at the counter of her own life — no custodian, her signature only.
- **Emily:** the reveal that she already did it — a printed confirmation slid across the
  table, done weeks ago, which means she had the conversation with her dad, alone, off-page;
  Ch. 5 and the Ch. 9 login screen retroactively lit. Played emotionally straight.

Everything true for all four girls equally is curriculum, not story — it lives in **Emily's
handwritten how-to-open-an-account cheat sheet**, reproduced as a facsimile, full version in
the back-matter appendix (tickers decoded, fractional shares, minimums, brokerages named in
threes: Fidelity / Schwab / Vanguard, "as of this writing"). Checking accounts first ("that's
it? that's the scary part?"). The teen tax deal: income under the standard deduction ⇒ Roth
money taxed ~0% in, never out — "Sixteen is the sale." **The exam:** Marcus scoffs; Keisha
defends the plan to his face — gap first + small Roth + cash buffer, what she owns, where the
return comes from, why her name matters — **slightly funny and slightly shaky doing it; a
flawless recitation reads like the author grading her own student.** They walk home owning
things. Later, quietly, Marcus to Emily: "how do you open the boring one?"
*Artifact: Emily's cheat-sheet facsimile.*
*Envelope: Your name goes on your money.*

**Ch. 13 — The Last Saturday** *(the 529 — the true end)*
Wind-down, one beat each: **the 30% payoff** — Elena earmarked her cut all season in her own
account and now funds the accounts the girls opened in Ch. 12: "your money was working while
you were" → Sofi deposits the 1965 $20, her mother beside her → Maya's Roth ("keeping has a
container now") → the amended, repealed-and-rewritten, coffee-stained envelope handed forward.
Everything is resolved — *which is when Elena asks Keisha to stay.* **The 529:** opened the
year she turned two, ~$60/mo that never flinched — 2020 and 2022 on the chart, deposits
marching through — **~$30–35k on ~$12–14k contributed**, more than half growth, glided to cash
as the date approached. Against the ~$80k dream: a beam, not the building — 529 + **the aid
letter, finally opened on-page (unopened since Ch. 1; the sticker price was never the price)**
+ her Ch. 12 plan + work-study + maybe a small, eyes-open loan (the sequel's door). **Keisha's
anger stays on the page** — "I declined the housing deposit. It was eighty dollars" — and now
the reader can prosecute it: the kept clipping, the closed door on the porch. Elena's answer
is imperfect: part audition, part confession — hiding was her wound; Keisha's mom never knew
either (their kitchen-table echo played emotionally straight). *Never invisible* finally turns
inward. Maya's quiet "must be nice," not resolved too fast. One disclaimer: a good sixteen
years is not the promise; the boring average was enough. **No jokes past Elena's "stay."**
*Envelope (different handwriting): The best time was years ago. The second-best time is now —
for someone who's two.* **Final image / artifact:** the 1976 slip, the 2026 confirmations, and
between them a statement dated the year Keisha turned two.

## Guardrails (v6 — written as instructions to AI drafting agents)

1. **Numbers: never compute, never invent.** No number may appear on the page unless it
   exists in the assumptions file (illustrative forward returns at 7% real; Elena's history at
   10.5% nominal; the 529's ≈12% nominal labeled *lucky decade* in-scene; the Roth limit and
   standard deduction flagged "check current year" and "as of this writing"). Agents never
   compute new figures inline; any new number a scene needs is flagged for human verification,
   never invented.

2. **No rescue before the assessment.** Ch. 12 is passed alone; gifts appear only after, and
   are education-locked or the girls' own money grown.

3. **Both twists unspoiled — including by the prose itself.** Elena's wealth before Ch. 11
   and the 529 before Ch. 13 appear nowhere in jacket copy, blurbs, chapter titles, or
   artwork. **The drafting agent must not foreshadow:** no dramatic-irony winks, no "if only
   I'd known," no loaded adjectives on the shoebox. Raise the questions; never answer them.
   Artwork constraint: no totals visible before Ch. 11; nothing hinting at the 529 before
   Ch. 13.

4. **Education, never advice.** The book teaches method, never tips. Naming in tiers:
   historical facts freely (the 1976 fund, Apple's IPO, ECOA 1974, the 1973–74 crash);
   category exemplars always in threes; **the girls' actual buys never named** — a printed
   ticker attached to a purchase is a stock tip with a plot, and it dates the book fatally.
   **These tiers bind the agent's inventions too:** when adding scene detail, never introduce
   real tickers, apps, or products beyond what the canon file names. An author's note carries
   the disclaimers.

5. **Nobody is the villain or the fool.** The villain is always the arrangement — including
   the one Elena wrote (Ch. 7). Bad men stay off-screen; Marcus is never humiliated.

6. **Every scene earns its pages** by advancing at least one of: a girl's arc, the comedy, or
   the money story — **and no scene may exist only to teach.** Two or three scenes per act
   play emotionally straight, with the money meaning legible only on reflection or re-read
   (see the declared list). The premise guarantees collisions with money; do not contort
   scenes to manufacture them.

7. **All content original; no verbatim quotes from any real person, living or dead.** Bogle,
   Lynch, and everyone else are paraphrased in Elena's voice or absent. Historical facts
   labeled.

8. **Comedy, two-tier** (see Voice & form): narration humor unlimited and mandatory;
   situational gags one per chapter; no jokes past the chapter's emotional turn; the
   screenshot test is the quality bar; character humor comes from each cast member's declared
   comic mechanism, escalated, never from new one-off props.

9. **The envelope is alive:** written never recited; fought over (one fight per act); mocked
   in-scene; amended (Ch. 7); one girl-authored rule repealed and rewritten (Chs. 5 → 10).
   **The envelope's exact wording at every chapter lives in the canon file; agents quote it
   from there, never from memory.**

10. **Voice rules:** first-person Keisha, present tense, **no foreknowledge and no
    retrospective narration** ("I didn't know it then, but…" is banned). The chat typeset on
    the page, 3–6 exchanges default, up to ~10 where the chat carries plot. Off-page scenes
    stay off-page (POV limit = theme). **Forbidden phrases** (stock constructions that flatten
    the voice): "I couldn't help but," "a mix of [X] and [Y]," "little did I know," "my heart
    sank/raced," "I let out a breath I didn't know I was holding" — the canon file maintains
    the full list; agents check drafts against it.

11. **Sequel exclusions are design, not oversight:** debt, credit scores, student loans,
    credit cards. **If these arise in-scene, characters may gesture at them but no mechanism
    is ever explained** — agents do not helpfully teach margin when Marcus craters or loan
    terms at Keisha's Ch. 13 line; that line is a pointer, not a lesson. **Fees/expense
    ratios are cut from the book entirely (v6)** — at most one line in the appendix cheat
    sheet ("pick the fund with the lowest expense ratio — lower is better, that's the whole
    rule"), nothing on any story page.

12. **Back matter:** an appendix with Emily's full cheat sheet (account-opening steps, the
    two questions, glossary — including one line on keeping a reachable cash buffer /
    emergency fund), the author's note on numbers and naming, and nothing that spoils either
    reveal for a reader who flips ahead — the appendix opens with a spoiler warning played as
    a joke in Keisha's voice.

13. **The canon file is ground truth.** A single state document tracks: ages, dates, running
    money totals per girl and for the booth, the ledger curves' values by chapter, the
    envelope's exact text per chapter, who knows which secret when, and the forbidden-phrase
    list. It is updated after every drafted chapter. On any conflict between a draft and the
    canon file, the canon file wins; agents never resolve a discrepancy by inventing.

14. **Voice anchors over adjectives.** The canon file holds one exemplar paragraph per voice
    (Keisha narration; each girl's chat register; Elena's dialogue). Agents match drafts
    against the anchors, not against descriptors like "dry" or "deadpan."

15. **Never state the moral.** Elena never recites a rule — and neither does the narrator or
    the prose. The envelope line is the *only* place a chapter's lesson may be stated
    explicitly. Any sentence that summarizes what a scene "taught" is cut.

16. **Cultural specificity is human-reviewed.** Sofi's family details (the quinceañera,
    "mija," the cash-only household) come from the canon file, never from generic rendering;
    all such scenes are flagged for human/sensitivity review before acceptance.

17. **Illustration rule binds every chapter** (see Illustration rule): at least one real
    story artifact per chapter, one visual grammar (Keisha's notebook), reveal constraints on
    art per guardrail 3.
