# The Thirty Percent — A Novel

## Book Plan v10

**Title:** *The Thirty Percent* (locked; retire other candidates)

**Category:** Adult commercial fiction / book-club women's fiction. Coming-of-age is flavor, not category. The terms "New Adult" and "financial literacy" never appear in pitch materials.

**Primary readership:** women 18–35, purchased off the adult shelf. Younger readers arrive via voice (BookTok does not read spine labels); no content is sanded down for them.

**Comps:** REQUIRED BEFORE DRAFTING. Two voice-driven, funny-warm, intergenerational women's fiction titles from the last three years. This is unfinished homework, deliberately left visible.

**Target length:** 78,000–85,000 words
**Narration:** first-person Keisha, present tense, comic, no hindsight
**Structure:** ~28 short chapters in three acts (short chapters suit the voice, the group-chat texture, the romance flickers, and the lie-maintenance beats)

---

## Core promise

A funny, big-hearted novel about three broke young women, one agua fresca stand, and the grandmother whose secret fortune forces every woman in the family to stop hiding.

## Revised logline

Keisha, nineteen, is $2,800 short of returning to college and has built an entire architecture of lies to avoid saying so — to her mother, her friends, and herself. When her grandmother Elena stakes Keisha and her friends in an agua fresca stand for thirty dollars and thirty percent, the summer becomes a crash course in friendship, ownership, desire, and the family talent for hiding. Because Keisha isn't the first woman in this family to disappear inside a secret. She's the third.

---

# The money architecture (locked decisions)

## The gap
**$2,800.** Shame-sized: small enough that one honest conversation would solve it, big enough to feel impossible alone. Never widened. The tragedy of the number is its smallness.

## The 529 — annual disbursement model
Elena opened a 529 when Keisha was two. Small monthly deposits, decades of growth, shifted to safety as college approached. It disburses a **fixed amount per year across four years**.

- **Freshman year:** the disbursement quietly covered Keisha's gap. Keisha never knew; the money arrived folded into what she assumed was "financial aid."
- **Sophomore year:** costs rose, aid shifted, and the fixed slice no longer covers everything. Hence the $2,800.
- **The reveal (Ch 24):** not a rescue. The money for this year is *already in her account*, invisible, mislabeled in her head. The beam was there all along, load-bearing — and still not enough. Emotional archaeology, not deus ex machina.
- **Mechanics stay off the page.** Elena gets one line — "It pays the same every year. The world doesn't charge the same every year" — and we move on.

## How the gap actually closes
Every line traceable to an action. Nothing requires a rich grandmother — only one who planted small and early.

| Source | Amount (approx.) | Earned by |
|---|---|---|
| 529 annual slice | already disbursed | Elena's planting, 17 years ago |
| Campus job (circulation desk) | covers most of remainder | Keisha asking a second time after rejection |
| Stand share | ~$1,000 | Keisha's summer labor |
| **Margin** | **~$80 to spare** | **Not "and then some." Never a surplus.** |

**No tuition loan from Elena.** The gap-closing stack must feel replicable to a reader with no Elena.

**The signing beat survives, repurposed:** a small formal loan from Elena for a laptop — real terms, real interest (token), negotiated by a girl who now reads contracts, signed last by Elena. The ritual of names, not rescue. (Ch 26.)

## Visible cost of the ending
The circulation desk job eats Keisha's sophomore social life. Loan payments run through spring. Junior year's gap is already visible on the horizon — but this time it has a name, a spreadsheet, and a plan. Cost is what keeps warm from curdling into tidy.

# The Elena question (locked: "Elena suspects")

Elena does not know the number. She knows the shape. She recognizes hiding from the inside because it is her own reflection: a girl pricing a dead man's receipts instead of talking about school, flinching at her phone, never once mentioning housing.

**Why she waits:** her wound. Elena was erased by people who acted on her behalf without asking. Her deepest rule is that you do not touch someone's money — or someone's crisis — uninvited. So she does what she has always done: leaves a door open and stands behind it.

## The three seeded beats (must survive every draft)
1. **Early (Ch 3):** Elena asks one direct question — "You have your housing?" Keisha lies smoothly. Elena never asks again. On reread: Elena recognized the lie and honored it, in her broken way.
2. **Middle (Ch 14):** an envelope with the 529 institution's name sits visible in Elena's paperwork. Keisha files it unread — rhyming with her archiving habit. The answer passes through her hands.
3. **Late (Ch 19):** Keisha sees Elena watching her leave. Neither speaks.

## The Chapter 19 near-miss (rewritten as mutual)
Keisha gets as far as "Can I ask you something about money?" Elena almost offers. Each retreats because the other retreated first. Two women trained by the same family pattern, missing each other by inches. This is the theme performing itself, not a plot hole.

## The Act III confrontation (Ch 24)
Keisha's accusation is not "you knew and let me panic." It is: **"You built an entire system of secret help so you would never have to have one honest conversation."** The 529 is love — engineered to avoid intimacy. Deposits instead of questions.

**The secret metastasized:** Elena hid the 529 from Keisha's mother too, because telling her would have revealed the fortune and cracked the whole architecture of invisibility. One secret required all the others. This makes the mother's Ch 27 admission — "nobody taught me either" — land harder: someone in the family could have taught her and chose the envelope instead.

**The trapdoor:** mid-tirade, Keisha's own sentence dies in her mouth. She hid $2,800 for four months from a mother she loves; Elena hid a portfolio for forty years. Same wound, different scale. Keisha arrives at this herself — Elena never says it, because from Elena it's deflection and from Keisha it's growth.

**Elena does not apologize well.** "Hiding was the first way I ever felt safe" is an explanation, and Keisha says out loud that it is not an apology. Elena gets to be understood, which is worse and better than winning.

# Keisha, activated (locked mechanics)

Avoidance stays as the arc — but avoidance, done honestly, is exhausting active work, and the work goes on the page as plot.

## Layer 1 — The lie is a machine she maintains
"Fall is handled" generates escalating demands:
1. **Withholds** (Ch 1): archives the hold notice.
2. **Fabricates** (Ch 6): forwards her mother an old housing email as if current.
3. **Spends on the fabrication** (Ch 11): pays a real housing deposit she can't afford — to keep the lie alive. This actively widens her gap and later drives the float borrowing.
4. **Lets her mother spend** (Ch 13): accepts dorm bedding bought on sale for a room she may never occupy. The bedding bag sits in the corner of her room for the rest of the book. Loaded gun.

The Ch 27 confession isn't admitting a number — it's unwinding a structure she built.

## Layer 2 — Her job at the stand expresses the flaw
Keisha is **operations**: supply runs, permits, setup, and — crucially — **the cash box**. She volunteers to hold the money because being trusted with money feels like competence.

- **Positive charge:** her operational grind is genuinely why the stand survives. In the Ch 12 value fight, her labor is part of the argument, not just refereed by her.
- **Negative charge:** in the desperate stretch (after the deposit payment), she "borrows" $60 from the stand float, meaning to replace it (Ch 16). Small. Confessed before it becomes theft-theft (Ch 21). But now the thumb-hover is the *escalation* of a transgression already underway — and Maya's catch carries the charge of a friend who has already noticed money moving wrong.

## Layer 3 — Refusals become failed attempts
A character who doesn't try is passive; a character who tries wrong is active.
- **The bursar call (Ch 2):** early, on the page. Hold music, a form she doesn't understand, a transfer that hangs up on her. *That's* when she starts archiving. Avoidance is a scar from a failed attempt.
- **The RA rejection (backstory, revealed Ch 5):** she applied in spring and was rejected. "I already tried" is the avoider's best alibi. The arc is asking *again*, differently, after a no.
- **The drafts folder (Chs 8, 15, 20):** the follow-up email to student employment sits in drafts, obsessively revised, from Ch 8 until she finally sends it in Ch 20. A visible gun on the wall.
- **The aborted ask (Ch 19):** launched and retreated — see the mutual near-miss above.

## Layer 4 — The braid
The deposit is why she raids the float; the float guilt is why the thumb-hover is plausible; the bursar failure is why asking Elena feels doomed; the RA rejection is why the drafts folder stays a drafts folder. Her avoidance generates the consequences that corner her.

**Protect the deadpan.** A narrator wryly documenting her own cover-up is comic gold. The more actively she digs, the funnier the voice gets.

# The romance (new): Keisha and Marcus

Slow burn on a budget — five beats, ~2,500 words total.

**Why them:** they are secretly the same character. Both convinced the slow path is humiliating; both tempted to solve shame with speed. Her thumb-hover and his crash are the same scene at different scales.

**Keisha's interrogation of herself:** is she attracted to Marcus, or to the escape he represents? She notices — deadpan, appalled — that she finds him most charming when the screenshots are green. Pure comedy-engine material.

**Maya is the complication, free:** her best friend and her brother. Maya notices first (she notices everyone's mood first). This deepens the Ch 21 catch: when Maya grabs Keisha's hovering thumb, she is standing between the two people she loves and the same cliff.

## The five beats
1. **Flicker (Ch 9):** Marcus shows up at the stand more often than a brother needs to. Keisha notices herself noticing.
2. **Flicker (Ch 12):** a moment inside the value fight — he takes her side for reasons that have nothing to do with math.
3. **The offer (Ch 17, his peak):** flush, half-guessing she's short, he offers: "I could just... make you the money." Fast money in its most seductive costume — a boy's face. **Keisha declining this is a bigger act than declining the app.**
4. **Maya's callout (Ch 18):** private, funny, sharp. Not a prohibition — a warning about the cliff.
5. **The beginning (Ch 26):** post-crash conversation. Two people with their names on new accounts. A date they each pay half of. A beginning, not a couple.

## Guardrails (non-negotiable)
- Marcus's crash and his crawl back to "the boring one" happen for **his own reasons, on his own page** (Ch 22). If he reforms for her, we've written a fixer-upper fantasy into a book about women refusing to be erased.
- The romance never redeems him and never rescues her.

# Core cast (v10 revisions)

## Keisha, 19 — narrator / protagonist
As before, plus the activation machinery above. Her victory is unchanged: not that college gets paid for, but that she stops disappearing from her own life. New shading: her competence is real — the stand survives on her operations — which makes her hidden incompetence (the gap, the float) hurt more.

## Maya, 21 — older friend / chaos engine
As before, with one upgrade: **her resolution is a choice with a price, not a confession of unreadiness.** In Ch 25 she takes her renegotiated ad-earned cut and splits it deliberately: most to the credit card, one unapologetic joyful purchase kept — chosen with eyes open, named as such. What it costs her: she sells her ticket to the summer's big concert to make the split work, and lets everyone see her do it. Joy with eyes open, priced. She still asks Emily to hold the cheat sheet "for when" — honesty, not failure — but she leaves the book having made one real trade.

## Emily, 18 — younger friend / math brain
Unchanged, and protected: she opens her account **before** Keisha knows (revealed Ch 25), proving the limited POV has missed other people's courage. Her cracked phone, her `phone_final_FINAL_actual.xlsx`, her terror of her own number — all intact.

## Sofi, 16 — the watched one
**Reframed. Age locked at 16** (the "16/17" hedge was crossover-marketing residue). She is not a bridge to teen readers; she is the **fourth generation of women in this money story**, watching Keisha the way Keisha watches Elena.

- **Recipe fight untouched** — it's her spine and it's an adult plot: unpaid family labor, IP, whose name goes on value. Her mother's counter-wisdom collides with it harder: family contribution shouldn't be invoiced; Sofi invoices anyway (Ch 13).
- **The custodial-account scene (Ch 25):** her recipe money cannot be solely in her name — an adult's name must sit on it. In this book that's not paperwork, it's a knife. She asks one calm, literal question about it that no one in the room can answer comfortably.
- **Opaque by design:** seen through Keisha's eyes, occasionally unnervingly perceptive, never focalized. She says the thing that ends a scene exactly three times in the book.
- **She gets the last look** (Ch 28 — see Ending).

## Elena, ~83 — grandmother / hidden investor
As before — the widowhood erasure, the 1976 index fund, the thrift-as-performance that turns from funny to sad — plus the "suspects" machinery above. Her two flaws remain secrecy and frugality to a fault. Her small spending beat survives (Ch 26): she pays full price for something beautiful, or takes the girls out without treating the bill like a personal attack. One item. Not a transformation.

## Marcus, 24 — Maya's brother / fast-money foil / love interest
As before, plus the romance. Critical upgrade: **his crash happens on the page** (Ch 22), not between chapters. We showed the peak; we show the fall — the cropped screenshot he can't crop his way out of, the quiet call to ask about getting his job back. His arc completes when he asks Emily how to open "the boring one," for his own reasons, before the Ch 26 conversation with Keisha.

## Keisha's mom — emotional anchor
As before: once per act, practical and perceptive, institutionally under-informed. Her Ch 27 admission now cuts deeper because the reader knows Elena chose the envelope over teaching her.

## Sofi's mom — supporting counter-wisdom
As before: cash, caution, liquidity, control. Not backward. Her collision with Sofi's invoice (Ch 13) is now a real scene, not a stance.

# The vendor, weaponized

The vendor chapter (now Ch 15) earns its place or dies. v10: it earns it.

The vendor offers $600 for the booth spot and the "brand." This forces a **real vote** — and Keisha, whose 30% share of a buyout is money she desperately needs *right now*, secretly wants to take it. She argues for selling in careful, over-practical language that the reader (but not the girls) can decode as panic. Maya is insulted. Emily runs payback-years math that says keep it. Sofi asks whether the recipe transfers with the brand — a question that stops the room.

The vote is 3–1 against selling. Keisha loses, hides her relief and her disappointment simultaneously, and the reader watches her almost sell her friends' summer to close a hole they don't know exists. Consequence, temptation, fracture. The valuation lesson comes along for free.

# Comedy engines (unchanged, one addition)

- **Keisha:** deadpan denial; over-practical avoidance; now also wry documentation of her own cover-up and her own inconvenient crush.
- **Maya:** theatrical confidence, bad-but-seductive logic.
- **Emily:** terrifying precision.
- **Sofi:** literal questions adults do not want answered.
- **Elena:** thrift-store severity, stinginess as performance.
- **Marcus:** finance-bro vocabulary applied to normal life — and now to flirting, catastrophically.
- **Keisha's mom:** practical bluntness.

Character-driven on every page; situational comedy stays limited.

# Form and devices

## Keep
Keisha's first-person comic narration; group chat; Elena's envelope; the napkin deal; the stand ledger; the amended agreement; the 1965 pay envelope; the 1976 fund slip; Emily's cheat sheet **as a one-page in-world artifact only**; the drafts-folder email as a recurring visual; the bedding bag in the corner.

## Artifact rule (unchanged)
Artifacts appear only when they create emotional or plot impact.

# Chapter plan — 28 chapters, three acts

Short chapters (~2,500–3,200 words). Group-chat interstitials between some chapters, unnumbered.

## Act I — The Lie and the Deal (Chs 1–8)

**1. The Lie at the Kitchen Table.** Mom asks about fall housing. "Handled." The hold notice, archived. Classmates post sophomore leases. Keisha flees to usefulness: Elena's garage sale. The 1965 pay envelope. The broken chair no one may replace.

**2. Hold Music.** FLASH-PRESENT: Keisha calls the bursar. Forty minutes, one incomprehensible form, one dropped transfer. She archives everything after this. Avoidance becomes a system with an origin story.

**3. Thirty Dollars for Thirty Percent.** The necklace receipt fight; Elena haggles ruthlessly over junk, refuses to sell treasures. The agua fresca idea. The napkin deal, treated like a merger. Sofi asks what "profit" means; nobody answers well. **Seed 1:** "You have your housing?" — one lie, never asked again. Marcus drops a green screenshot in the chat: retirement by 35 "ahead of schedule."

**4. Ten Customers.** First Saturday flop. Too much ice, too many cups, ten customers. The are-we-profitable fight. Elena walks them through the wreckage. They feel poor in public.

**5. The Spring No.** Keisha's RA rejection, revealed — she applied months ago, got a no, told no one. "I already tried" installed as her alibi. She takes the cash box: being trusted with money feels like competence.

**6. The Forwarded Email.** Mom asks about the deposit. Keisha forwards an old housing email as if current. Fabrication, stage two. The stand has its first decent Saturday; the split-it-now vs. reinvest fight begins.

**7. Elena Takes Her Cut.** Thirty percent in cash, no ice hauled. Ownership vs. labor. Emily's quiet home scene: a family where no one talks about money directly. Elena refuses to replace the cracked cooler handle — "it still has one good side."

**8. Drafts (1).** Keisha drafts the follow-up email to student employment. Revises it four times. Doesn't send. The stand finds its rhythm; the group chat gets funny; brief, dangerous optimism.

## Act II — The Stand Works, Then Doesn't (Chs 9–22)

**9. The Brother Who Keeps Showing Up.** Marcus at the stand more than a brother needs to be. Keisha notices herself noticing. **Romance beat 1.** His portfolio-manager language applied to cup sizes.

**10. Maya's Ad.** Maya spends her own money on promotion. Sales jump. She wants a bigger cut.

**11. The Deposit.** Mom pushes on housing paperwork. Keisha pays a real deposit she cannot afford to keep the lie alive — actively widening her own gap. Fabrication, stage three. She recounts the cash box twice that night.

**12. The Value Fight.** Emily's math proves the ad mattered. Keisha hates that the math picks sides — and her operations labor enters the argument. Marcus takes Keisha's side for non-math reasons. **Romance beat 2.** Sofi, listening: no one has priced the recipe.

**13. The Invoice.** Sofi prices the recipe. Her mother is appalled — family contribution isn't invoiced. Sofi invoices anyway. The girls confront Elena; she is defensive, then quiet: she wrote the deal she wished someone had offered her young, not a fair one. **The Amendment:** recipe named as Sofi's, labor repriced, Elena signs last. Meanwhile: Mom buys dorm bedding on sale. The bag goes in the corner.

**14. The Envelope She Files.** Helping Elena with paperwork, Keisha handles an envelope bearing the 529 institution's name. Files it unread. **Seed 2.** The answer passes through her hands.

**15. The Vendor.** $600 for the booth and the "brand." The real vote. Keisha argues to sell in over-practical language that is decoded panic. Sofi: does the recipe transfer? Vote 3–1 against. Keisha loses, and hides relief and disappointment at once.

**16. The Float.** After the deposit, short everywhere, Keisha borrows $60 from the stand float. Means to replace it. Recounts the box three times. Narrates it as "internal bridge financing."

**17. The Offer.** Marcus's peak: he's quit his job, talks like a man who escaped gravity. Flush and half-guessing, he offers to make her the money. **Romance beat 3.** Keisha declines — barely — and cannot fully explain to herself which temptation she just survived. Elena patches the umbrella with tape.

**18. Maya Sees It.** **Romance beat 4:** Maya's private callout — funny, sharp, not a prohibition. A warning about the cliff, from the person who loves both of them. Rain kills a Saturday; the stand makes nothing; one corner, one product, one weather system.

**19. The Near-Miss.** Emily's best-case summer math: even if everything goes right, Keisha is short. Keisha gets as far as "Can I ask you something about money?" Elena almost offers. Both retreat. **Seed 3:** Elena watching her leave. Two women missing each other by inches.

**20. The Thumb.** The investing tab. One hyped stock could close the gap. The thumb hovers — over money that is partly float money. **Maya catches her.** Not Elena, not the ledger: Maya, standing between the two people she loves and the same cliff. That night Keisha audits Marcus's full-year return and sees red. She opens the drafts folder and finally hits send.

**21. Sixty Dollars.** Keisha confesses the float to the girls before it's discovered. The hardest scene in Act II: smaller than the college secret, and the rehearsal for it. Emily re-runs the ledger without a word. Maya, who has been overdrawn her whole adult life, is the one who says it's survivable.

**22. The Crash.** On the page: Marcus's screenshot he can't crop his way out of. The quiet call about getting his job back. No one gloats — Keisha least of all, because she knows exactly which costume that temptation wears. He asks Emily, for his own reasons, how to open "the boring one."

## Act III — The Truth and the Names (Chs 23–28)

**23. The Ledger and the Envelope.** Season winding down. Stand share tallies: ~$1,000 each after the amendment. Still short. Keisha, cornered by her own braid — deposit, float, rejection, drafts — decides to stop hiding, and starts with Elena.

**24. Elena's Story.** Widowhood; accounts she couldn't access; credit she didn't have; signatures expected from a man who was gone. The 1976 fund slip. Decades of boring, held. Then the reveal: the 529, planted at two, disbursing annually — **it paid last year too.** The beam was always there and is still not enough. Keisha's fury: *you built a system of secret help so you'd never have to have one honest conversation.* Mid-tirade, the trapdoor: her own sentence dies in her mouth. Elena explains without apologizing well; Keisha names the difference out loud.

**25. Names on Accounts.** Emily has already opened hers — quietly, weeks ago. Maya's choice with a price: the card paid down, one joyful purchase kept with eyes open, the concert ticket sold in front of everyone. Sofi's custodial account: an adult's name must sit on her money — she asks the calm, literal question no one can answer comfortably. Keisha signs alone.

**26. Full Price.** Elena's small change: she pays full price for something beautiful — one item, not a transformation. The laptop loan: real terms, token interest, negotiated by a girl who reads contracts now; Elena signs last. **Romance beat 5:** the post-crash conversation with Marcus. Two people with their names on new accounts. A date they each pay half of. A beginning.

**27. The Kitchen Table Again.** Everything: the hold notice, the $2,800, the deposit, the bedding bag, the months. Mom doesn't rescue her with wisdom — she admits she didn't know what to ask; the lie worked because it landed inside another woman's missing education. And now the reader knows who chose the envelope over teaching her. The gap closes on the page: 529 slice + circulation-desk job (the answered email) + stand share. About $80 to spare. The bedding comes out of the bag.

**28. The Watched One.** Move-in weekend. The visible costs named: the desk job that will eat her semester, loan payments through spring, junior year's gap already on the horizon — nameable now. The stand's future left open for next summer, amended agreement in the ledger. Final image: Keisha catches Sofi observing her — the way Chapter 1 had Keisha observing Elena's rituals. The pattern, transmitting. No speech needed.

# Ending note (revised)

Warm but not tidy — now enforced structurally, not just tonally.

Keisha returns to school **at a visible cost**. Maya is not a perfect saver; she made one real trade. Emily is not fearless; she was brave once, early, in secret. Marcus is not a financial saint; he is back at work, holding a boring account, at the beginning of something with Keisha. Elena is not absolved; she is understood, which is worse and better. Sofi is watching.

One woman planted money.
One woman admits she was never taught.
One woman says the number out loud.
One woman learns to pay full price, once.
And the youngest one is taking notes.
